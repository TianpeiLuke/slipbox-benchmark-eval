# Payment Processor

## Definition

In the corpus, a payment processor is named as one of several distinct roles a company can hold in a transaction — the entity that handles the movement of payments — separate from being the customer, the merchant, or a market maker, even when a single company (like Alameda Research for FTX, or Google Play for its app store) plays more than one of those roles at once.

## Context

The corpus surfaces the term across two very different settings: crypto-market infrastructure and app-store commerce.
In the FTX criminal trial, defense counsel Mark Cohen argued that Alameda Research "acted legally as an FTX customer, payment processor, and market maker" — the third role described in the corpus as a "financial entity that acts as a trading partner for customers looking to buy and sell cryptocurrencies."
In fintech coverage, "payment processor" describes companies such as Stripe, Fiserv, and FIS; the startup Rainforest is described as competing to win software-platform business away from these "legacy processors."
In the Epic v. Google trial, Google Play's own billing system is treated as the payment processor for in-app purchases, and the antitrust dispute centers on whether Google may tie that processing to app-store access; per Epic CEO Tim Sweeney, Google charges other developers 30% but struck a special deal letting Spotify pay 0% when it processes its own payments, or 4% when it uses Google's payment service instead.

## Key Characteristics

- **One of several roles a single company can hold** — the FTX defense listed "customer, payment processor, and market maker" as three separate roles Alameda played for FTX, distinct from being FTX itself.
- **A fee-generating, competitive service** — Google charges up to 30% for Play billing (discounted to 4%, or waived to 0% for self-processed payments, under Spotify's special deal), and a separate "User Choice Billing" program launched in 2022 knocks only 4 percentage points off Google's standard fee for developers using their own payment system; Rainforest, Stripe, Fiserv, and FIS compete for the equivalent software-platform and merchant business.
- **Built differently for different customer types** — Rainforest founder Joshua Silver argues that existing "modern processors" (incumbents like Fiserv and FIS) were built directly for merchants, not software platforms, and have had to retrofit their systems to handle software companies' payment-processing and reporting needs — the gap Rainforest positions itself to fill.

## Background (external)

Outside this corpus, a payment processor is generally described as the intermediary that transfers payment data and messaging between the card-issuing bank, the merchant, and the acquiring bank to authorize and settle a transaction, and is typically categorized as either front-end (handling authorization) or back-end (handling settlement) (Wikipedia, "Payment processor," accessed 2026-09-02).
