# Antitrust

Antitrust is the label the corpus uses for a 2023 wave of U.S. and EU legal actions against dominant tech and payments companies — the FTC's suit against Amazon, the DOJ's *US v. Google* search-monopoly trial, Epic Games' Sherman Act/Cartwright Act suit against Google Play, Helena World Chronicle's Sherman Act/Clayton Act class action against Google, PayPal's anti-steering class action, and the EU's competition review of Amazon's iRobot acquisition.

## Context

The corpus documents a wave of parallel antitrust actions against major tech and payments companies in 2023. The FTC and 17 state attorneys general sued Amazon, alleging it used its dominant position to inflate prices, overcharge sellers, and stifle competition via an "anti-discounting strategy" and buy-box penalties for third-party sellers who priced lower elsewhere; the FTC sought to hold Amazon liable for violating anti-monopoly law, with remedies to be determined only after a liability finding. Separately, the DOJ's *US v. Google* trial over Google's search dominance turned on the Information Services Agreement making Google the default search engine on Apple devices — Apple's Eddy Cue testified about the ISA's revenue-share renegotiation with Google (specific dollar figures were reserved for closed court sessions), while separate testimony from Google's search head Prabhakar Raghavan put Google's 2021 default-placement payments at $26.3 billion total, with a New York Times report cited alongside that testimony putting Apple's share at roughly $18 billion; the DOJ argued Google's default-placement deals with Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla foreclosed rivals from the data needed to improve competing search products. Epic Games separately sued Google over Google Play's app-distribution and payment-processing practices, invoking Sherman Act Sections 1 and 2 alongside California's Cartwright Act and Unfair Competition Law. A class action by news publisher Helena World Chronicle accused Google of "anticompetitive behavior in violation of U.S. antitrust law, the Sherman Act, and others" over its use of publisher content in Knowledge Graph and AI search features. PayPal was hit with a class action alleging its anti-steering rules — modeled on tactics Visa and Mastercard abandoned after a DOJ antitrust suit — stifled competition from lower-cost payment platforms like Stripe. In the EU, regulators used antitrust competition-concern findings (a formal statement of objections) to scrutinize Amazon's proposed acquisition of iRobot over fears it would let Amazon foreclose rival robot-vacuum makers on its marketplace.

## Key Characteristics

- **Statutory basis** — in the U.S., Sherman Act Sections 1 (anticompetitive agreements) and 2 (monopolization) and the Clayton Act are the primary tools; the FTC additionally proceeds under anti-monopoly/unfair-methods-of-competition authority, and states can add their own statutes (e.g., California's Cartwright Act).
- **Conduct at issue** — cases target using dominance to raise prices or fees, penalize rivals' lower prices (Amazon's buy-box mechanism), pay for default placement to foreclose competitors (Google-Apple), restrict app distribution and billing options (Google Play), or impose anti-steering rules that block cheaper alternatives (PayPal).
- **Enforcers vary by jurisdiction** — DOJ and FTC (plus state AGs) bring U.S. federal cases; private plaintiffs can also bring antitrust class actions (Helena World Chronicle, PayPal consumers); the European Commission enforces EU competition law and can use a formal statement of objections short of blocking a deal to press for remedies.
- **Remedies are typically litigated after liability** — the FTC's Amazon complaint explicitly left remedies undetermined pending a liability finding, and the historic Microsoft case (the precedent both DOJ v. Google and Epic v. Google draw on) resulted in required API-sharing and a ban on anticompetitive, exclusive deals with computer manufacturers.
- **Merger review is a distinct antitrust track** — the EU's iRobot objections show competition authorities scrutinizing prospective mergers for foreclosure risk before a deal closes, separate from post-hoc conduct cases.

## Background (external)

The corpus uses "antitrust" as a familiar label for these cases without defining the term itself. Per the Federal Trade Commission, U.S. antitrust law originates with the Sherman Act of 1890, a "comprehensive charter of economic liberty aimed at preserving free and unfettered competition," joined in 1914 by the FTC Act and the Clayton Act (FTC, *The Antitrust Laws*, accessed 2026-09-02). Sherman Act Section 1 prohibits contracts, combinations, or conspiracies that restrain trade, and Section 2 addresses monopolization and attempted monopolization; the Clayton Act separately reaches practices like mergers that may substantially lessen competition, and the FTC Act prohibits "unfair methods of competition" (FTC, *The Antitrust Laws*, accessed 2026-09-02). The Department of Justice brings criminal Sherman Act cases, the FTC enforces the FTC Act against similar conduct, and state attorneys general and private plaintiffs can enforce parallel state antitrust statutes (FTC, *The Antitrust Laws*, accessed 2026-09-02).
