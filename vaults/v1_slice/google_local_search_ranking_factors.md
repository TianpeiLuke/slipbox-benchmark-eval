# Google Local Search Ranking Factors

Google's public documentation says it uses three categories to rank results that are local to a searcher: relevance, distance, and prominence. To improve local ranking, Google encourages businesses to provide a trove of information on their profile — basics like updated hours of operation and accurate location details, but also responding to reviews, adding photos, and even posting individual items in stock to their Google page. The prominence measure in Maps includes data from the web: links to the business, how the business ranks in traditional search, and press coverage, for example. In this way SEO affects how a business appears in local search results, and news articles and links are one way Google tries to determine the relevance of a page — which is why reporters' inboxes are overrun with spammy requests to buy "guest posts" on their sites from marketers.

The model bounds what a keyword-matched business name can do. Asked whether naming a business after a search term works, Danny Sullivan, Google's public liaison for Search, said, "I doubt it": owners doing this might find success, but Google pulls in other data to serve results to users, like location, reviews, or ratings — a "hodgepodge of different things that we have that are out there." Sullivan added, "I've seen these kinds of things over the years, and they kind of make me laugh and have a bit of a chuckle. But I don't think they're really great marketing campaigns," and said Google's advice has always been that businesses should make decisions that serve their customers, not the platform.

The behaviour the names target is real even if the leverage is limited. The term "Thai food near me" is searched, on average, nearly a million times a month in the US, according to Semrush, a company that provides keyword research and other popular SEO tools; Semrush notes you will need referring domains and optimized content to compete for the term and grades it as "difficult" to rank for — competitive, but not even the hardest category. Adding "near me" to a search term is both a learned behavior and encouraged by Google, since "___ near me" is a top autofilled search suggestion on both Search and Maps and has become synonymous with Google, which is why the joke works. One anomaly showed the distance factor being outweighed: a search The Verge's reporter did in Google Maps for "Thai food near me" from Wisconsin surfaced the Manhattan restaurant as a suggestion above any local businesses, making it easier to find from the Midwest than from Brooklyn. Sullivan said this could be due to Google recognizing there is a business with an exact-match name that has some degree of notoriety that people are searching for in different areas, and that while it could encourage others to name businesses this way, he does not see it as an issue that needs addressing.
