# Total Addressable Market (TAM)

## Definition

Total addressable market (TAM) is the overall revenue opportunity a market represents — the size of the "pie" a company could theoretically capture — as distinct from market share, the slice of that pie a specific competitor has actually won.
The corpus uses "TAM" directly only once: in a TechCrunch roundup on AI startup strategy, after VC Edward Tsai of Alumni Ventures argued that startups "may want to temporarily reduce their focus on defensibility and increase their focus on products that deliver compelling value and focusing on speed to market," the article's author adds, as an editorial aside rather than part of Tsai's quote, "Presuming massive TAM, that could work!" — treating TAM as a measure of how much upside is still up for grabs regardless of who wins any single deal.
Far more often the corpus talks about the narrower, already-contested slice of a market — "market share" — across search, payments, betting, retail, crypto, autos, and music.

## Context

The corpus's clearest TAM reference sits inside a broader discussion of how AI startups should compete: Edward Tsai of Alumni Ventures told TechCrunch that startups might "temporarily reduce their focus on defensibility and increase their focus on products that deliver compelling value and focusing on speed to market," a view the article's author then glossed with "Presuming massive TAM, that could work!" and Rick Grinnell of Glasswing Ventures separately described the goal of "capturing market share or meeting a critical market need before competitors" — TAM as the size of the opportunity, market share as who is winning it.
The rest of the corpus's "market share" usage documents that same capture-and-defend dynamic in mature markets rather than emerging ones: Google's roughly 90% share of online search (and the $26.3 billion it paid in 2021 to secure default placement across Apple, Samsung, and Mozilla to protect that share); Chrome's climb from nearly 50% of desktop browser usage at the end of 2014 to nearly 65% by the end of 2017, the same year Google regained the default-search slot on Firefox; Binance's share of the crypto-exchange market falling from over 60% to under 50% after its DOJ settlement; Reliance Retail's roughly 3% share of India's retail market against Walmart's roughly 10% of the US market; Capitol Music Group's "high-5%" US label market share; Tesla losing EV market share to rivals amid price cuts; and fintech Rainforest explicitly trying to "take market share" from Stripe and legacy processors Fiserv and FIS.
Sportsbooks entering new states (Kentucky, Vermont) frame their promotional bonus wars in the same terms — "fighting for market share" in a market that is still being carved up as it launches.

## Key Characteristics

- **TAM is the whole opportunity; market share is the captured slice** — the corpus's one TAM mention treats it as license to chase growth over defensibility, while every market-share mention describes a company's already-won or already-lost portion of a market.
- **Market share is measured differently by market** — percentage of search queries (Google's ~90%), percentage of desktop browser usage (Chrome's ~65%), percentage of exchange volume/assets (Binance's 60%→50% decline), percentage of retail floor space or sales (Reliance Retail's ~3% vs. Walmart's ~10%), and record-label percentage of a national market (Capitol Music Group's "high-5%").
- **Share can be bought as well as earned** — Google's $26.3 billion in 2021 default-placement payments to Apple and other partners, and sportsbooks' signup bonuses in newly legalized states, are both framed as direct spend to acquire or defend market share rather than organic growth.
- **Share shifts are framed as zero-sum among incumbents** — Rainforest is described as taking share "from" Stripe, Fiserv, and FIS; EV rivals are described as taking share "from" Tesla; new entrants into Vermont and Kentucky sports betting are described as "fighting" each other for the same pool of bettors.
- **A large presumed TAM changes competitive strategy** — the corpus's TechCrunch commentary on VC Edward Tsai's view suggests that when the addressable opportunity is presumed to be very large, startups may rationally prioritize speed to market over building defensible moats, since there is enough unclaimed opportunity for multiple winners.

## Background (external)

The corpus does not spell out the "total addressable market" acronym or define TAM's relationship to narrower measures. Per Wikipedia, TAM is the total market demand for a product or service, typically expressed in annual revenue or units, if 100% of that market were captured; it is conventionally narrowed first to a Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM, the portion a company could realistically reach) and then to a Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM, the portion realistically obtainable), with market share denoting how much of the SAM/SOM a given competitor currently holds (Wikipedia, *Total addressable market*, accessed 2026-09-02).
