# Criminal Trial

## Definition

In this corpus, "criminal trial" names the government's federal prosecution of Sam Bankman-Fried in Manhattan on seven charges of fraud and conspiracy tied to the collapse of FTX, in which prosecutors presented witness testimony to prove guilt while the defense contested it, U.S. District Judge Lewis Kaplan presided, and a jury returned the verdict.
The corpus uses the term specifically to distinguish this proceeding — and the format of Epic Games v. Google, a civil jury trial — from non-criminal, civil proceedings such as the Trump/Engoron case, which sought financial penalties rather than imprisonment.

## Context

The corpus's central example is Sam Bankman-Fried's federal criminal trial in Manhattan, on seven charges of fraud and conspiracy tied to the collapse of FTX, presided over by U.S. District Judge Lewis Kaplan; the trial ran roughly six weeks, opening with jury selection on October 3, 2023 and opening statements the next day, and closing in a guilty verdict on all counts.
The proceeding follows a recognizable structure the corpus documents in sequence: jury selection, opening statements from prosecution and defense, the prosecution's case (fact witnesses like Adam Yedidia and Matt Huang, cooperating former co-conspirators Gary Wang and Caroline Ellison testifying under cooperation agreements, and expert testimony from accounting professor Peter Easton), cross-examination, the defendant's own testimony and cross-examination, closing arguments, and jury deliberation toward a verdict.
The corpus also shows what a criminal trial is not, by contrast: the parallel civil case against Donald Trump before Judge Arthur Engoron was a non-jury ("bench") trial brought by the New York attorney general seeking financial penalties, not imprisonment, and the corpus notes Manhattan prosecutors had considered and declined to bring a criminal case over the same underlying conduct — the state instead pursued civil fraud remedies. Epic Games v. Google is likewise a civil antitrust jury trial (Sherman Act, Cartwright Act claims) seeking injunctive and monetary relief, not a criminal prosecution, even though it shares the jury-trial format.

## Key Characteristics

- **Adversarial structure** — a prosecution (the government, e.g. Assistant U.S. Attorneys Danielle Sassoon, Nicolas Roos, and Thane Rehn in the SBF case) argues guilt while defense counsel (Mark Cohen, Christian Everdell) argues innocence or lack of criminal intent.
- **Jury as fact-finder** — a jury, not the judge, decides guilt; in the SBF trial the jury included a train conductor and a retired investment banker, and reporters repeatedly note what testimony "the jury saw" or "the jury will have to decide."
- **Burden and standard tied to criminal charges** — the prosecution must show the defendant's conduct was not just harmful but criminal; as one account puts it, "Just losing $8 billion is not a crime... But if Bankman-Fried lied to customers and lenders about what he was doing... that's a crime," turning the case on proving intentional deceit and conspiracy rather than mere loss or business failure.
- **Cooperating witnesses and immunity/plea deals** — co-conspirators such as Gary Wang, Caroline Ellison, and witnesses like Adam Yedidia testified under cooperation agreements or legal immunity, a common feature of criminal trials involving multiple alleged participants.
- **Judge as referee, not fact-finder** — Judge Kaplan ruled on objections, instructed the jury on the law, and controlled testimony (e.g., telling the jury he was the "final authority" on the definition of "market manipulation"), while leaving the guilt determination to the jury.
- **Sentencing follows conviction** — a guilty verdict is followed by a separate sentencing phase; the corpus notes SBF's charges could put him in prison for the rest of his life and co-conspirator Gary Wang faced up to 50 years pending sentencing.
- **Distinct from civil trials** — civil proceedings (Trump/Engoron, Epic v. Google) can share elements like juries or trial-style testimony but seek monetary or injunctive remedies under a different legal standard, and can proceed without a jury at all (non-jury "bench" trial).

## Background (external)

Outside the corpus, a criminal trial is a court proceeding, initiated only by the government, in which the prosecution must prove the defendant's guilt "beyond a reasonable doubt" — a heavier standard than in civil cases — while the jury decides guilt and the judge oversees the proceeding and, on a guilty verdict, determines the sentence (U.S. Courts, "Criminal Cases," uscourts.gov, accessed 2026-09-02).
