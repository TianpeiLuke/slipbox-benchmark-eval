# The Mechanism Of FTX's Collapse

FTX came apart in early November 2022 through a chain with four links: a leaked balance sheet revealed that Alameda's largest holding was FTX's own token in a quantity larger than the token's whole market, the disclosure destroyed confidence, the world's largest rival exchange pulled its holdings out, and the resulting bank run forced FTX and Alameda into Chapter 11 within days. TechCrunch's 2023-10-01 account sets out the sequence: in early November 2022, that all changed.

Concerns surrounding FTX's liquidity grew after CoinDesk published a copy of Alameda's balance sheet, showing the firm held $14.6 billion in assets and $8 billion in liabilities as of June 30, 2022. The balance sheet showed that the $5.82 billion in FTT tokens that Alameda owned was 193% higher than the total FTT market cap, which was about $3 billion at the time — meaning Alameda purported to have more FTT tokens on its balance sheet than what existed in the world. Around the same time it was exposed, the world's largest crypto exchange, Binance, started pulling out its remaining $2.1 billion equivalent of cash in BUSD and FTT; Binance had held an equity position in FTX from 2019 to 2021. That withdrawal essentially triggered a bank run on FTX. FTX and Alameda filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the U.S. mid-November 2022, Bankman-Fried resigned, and John J. Ray III, the Enron turnaround veteran, was appointed the new CEO.

The condition the whole mechanism rests on is the fragility of the underlying valuations. As TechCrunch put it on 2023-10-02, all of that activity came to a crashing end because the value of cryptocurrencies on FTX was not banked to much more than speculation and the promise of higher value — so when those valuations fell, they fell very, very hard. The chain therefore only runs as described where an asset is priced on expectation, held in size by a related party, and exposed to a counterparty large enough that its exit alone starts the run.
