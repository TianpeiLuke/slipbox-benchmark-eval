# The Google Antitrust Case

The Google antitrust case is the U.S. government's trial over Google's dominance in the online search market, stemming from a 2020 antitrust claim, in which the government argues that Google uses its platforms and its deals with partners to block out competition in search or advertising and thereby to hinder competitors from accessing the data they would need to improve their products.

Its defining features are the conduct at issue and the defence offered against it. Google CEO Sundar Pichai testified Monday in the trial, defending Google's business tactics, including its deal with Apple and other partners to make Google the default search engine. Google's answer is that it is just doing business: everybody wants Google as the default engine because it is the best, and that, the company says, does not make its actions illegal.

The important boundary case is which Google antitrust matter this is. The 2020 search claim is separate from the one filed in January by the Department of Justice, along with eight states including New York, California and Colorado, which aims to "halt Google's anticompetitive scheme, unwind Google's monopolistic grip on the market and restore competition to digital advertising." The search case and the digital-advertising case are distinct proceedings and should not be conflated.

The trial is the venue in which the underlying numbers surface. Monday's hearing revealed plenty of juicy tidbits, including the $26.3 billion Google spent making itself the default search engine across platforms in 2021, and how Google tried to take it further and have Chrome preinstalled on iPhones.
