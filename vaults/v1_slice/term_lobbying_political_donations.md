# Lobbying and Political Donations

Lobbying and political donations, in the corpus, are the practice of routing money to political candidates, parties, or officials to gain influence with regulators and lawmakers.

## Context

The corpus's central example is Sam Bankman-Fried's use of FTX- and Alameda-linked funds for political spending during his rise, which became a prosecution theme at his fraud trial: TechCrunch reports he "had close ties to U.S. regulators and government officials, many of whom he donated to," and that Michael Lewis's book "Going Infinite" surfaced his "attempt to buy off Trump to get him to not run again for president" by discussing with Mitch McConnell how to "fund political candidates who were looking to offset Trump's influence in the Republican Party." At trial, prosecutor Thane Rehn told the jury Bankman-Fried used stolen customer funds to "donate millions to political candidates," and Nishad Singh, described as a longtime family friend of Bankman-Fried, pleaded guilty to charges that included counts "around his political donations." The practice also extended to those around Bankman-Fried: The Verge names "Barbara Fried's political action committee, Mind the Gap" among the entities caught up in the case.

## Key Characteristics

- **Funding source under dispute** — the prosecution's theory was that the political donations were financed with misappropriated FTX customer deposits, not Bankman-Fried's own money.
- **Instrument of influence, not charity** — donations to candidates and officials are described alongside efforts to buy access (e.g., the reported offer to Trump), distinguishing them from Bankman-Fried's professed effective-altruism philanthropy.
- **Bipartisan reach** — the reporting describes both engagement with Democratic-aligned regulators/officials and an attempt to influence a Republican presidential race, i.e., donations spanning both parties.
- **Legal exposure** — political donations became a distinct criminal count (against Nishad Singh) and a recurring prosecution talking point against Bankman-Fried, showing the practice can carry direct legal consequences when the underlying funds are alleged to be stolen.
- **Family/associate involvement** — the practice was not confined to Bankman-Fried himself; his mother Barbara Fried's PAC, Mind the Gap, is named as part of the same political-spending network.
