# Bankman-Fried And Ellison: Relationship And Alameda Leadership

Caroline Ellison testified that she and Bankman-Fried started sleeping together in the fall of 2018, on and off, when she was a trader and he was CEO, and that they later dated twice — first from the summer of 2020 through the summer of 2021, a relationship they agreed to keep secret though some people found out, and again from the fall of 2021 until the spring of 2022, when they lived together. She was named co-CEO of Alameda with Sam Trabucco in 2021 while she and Bankman-Fried were broken up, and CEO in 2022; the goal, Bankman-Fried told her, was to "optically" separate Alameda Research and FTX. "The whole time we were dating, he was my boss at work," she said. They broke up because she wanted more from the relationship and he was distant and not paying enough attention to her. He did not grant her equity even though she asked, telling her it would be too complicated; instead she got a $200,000 salary, even as CEO, and bonuses twice a year ranging from $100,000 to $20 million.

Bankman-Fried gave a different rationale for the handover. In 2021, he testified, FTX was growing to millions of users with $1 billion of revenue, he was working 12 to 22 hours a day and taking one day off every couple of months, and because FTX had grown so much he could no longer run both companies — so he handed Alameda off to Ellison and Trabucco, who immediately after being named co-CEO promptly drifted away to early retirement. Bankman-Fried did remain involved in hedging and risk at Alameda.

Ellison's proximity also gave her an unusual view of his character. She said Alameda was troubled from her earliest time there in 2018: "Shortly after I started, I learned the company was in worse shape than I realized." Alameda had initially been funded with loans "from acquaintances," and those loans were recalled a few weeks after she arrived; when she asked Bankman-Fried why he had not shared the company's shaky circumstances in the job offer, "He hadn't known how to tell me," she said. She described him as "very ambitious," said he told her there was a 5 percent chance he would become president of the United States, and said he told her that if there were a coin flip where tails destroyed the world and heads made the world twice as good, he would flip the coin — which he called being "risk-neutral," and which The Verge's reporter called a fancy way of saying he was a gambling addict. He made her CEO of his sin-eater hedge fund while refusing to give her equity.
