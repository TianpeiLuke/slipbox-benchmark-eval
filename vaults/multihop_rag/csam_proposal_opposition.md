# Johansson's Rebuttals To The Case Against The CSAM Scanning Proposal

Against opponents who characterise her CSAM-scanning proposal as mass surveillance, as a slippery slope, and as a law written around two vendors' technology, Ylva Johansson argued to MEPs that each of those premises is wrong: the proposal names no technology, would scan less than happens today, and forbids using detection for anything other than child sexual abuse. TechCrunch reported her answers on 25 October 2023, from her appearance before the committee.

The opposition she was answering had been aired earlier that week. A seminar organized by the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS), an advisory body to the Commission on data protection law, heard from more than 20 speakers across civil society, academia and industry expressing deep misgivings about the Commission's approach — including a warning from the EDPS himself that the EU could be at a tipping point for freedom and democracy if it does not turn back from the plan to do non-targeted scanning of private messages. Johansson had been invited to participate in the seminar but declined to attend, and she did not offer a direct response to the EDPS' concerns at the hearing, instead countering a number of the arguments heard at the session.

Her first counter attacks the premise that the proposal is built around particular vendors. She denied that only Thorn and Microsoft have technology "that is necessary for the scanning", calling that claim "absolutely not true". "There are no specific technologies mentioned [in the proposal] and I think this is an important part. So there's no specific technology that's been favoured in this proposal," she told the committee, adding: "So many technologies are being developed all the time — while we are speaking — and they will continue to develop. So I think it's important that the legislation has to be technology neutral."

Her second counter attacks the inference from the proposal to mass surveillance. "My proposal would not mean that all communication will be scanned. Compared to the situation today it will be much more limited," she claimed, referencing the temporary ePrivacy derogation that currently gives messaging firms a legal basis to scan non-encrypted content for CSAM — a derogation intended to be replaced by the proposed regulation which, critics contend, will force platforms to scan end-to-end encrypted content too. "Today companies are allowed to scan if they search for child sexual abuse material. That's why we receive these 5.2 million videos and pictures and grooming attempts — 70% from private communication. If my proposal is adopted, this will be limited."

The warrant she offered for that comparison is the proposal's ordering of obligations: in-scope platforms are first required to deploy prevention measures to try to stop the spread of CSAM and/or prevent abuse of their tools by people intent on abusing children. "First comes prevention. Only if prevention is not enough, then you might be allowed to do detection — but only after a court decision," she said. "So only those that really cannot deal with the problem with mitigating measures… and only after a court decision and only during a specific period they will be allowed to do the detection. We will also limit the reporting so that we will also receive fewer but hopefully better reports."

Her third counter attacks the overreach and slippery-slope premises by pointing outside the proposal, to other EU laws acting as balancing checks on the scope of possible CSAM-scanning. "It's also important that we continue to comply with all relevant legislation. For example the GDPR and other requirements, there are no derogation from that in my proposal," she said. "It's also important — and I know that's been part of the debate — that it should not be a slippery slope. The proposal specifically prohibits using the detection technologies for any other purpose than the detection of child sexual abuse online — and only with verified indicators of child sexual abuse provided by the EU Centre."

That defensive strategy carries its own exposure. Given her reliance on pointing to the existence of a wider EU legal framework doing the heavy lifting and protecting Europeans' fundamental rights as a way to assuage critics, and given she is also invoking respect for the rule of law as a buttress against the risk of content-scanning mission creep, it is doubly relevant that the Commission now finds itself in a bind — forced to investigate whether its own officials ignored legal requirements in a bid to covertly sweep past critics.
