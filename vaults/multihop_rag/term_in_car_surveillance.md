# In-Car Surveillance

## Definition

As the corpus uses it, in-car surveillance is camera-based monitoring that involves a vehicle: either a camera mounted inside the vehicle itself (Uber's proposed in-vehicle cameras, headlined by one source as "in-car cameras") or a surveillance-camera system deployed together with — but not necessarily mounted on — vehicle patrols at a guarded perimeter.

## Context

The corpus surfaces the term in two contrasting settings. Along the Gaza border, the barrier that separated Israel and the Gaza Strip until October 7 was "almost unmanned by soldiers, and relied mainly on sensors, above and below ground, surveillance cameras, and vehicle patrols," with no surrounding minefield. In occupied East Jerusalem, surveillance cameras — some supplied by the Chinese firm Hikvision — blanket the Damascus Gate and residential streets in Silwan, watching Palestinians "at all times" per Amnesty International's *Automated Apartheid* report. In the consumer setting, hundreds of Uber sexual-assault survivors and their attorneys are calling for "in-vehicle surveillance cameras" installed inside the cars themselves, arguing that Uber's existing app-based safety features (a 911 button, location-sharing, audio recording) fail riders who cannot reach their phone during an assault.

## Key Characteristics

- **Vehicle involvement, not always vehicle-mounted** — Uber's proposed cameras would be mounted inside the ride-hail car itself, while the Gaza barrier's surveillance cameras were deployed alongside separate vehicle patrols rather than mounted on them; both are vehicle-linked, but in different ways, distinguishing the concept from generic fixed urban CCTV.
- **Dual purpose across security and safety** — the same camera-surveillance concept covers state border security (Gaza barrier), urban population monitoring (East Jerusalem), and consumer personal-safety demands (Uber riders), with very different stakeholders and stakes.
- **Deterrence rationale** — attorney Rachel Abrams argues mandatory in-vehicle cameras are "essential for safety," citing taxi studies she says show cameras drastically reduce assaults against both passengers and drivers.
- **Voluntary versus mandatory tension** — in the Uber case, cameras are currently something individual drivers opt into via personal dash cams or a piloted smartphone recording feature, not something the platform mandates; Uber has not explained why it hasn't made cameras mandatory despite survivors' demands.
- **Vendor entanglement with human-rights controversy** — in the East Jerusalem deployment, the surveillance-camera vendor Hikvision has itself been accused of aiding repression, illustrating that the camera supply chain can carry its own rights concerns independent of how footage is used.
