# Ray-Ban Meta: Camera Quality And Quirks

The Ray-Ban Meta camera's output surprised people who saw it: The Verge reviewer's tech-savvy co-workers found the quality surprisingly good for smart glasses, her friends and family thought the footage was shot on a phone, and the outlet's own video team repeatedly remarked the quality was better than expected. Someone into photography can tell it does not match the latest and greatest phones, but it is good enough to match a phone from a few years ago and therefore shareable on social media and in the group chat — which she called a Big Deal. The Verge's resident camera expert Becca Farsace noted the stabilization is surprisingly good: there is some wobbliness, but it does not look like garbage, especially viewed on a phone. Low-light performance was also better than anticipated, with no massive drop in video quality moving from light to dark environments, and color reproduction and detail are solid in good lighting.

Capture is hands-free or one-touch. You can use the "Hey Meta" command or the capture button on the top of the right arm — press once for photos, long-press to start recording video — with accompanying audio cues and an LED in your right peripheral vision. The LED capture indicator sits on the right side of the frame; the camera is opposite, in front of the left hinge. The reviewer valued phone-free, hands-free video in daily life, saying she now has many videos of her cats Petey and Pablo that would have been hard to capture otherwise because both get weird when a phone comes out — refusing to look, leaving, or in Petey's case trying to eat the camera — and that having both hands free made filming and interacting easier, including lifting a 19-pound Pablo for the cat-twirling meme. She saw it as a discreet GoPro alternative for filming cooking instructions, parts of running or cycling routes, a scenic drive, or slice-of-life candids of kids.

The quirks follow from where the camera sits. What you see is not what the camera sees, because it is in front of the left hinge rather than in your eyeball, so shots come out mildly off-center unless you account for it; this is also how she learned she often tilts her head like a confused puppy, and many photos and videos featured unintentional Dutch angles and wisps or full chunks of her bangs, while Becca had issues with her hat popping up in shots. There is also a one- to two-second delay when taking photos, so a lot of her landscape photos came out blurry while walking unless she stood completely still, and there is no way to preview images via your phone unless you are livestreaming.
