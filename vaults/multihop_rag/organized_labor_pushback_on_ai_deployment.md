# Organized Labor's Pushback On AI Deployment

Organized labor's argument, voiced by AFL-CIO President Liz Shuler at the conclusion of Tuesday's Senate AI forum, is that AI's effect on workers depends entirely on whether workers are included in the process: "If we fail to involve workers and unions across the entire innovation process, AI will curtail our rights, threaten good jobs and undermine our democracy. But the responsible adoption of AI, properly regulated, has the potential to create opportunity, improve working conditions and build prosperity."

The grounds Shuler offered are that the technology is already being used against workers — "Working people are fighting back against artificial intelligence and other technology used to eliminate workers or undermine and exploit us" — so the warrant joining grounds to claim is that regulation plus worker participation is what separates the beneficial deployment path from the harmful one, rather than the technology itself deciding the outcome.

The same argument came from inside the industry. While Andreessen calls for AI advancement at any cost and Tegmark continues to advocate for a developmental "time out," rank-and-file AI industry workers pressed their own case ahead of the forum: on Monday, employees from two dozen leading AI firms published an open letter to Senator Schumer demanding Congress act to safeguard their livelihoods from the "dystopian future" that Andreessen's screed would require. "Establishing robust protections related to workplace technology and rebalancing power between workers and employers could reorient the economy and tech innovation toward more equitable and sustainable outcomes," the letter's authors argue. The previous month, Senator Ed Markey (D-MA) and Representative Pramila Jayapal (WA-07) had called on leading AI companies to "answer for the working conditions of their data workers, laborers who are often paid low wages and provided no benefits but keep AI products online."
