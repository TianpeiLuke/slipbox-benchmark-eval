# How The Texas And Florida Laws Reached The Supreme Court

The two state laws reached the Supreme Court through a four-stage path: parallel Republican-passed statutes in 2021, industry lawsuits under the First Amendment, federal trial-court injunctions blocking both laws on constitutional grounds, and then a clash between two federal appellate courts that gave the Supreme Court one of its standard reasons to take a case.

Stage one is enactment. These cases actually started in Florida and Texas a few years ago before wending their way to the Supreme Court in 2023. In those two states, Republican lawmakers passed parallel laws to control how social media companies operate: in Florida, Governor Ron DeSantis signed Senate Bill 7072 into law in May 2021; in Texas, House Bill 20 made it through the state legislature and was signed by Governor Greg Abbott in September 2021.

Stage two is the challenge, and it explains why these two states rather than others. "The reason why these cases are in front of the Supreme Court is actually relatively simple: Florida and Texas were more or less the first out of the gate in imposing this type of restriction on social media companies," Barrett explained. "So when the industry sued the states under the First Amendment, these were the first cases that were litigated, so they went up through the court system." Both laws made their way through the lower courts after tech industry group NetChoice and the Computer and Communications Industry Association (CCIA) issued legal challenges against them.

Stage three and four are where the structure becomes decisive, because that path was complicated and contradictory, which is part of how the case landed in the Supreme Court's lap: "In both cases, you had federal trial judges who entered injunctions blocking the laws on constitutional grounds. And then you had two different federal appellate courts — in the case of Florida, the Eleventh Circuit, in the case of Texas, the Fifth Circuit. The two appellate courts clashed… and there was an explicit conflict between the two federal appellate courts. And that type of conflict is one of the bases that the US Supreme Court uses for deciding when to take cases."

The relation that makes the model work holds only for parallel laws that split the circuits: it is the explicit conflict between the Eleventh and Fifth Circuits, not the trial-court injunctions on their own, that supplies the recognised basis for review.
