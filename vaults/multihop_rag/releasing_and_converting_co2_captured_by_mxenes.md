# Releasing and Converting CO2 Captured by MXenes

Trapping CO2 anywhere is just a first step in managing this pollutant, says Vitalie Stavila, a materials chemist at Sandia National Laboratories in Livermore, California: "The problem is, what are you going to do with all that CO2?" Scientists will have to figure out how to retrieve the trapped gas from MXenes once they are full.

Two release routes are described: MXenes could be heated up to release their CO2 for long-term storage somewhere else, or flushing MXenes with a different kind of gas, such as hydrogen, might remove the CO2 from them, after which the MXenes could be reused for a new round of carbon capture. MXenes could also help transform the CO2 they capture into other useful compounds, Stavila says, because they are catalytic — they can help drive other chemicals, such as CO2, to react — and because they conduct electricity and like sharing electrons with CO2, they can ferry those electrons to other compounds. As a result, "you can do [transformations of CO2] that may not be otherwise possible," Stavila says: with a boost from MXenes, CO2 can react with hydrogen to form methanol or formic acid, liquids that can be used as fuels to reduce reliance on newly mined fossil fuels, which add new CO2 to the atmosphere, or better yet stored somewhere to keep their carbon out of the air for good. A published diagram shows how a dry MXene or a wet one captures and stores carbon dioxide molecules within its structure.
