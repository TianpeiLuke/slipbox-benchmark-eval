# Why The Illness Is Unlikely To Be Viral, And Why The Bacterial Finding Is Not Settled

This is a challenge to the premise that the canine illness is viral — the inference people draw from its presentation. While the respiratory symptoms seem to be like a viral illness, testing has been negative for a virus, Cantu-Schomus said, so the symptomatic resemblance is not backed by test results.

The stronger attack is methodological. It is very unlikely that the cause will turn out to be viral, said Colin Parrish, a professor of virology at the College of Veterinary Medicine at Cornell University: "With the sequencing methods people use to look for unknown viruses, its signature would have been clear in a few days." The premise under attack is the assumption that an unknown virus could still be hiding from detection; Parrish's warrant is that contemporary sequencing methods would have surfaced a viral signature within days, so continued absence of a signature is itself evidence against a viral cause.

The same caution is then turned on the competing bacterial explanation rather than spent only on the viral one. So it is possible that the New Hampshire scientists could have hit upon the right pathogen, although their results need to be confirmed by more research, Parrish said. The position is symmetrical: the viral hypothesis is very unlikely on sequencing grounds, and the bacterial candidate is possible but unconfirmed pending further research.
