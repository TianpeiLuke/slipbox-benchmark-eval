# Canine Respiratory Illness Cause: Evidence and Caveats

The premise under attack is the natural inference from symptoms to a viral cause. While the respiratory symptoms seem to be like a viral illness, testing has been negative for a virus, Cantu-Schomus said — so the symptom resemblance does not carry through to a viral diagnosis.

Colin Parrish, a professor of virology at the College of Veterinary Medicine at Cornell University, pushes the objection further, from a negative test to a positive reason to expect one. It is very unlikely that the cause will turn out to be viral, he said: "With the sequencing methods people use to look for unknown viruses, its signature would have been clear in a few days." The warrant is the sensitivity and speed of the search method — if a virus were there, the search that has already been run would have shown it.

The same caution is then applied to the competing bacterial claim rather than only to the viral one. So it is possible that the New Hampshire scientists could have hit upon the right pathogen, Parrish said, although their results need to be confirmed by more research. The objection is therefore two-sided: it rules the viral explanation very unlikely while holding the bacterial identification to an unmet confirmation standard.
