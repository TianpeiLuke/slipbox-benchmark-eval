# Detour (1945)

Edgar G. Ulmer's Detour is a standout example of film noir aesthetics and low-budget moviemaking, and Polygon's Pete Volk presents it as inseparable from the circumstances of its production. It streams on Prime Video and the Criterion Channel, free with ads on Pluto TV or Plex, and free with a library card on Kanopy.

The necessary context is Ulmer's career. He was a prolific director in the Classic Hollywood era who made the groundbreaking psychological thriller The Black Cat, the first movie to star horror legends Bela Lugosi and Boris Karloff, and a huge hit for Universal — but he was blacklisted from most Hollywood studios shortly afterward, when his affair with the wife of a nephew of Universal studio head Carl Laemmle turned into a divorce and remarriage. Detour was one of many micro-budget movies he then made for Producers Releasing Corporation, the smallest of the Hollywood studios at the time. The film follows a down-on-his-luck young man hitchhiking from New York to Los Angeles to meet up with his love, who had moved to Hollywood hoping to make it big, until a mysterious stranger he meets along the way upends everything. Volk notes that the tiny budget gives the 66-minute film an odd aura, with sparse sets and back projection that is inconsistent at best, but argues it undoubtedly works: a haunting movie about the unluckiest man in the universe, in which Ulmer's striking images and evocative use of lighting, plus an unforgettable performance by Ann Savage, paint a stark picture of an uncaring world.
