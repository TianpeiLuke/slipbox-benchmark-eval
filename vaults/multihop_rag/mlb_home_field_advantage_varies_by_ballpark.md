# Home-Field Advantage In MLB Varies By Ballpark

Home-field advantage in Major League Baseball just isn't the same from one ballpark to the next, The New York Times argued on 8 October 2023 — a claim grounded in the contrast between the two American League Wild Card Series played that week.

In one AL Wild Card Series, the whole thing turned on a play designed around crowd noise: it was too loud at Target Field, Twins shortstop Carlos Correa realised, for the third-base coach to warn Blue Jays baserunners about a developing pickoff play, so the Twins used the noise to their advantage, picked off Vladimir Guerrero Jr. and secured a two-game sweep. In the other AL Wild Card Series, empty seats were everywhere: the Rays are an excellent team that thrives on their underdog status — low payroll, injured players, they always find a way — but playing in front of two of the smallest postseason crowds in the past 100 years, they were swept by a Rangers team that had nine fewer wins in the regular season. The indifference was deafening.

The warrant is that the postseason's greatest moments tend to be punctuated by the crowds who witness and react in real time, so the crowd is part of the competitive environment rather than background. Phillies third baseman Alec Bohm made the players' version of the point as Philadelphia swept the Marlins to improve to 24-11 in postseason games at Citizens Bank Park: "To be honest with you, I think we have one of the best home-field advantages in baseball. People say it's difficult to play here and things like that. I think going through that type of stuff and learning how to play here, that just makes this time of year that much better." On that basis, after seeing the way crowds could respond — or not — to October baseball that week, The Athletic ranked the home-field environments of the eight remaining playoff teams, from the replaceable to the Phanatical.
