# The Jellyfish Striped Water Tank Learning Experiment

The team put 12 jellyfish into a round water tank surrounded by low-contrast gray and white stripes — which might appear to a jellyfish like distant mangrove roots in clear water — and filmed them for about seven minutes, during which the animals learned to keep away from the wall.

At first the animals seemed to see the gray stripes as distant roots and swam into the tank wall, but those collisions seemed to lead the jellyfish to reconsider the stripes, and soon the creatures treated them more like close roots in murky water and avoided them. The jellies' average distance from the wall was about 2.5 centimeters (1 inch) in the first couple of minutes in the tank, and by the final few minutes they were an average 3.6 centimeters (1.4 inches) from the wall; they also stopped bumping into the wall quite so much, with their average rate dropping from 1.8 bumps per minute to 0.78 per minute. "I found that really amazing," says Nagayasu Nakanishi, a biologist at the University of Arkansas in Fayetteville who has studied jellyfish nervous systems before but did not take part in the new work. "I never thought that jellyfish could really learn."
