# Objections To X's $1 Bot Fee

Critics of X's fee attack the premise that price is a deterrent bots cannot absorb, and add a second objection the deterrence argument ignores entirely: that the same price is not the same burden for every genuine user. On the first point, critics have pointed out that a small fee like $1 or even $8 won't stop all bots.

Mullenweg's version of the objection turns on what spammers already pay. He argued that spammers buy domains to use at a cost that's "usually a lot more than a dollar per year," and that "millions are used for spam or nefarious purposes" — so a one-dollar threshold sits below a cost bad actors demonstrably already clear. He also noted that spammers may use stolen credit cards and identities to conduct their transactions, which severs the assumed link between charging a fee and imposing a real cost on the payer. His conclusion concedes a short-run effect while denying the durable one: "Charging may cause a short-term drop in bots while the bad guys update their scripts, but the value of manipulating X/Twitter is so high I imagine there is already millions of dollars being spent on it," Mullenweg wrote on his blog.

The second objection concerns who else the friction catches. In addition to presenting friction at sign-up, other critics have argued a fee could exclude genuine users and increase the digital divide. Aditya Vashistha, an assistant professor of information science at Cornell University who studies computing systems in the Global South, made the point through a cross-country comparison: "Even the differences in the cost of living in the Philippines and New Zealand, the ability to afford $1 is not the same in the two countries." He accepted the deterrence rationale might hold and still rejected the policy on this ground: "Even if the rationale behind creating a paywall is right, such policies would broaden the digital divide, making it difficult for people in the Philippines to be part of a global community on X compared to those in New Zealand."

One thing the objections do not establish is novelty of the mechanism itself. Charging users a small fee to use a social platform is not a new idea: WhatsApp for years charged users a $1 annual fee, up until 2016, as a means of monetization ahead of its creation of commercial services.
