# Melissa Lucashenko: Family History, Late-Discovered Ancestry And A Writing Career

Melissa Lucashenko is a novelist preoccupied with survival who knows a lot about it personally. Born to an Aboriginal mother, Cecile, and a Russian-speaking Ukrainian father — Vladimir Lucashenko, known as Wally Lucas, whose family fled the Russian Revolution via China — she and her six older brothers grew up on the outskirts of Logan, south of Brisbane, in circumstances where the chances of becoming an author, let alone a celebrated one, were remote. "My father was a meatworker and a cane cutter … Mum worked at a fish and chip shop and sold plants at the local markets," she says. "Mum left school in about grade three or four. She was a domestic from a very young age, as her mother and grandmother were. At one point as a young mother she was working three jobs. Dad went to high school but I'm not sure how far he got. As a child, he was in an orphanage in Brisbane at one point with his brothers, after his father was violent, and his mother needed to escape."

She did not know she was of Aboriginal descent until she was 15, when someone she knew "picked it" and told her, "You're Aboriginal … go home and ask your parents." Around that time a photo appeared on her mother's dresser of "this very black woman"; when she asked who it was, her mother said, "Oh, that's my grandmother. That's why we've all got olive skin and dark hair." Lucashenko was shocked and felt "momentary disquiet", something she attributes to internalised racism, but says it "instantly made sense". "For the 40 years since then, I've been learning the culture and I've had different teachers from different mobs and I've taught other people what I know." She later learnt that her grandmother Eleanor had defeated an attempt by authorities to remove her as a child in about 1907 or 1908, in Wolvi in Queensland's Gympie region, and that her great-grandmother Christina Copson had worked as a domestic servant from the age of eight: "She was a slave, she had no rights." The family came under extra scrutiny after Copson shot and injured a man who tried to rape her; she was arrested and jailed for a week, told the judge she had acted in self-defence, and was found not guilty, with her perpetrator jailed for six months instead. The would-be rapist was an Aboriginal man — "No way she would have gotten away with shooting a white assailant," Lucashenko says.

Her career path ran through work and education before writing. On leaving school in 1983 at the end of year 12 she worked as a barmaid, a delivery truck driver and a support worker for women out of prison; she used to be obsessed with karate and has a black belt in it. In 1990 she graduated from Griffith University with an honours degree in public policy, the first in her immediate family to go to university — "My mother had no education and was determined that her daughter would have the chances she didn't. There was no skipping school in my household. I was always in the library if I wasn't on the back of a horse ... I was taken up to the headmaster's office in grade one and made to read aloud and they all oohed and ahhed." She published her first book, Steam Pigs, in 1997; it won the Dobbie Literary Award for women's fiction and she was away, publishing three more books over the next 15 or so years. In 2018 she told The Sydney Morning Herald that "Australia is a profoundly racist country and that is why I write more than anything else". She married Bill, a foreign aid specialist for AusAID, had a son and a daughter, and the family moved around for his job, living in Canberra and on overseas postings including Tonga, eventually settling on her ancestral land, Bundjalung, in the northern coastal area of NSW, buying a property and raising horses. That idyll ended in 2007 with divorce: she could not afford to stay on the land and worried about becoming a "bag lady", and around that time her 16-year-old daughter was hospitalised with mental health difficulties and the two moved back to Logan City, poor again. As she said in a 2013 Melbourne Writers' Festival address: "I knew what life was like in a suburb where the majority of people were ordinary, decent Australians, but a significant minority were prepared to sell their children's Ritalin in order to fund a heroin habit. Like some kind of weirdly reverse Charles Ryder coming upon Brideshead, I'd been there before. I knew all about it." When she won the Miles Franklin in 2019 for Too Much Lip — a novel the judges called "a novel of celebratory defiance" — her first reaction was "Bugger me dead!", which the profile says neatly sums her up: unpretentious, frank and on easy terms with vernacular.
