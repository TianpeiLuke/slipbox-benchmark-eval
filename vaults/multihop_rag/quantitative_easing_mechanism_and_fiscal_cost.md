# Quantitative Easing: Mechanism And Fiscal Cost

Quantitative easing effectively swaps long-term borrowing costs for short-term ones — a relation that was a lucrative deal for taxpayers when official interest rates were low, and that has now turned into a disastrous trade as rates have risen. The clearest depiction of the problem is in the UK, where the Bank of England secured taxpayer indemnity for any losses on QE: over the next decade, it estimates, its purchases will cost the government over £200 billion ($243 billion).

The tool's earlier record is also mixed. With a more flexible approach to the 2% targets, monetary policy after the 2008 financial crisis would have looked very different in many parts of the world: trillions of dollars, euros, yen and pounds of asset purchases did little to raise prices in the face of global disinflationary forces, until governments used the money they raised to stuff cash into consumers' pockets during Covid lockdowns.
