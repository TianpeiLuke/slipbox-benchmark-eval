# Open Source

Open source, in the corpus's AI-industry usage, is the label companies apply to AI models, tools, and frameworks whose code or methodology they make available for outside use, positioned as the opposite pole from a "closed" or "proprietary" approach where a single company controls access.
The term is contested rather than binary: TechCrunch's own reporting shows companies claiming the "open source" label while attaching licensing restrictions, so the word functions as a marketing and strategic banner as much as a precise technical category.

## Context

The corpus uses "open source" mainly to describe a strategic fault line in generative AI between companies favoring open distribution of models (Meta, Mistral AI, Hugging Face, Stability AI) and companies favoring proprietary control (OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Anthropic).
Meta's Yann LeCun and roughly 70 co-signatories published an open letter arguing that "increasing public access and scrutiny makes technology safer, not more dangerous," directly rebutting the argument from OpenAI and Google DeepMind that proprietary AI is safer than open source.
A leaked internal Google memo ("we have no moat, and neither does OpenAI") treated the rise of open source AI models — following a Meta foundation model leak in March 2023 — as an existential threat to proprietary LLM providers, because openness lets a broader community collaborate and experiment in ways closed models cannot match.
Meta went on to co-found the AI Alliance with IBM to promote "open innovation" and "open source AI models and frameworks," while pointed omissions from that alliance's membership (Google, OpenAI, Microsoft, Anthropic, Cohere, Adept) map directly onto the open-versus-closed divide.
The label also shows up outside the Meta/OpenAI rivalry: French startup Mistral AI builds "open source large language models," Giskard builds an "open source testing framework" for LLMs, OpenAI's own Whisper speech-recognition system is described as open source, and Auto-GPT is an open-source app built atop OpenAI's proprietary GPT models — showing open and closed components routinely coexist in the same AI stack.
One VC analysis of the generative AI tech stack expects the foundation-model layer to settle into an oligopoly "with the addition of a strong open source contingency that will drive considerable third-party adoption," treating open source as a distinct, persistent segment of the market rather than a transitional phase.

## Key Characteristics

- **Positioned against "proprietary" / "closed"** — the corpus consistently frames open source as the opposite pole from proprietary, centrally-controlled AI development.
- **Safety argument cuts both ways** — proprietary-AI advocates (OpenAI, Google DeepMind) argue closed control limits catastrophic misuse; open source advocates (LeCun and signatories) argue public scrutiny makes technology safer, not more dangerous.
- **The label is contestable** — Meta calls Llama open source, but TechCrunch reports licensing terms (a ban on using Llama to train other models, and a special-license requirement for apps with over 700 million monthly users) that restrict use for large competitors, so the "open source" claim is disputed even by outlets covering it.
- **Used as coalition-building rhetoric** — the AI Alliance's founding materials cast "open innovation" and "open science" against a vision that would "relegate AI innovation... to a small number of companies with a closed, proprietary vision," turning openness into a membership and marketing signal.
- **Spans models, tools, and frameworks** — the corpus applies "open source" to LLMs (Mistral's models, Llama), a testing framework (Giskard), a speech-recognition system (Whisper), and a task-automation app (Auto-GPT), not just to full foundation models.

## Background (external)

Outside the corpus, "open source" has a formal technical definition: the Open Source Initiative (OSI) specifies that open-source software's distribution terms must satisfy ten criteria — including free redistribution, availability of source code, and permission to create derived works — and that access to source code alone is not sufficient to qualify (Open Source Initiative, "The Open Source Definition," https://opensource.org/osd, accessed 2026-09-02).
That formal criteria-based definition does not appear anywhere in the corpus; the corpus instead uses "open source" informally, as a contested marketing and strategic label rather than a precise technical category (see Definition and Key Characteristics above).
