# Plea Agreement

## Definition

In the corpus, a plea agreement is the deal by which a criminal defendant (or, in Binance's case, a company) pleads guilty to specified charges in exchange for concessions, rather than going to trial.
It takes effect only once a judge accepts it, and it can bind the party to concrete obligations — cooperating with prosecutors, accepting a recommended fine, stepping back from a role, or admitting specific facts — beyond the bare admission of guilt.

## Context

The corpus shows plea agreements as the mechanism by which several defendants in overlapping 2023 white-collar and criminal cases resolved their charges outside of a trial.
FTX co-founder Gary Wang and Alameda Research CEO Caroline Ellison both pleaded guilty in December 2022 and agreed to cooperate with prosecutors, becoming key witnesses against Sam Bankman-Fried at his fraud trial (doc_0010, doc_0086).
FTX engineering head Nishad Singh similarly pleaded guilty to six charges (doc_0121).
Binance CEO Changpeng "CZ" Zhao and Binance itself each entered separate plea agreements with the U.S. Department of Justice, resolving anti-money-laundering and sanctions violations as part of a $4.3 billion settlement (doc_0374, doc_0375).
YouTube parenting influencer Ruby Franke's plea agreement, accepted by a judge, both formalized her guilty plea to child-abuse charges and documented detailed admissions about the abuse itself (doc_0269).
The corpus also shows a distinct but related instrument — the cooperation agreement Gary Wang signed, which specifically obligated him to meet with prosecutors and answer truthfully (doc_0226) — and notes that a witness's incentive to plead guilty and cooperate (the hope of sentencing leniency) is something a defense team can attack on cross-examination (doc_0293).

## Key Characteristics

- **Court acceptance is required** — a judge must accept the plea agreement before it takes effect; Ruby Franke's sentencing was scheduled only after Judge John J. Walton accepted her plea agreement (doc_0269).
- **Negotiated concessions, not just an admission** — the document can specify what each side gives up: Zhao agreed to a recommended $150 million CFTC fine and to not contradict his acceptance of responsibility, while Binance's agreement barred Zhao from any future role at the company for three years and required it to maintain an independent compliance monitor (doc_0374).
- **Can include cooperation with prosecutors** — Wang and Ellison's guilty pleas were paired with plans to cooperate with, and testify for, the prosecution (doc_0010); Wang's cooperation obligations were spelled out in a separate signed agreement (doc_0226).
- **Distinct from pleading not guilty** — a defendant who instead maintains innocence, as Bankman-Fried did, pleads not guilty and proceeds to trial rather than entering a plea agreement (doc_0010).
- **Can contain substantive factual admissions** — Franke's plea agreement recorded specific admitted details of the abuse itself, beyond the bare fact of guilt (doc_0269).
- **Creates a cross-examination target** — because cooperating witnesses who pleaded guilty may hope for sentencing leniency, defense counsel can (and, per the corpus, did) argue this gives them an incentive to shape testimony favorably to the prosecution (doc_0293).

## Background (external)

Outside the corpus, the general legal definition is broader than any single case above: a plea bargain (or plea agreement) is an agreement between a defendant and a prosecutor in which the defendant agrees to plead guilty to some or all of the charges, usually in exchange for a concession from the prosecutor, such as a reduced charge or a more lenient sentencing recommendation.
