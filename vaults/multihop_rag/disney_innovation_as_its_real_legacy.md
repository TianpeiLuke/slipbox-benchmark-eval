# Innovation Is Disney's Real Legacy

Polygon argues that Disney's biggest legacy is not the princesses, the theme parks, or even Mickey Mouse, but the fact that all those elements started out as daring projects no one else was doing.

The grounds are a record of risk. Over the past 100 years Disney has taken big, risky swings and fumbled many times: the dark and dreary Black Cauldron almost bankrupted the studio in 1985, and the off-kilter adventure movies of the early 2000s flopped, with the studio's mold-breaking passion projects often turning into megaflops. Yet some of the company's most memorable movies stemmed from those same risks. The original 1940 Fantasia was one of Walt Disney's most ambitious passion projects turned box-office failures, but the Sorcerer Mickey short is as iconic as the mouse himself. Lilo & Stitch was so weird that the filmmakers basically hid it from the rest of the company, and it ended up making back more than three times its budget while turning Stitch into one of the studio's most memorable characters. Even The Little Mermaid, the film that kickstarted the Disney Renaissance, was a risk, since a big Broadway-style musical of its type had not been done in animation before. The warrant is Walt Disney himself, who famously pushed for progress and innovation in the studio's creative endeavors even when that meant poor financial decisions; what has made the studio special is how it looked forward, trying new things in the face of failure and taking chances other production houses could not afford or risk. Polygon's complaint is that Disney and its fans keep forgetting this, clinging to the past with such ardor that it does their idol's legacy a disservice.
