# Disinformation On X After The Hamas Attacks On Israel

In the days after Saturday's Hamas attacks on Israel, posts identified as false circulated widely on X, including footage repurposed from a video game and clips entirely unrelated to the conflict. TechCrunch reported the pattern in two articles, on 10 and 13 October 2023, drawing on fact-checkers, public media, civil society organisations and X's own safety account.

The observed content fell into several kinds. Graphic videos apparently showing terrorist attacks on civilians circulated on X from Saturday onward. Alongside them were posts purporting to show footage from the attacks inside Israel, or from Israel's subsequent retaliation on targets in the Gaza Strip, which fact-checkers identified as false. A number of videos posted since the attacks were identified as entirely unrelated to the conflict — including footage filmed the previous month in Egypt, and a clip from the video game Arma 3 posted with the false claim that it showed Hamas missile attacks on Israel. Shayan Sardarizadeh (@Shayan86) flagged the Arma 3 clip on October 9, 2023: "This video certainly doesn't show a new air assault on Israel by Hamas militants, because it's actually from the video game Arma 3."

The volume was substantial. In remarks via X's safety account, the company reported more than 50 million posts globally over the past two days that referenced the weekend's terrorist attack, underlining the scope of content generated.

Two conditions shaped the environment. First, the attacks themselves: Hamas militants inside Gaza got past border fences and mounted a series of surprise attacks on Israeli civilians and tourists, after which Israel's prime minister declared "we are at war" and its military retaliated by firing scores of missiles into the Gaza Strip. By Thursday afternoon, per TechCrunch's 13 October report, Hamas' attack had killed more than 1,200 people according to the Israeli Military, and at least 1,537 people had been killed in Gaza by Israel's retaliatory strikes according to the Gaza Ministry of Health. Second, amplification on the platform itself: Eliot Higgins (@EliotHiggins) wrote on October 10, 2023 that "a totally false claim about Israel bombing a church in Gaza" had gone viral "thanks to multiple blue tick accounts repeating an unverified claim that had no evidence to back it up", adding that "Musk has created a fundamental issue with Twitter's credibility in moments of crisis."

X's owner was himself part of the observed pattern: at one point Musk recommended that people follow accounts that had posted antisemitic comments and false information in the past, though he subsequently deleted the tweet in which he made the suggestion. A Wired report published the day before TechCrunch's 10 October article summed up the situation on the platform under the headline "The Israel-Hamas War Is Drowning X in Disinformation."
