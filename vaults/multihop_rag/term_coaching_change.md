# Coaching Change

## Definition

A coaching change is the replacement of a team's head coach — by firing, mutual departure, or resignation — followed by the appointment of a successor, whether an outside hire, an internal promotion, or an interim/caretaker placeholder.

## Context

The corpus documents coaching changes across sports and settings: mid-season firings in the NHL and NFL, off-season managerial exits in club and international soccer/rugby, and speculative hot-seat coverage that treats a coach's job security itself as an ongoing storyline ahead of any actual move.

The Minnesota Wild "relieved head coach Dean Evason and assistant coach Bob Woods of their duties effective immediately," naming John Hynes as Evason's successor mid-season after a losing stretch left the team "far out of the playoff picture" (doc_0340).

Manchester United's Erik ten Hag was reported to be under mounting pressure, with Sporting News laying out replacement candidates — including permanent hires like Zinedine Zidane and Julen Lopetegui and a caretaker option in club legend Michael Carrick — "if Manchester United decide to pull the plug" on his tenure (doc_0315); the same piece notes United had already cycled through eight managers, including caretakers Ryan Giggs, Ralf Rangnick, and Carrick, since Alex Ferguson's 2013 retirement.

Carolina Panthers owner David Tepper's impatience with head coach Frank Reich was framed against his earlier in-season firing of Reich's predecessor, Matt Rhule, as precedent for how quickly an owner might act (doc_0474); Yardbarker's NFC "hot seat" roundup names head coaches such as New Orleans' Dennis Allen and Chicago's Matt Eberflus as under pressure for their job status ahead of any actual move (doc_0437).

In international coaching, the Springboks' World Cup-winning head coach Jacques Nienaber and director of rugby Rassie Erasmus departed together after South Africa's 2023 Rugby World Cup triumph, and All Blacks coach Ian Foster's tenure ended the same tournament with Scott Robertson set to take over (doc_0022); U.S. Soccer's appointment of Emma Hayes away from Chelsea to lead the USWNT followed a formal, multi-round search process after the prior head coach, Vlatko Andonovski, departed following a World Cup exit (doc_0108).

## Key Characteristics

- **Trigger is performance or results** — the Wild's move came after a 5-10-4 record and a seven-game winless streak (doc_0340); the Panthers' pressure on Reich stemmed from a 1-7 record and a struggling offense (doc_0474).
- **In-season vs. off-season timing** — some changes happen mid-season as an emergency reset (Wild firing Evason, doc_0340; the precedent of Carolina firing Rhule in-season, doc_0474), while others follow a season's end (Nienaber/Erasmus and Foster departing after the World Cup final, doc_0022; Hayes's move to the USWNT set for after her Chelsea season concludes, doc_0108).
- **Successor types vary** — a permanent outside hire (John Hynes for the Wild, doc_0340; Emma Hayes for the USWNT, doc_0108), a permanent internal promotion (Evason himself had been promoted to Wild head coach after the team fired Bruce Boudreau in-season, doc_0340), or a caretaker/interim stopgap (Michael Carrick's past spells in charge of Manchester United, doc_0315).
- **"Hot seat" speculation precedes the actual move** — media coverage frequently discusses a head coach's job security, and the club's history of prior changes, well before any change occurs, treating pressure itself as newsworthy (doc_0437, doc_0474).
- **The exiting coach's own track record and contract carry forward** — Evason's Wild contract obligations continued after his firing (doc_0340), and Reich's overall coaching record was reported inclusive of a prior in-season firing by the Colts (doc_0474).
