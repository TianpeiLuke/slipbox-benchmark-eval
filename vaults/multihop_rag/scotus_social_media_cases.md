# The Supreme Court's Pair Of Social Media Moderation Cases

The Supreme Court's social media cases are a pair of challenges, which the Court decided it would hear in the week before this October 4, 2023 report, over whether two state laws are allowed to reshape the ability of social media companies to control what does — and doesn't — appear on their platforms. The laws at issue were crafted by Republicans as state-specific measures that order platforms to keep their hands off of some social media posts.

The defining feature of the pair is their shared political origin. Since the early days of the Trump administration, Republicans have accused social media companies of deliberately suppressing conservative viewpoints, and conservative lawmakers in Florida and Texas passed laws to restrict how those companies are allowed to moderate content — whether animated by those perceived differences or by the political perks of accusing social platforms of anti-conservative bias.

The boundary case worth keeping is the evidentiary one. Research has not supported the claims of deliberate suppression of conservative viewpoints. What researchers have demonstrated is something adjacent but different: conservative social media users are disproportionately exposed to political misinformation, a phenomenon that could explain anecdotal claims of ideologically lopsided enforcement on social platforms. Exposure to misinformation, and the enforcement that follows it, is not the same finding as viewpoint-based suppression.

To explain the Supreme Court's decision to wade into these issues — and what happens next — TechCrunch spoke to Paul Barrett, NYU adjunct law professor and Deputy Director of NYU Stern's Center for Business and Human Rights.
