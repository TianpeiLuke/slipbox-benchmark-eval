# How Counterfeit Jet Engine Parts Are Detected — And Why AOG's Were Hard To Catch

AOG is understood to have sold mainly smaller parts for jet engines — bolts, nuts, washers, dampers and seals — which do not carry a serial number and are therefore harder to trace, though some bigger parts appear linked to the scandal and some turbine blades have also been identified by CFM as suspect. CFM is understood to believe AOG had been passing off old parts for new ones.

The detection process has two stages, according to Steve Borrowdale, managing director at Multiflight, a Leeds-based engineer and parts supplier, who says sneaking parts into a jet engine is difficult. After vetting a seller for experience and approval certification, a buyer would also carefully inspect a part from a new supplier: "You get the box, you'd have a look, just for simple things. If there's a brand-new part coming in a non-identified box, you might start to question it." Choosing parts without serial numbers could make falsifying documents easier, he said, but it would still take great skill — "You couldn't just take a novice off the street and start selling these things, you would have to have gotten some training somewhere along the way." The environment such a seller operates in is a dazzling patchwork of thousands of often small manufacturers, brokers and distributors. According to filings at Companies House, AOG grew slowly from 2015 until 2019, when the company had net assets of £22,000 ($42,000).
