# Cannabis Use in Pregnancy: JAMA Study Findings

A study published Tuesday, Dec. 12, 2023 in the journal JAMA suggests that using cannabis during pregnancy may come with a higher risk of poor outcomes such as babies being born small for their gestational age, with the risks highest for those who use the drug in both the first trimester and later in gestation, as opposed to only the first trimester.

"What I think this study evaluates, that has not been evaluated previously, is whether the timing and amount of exposure matters, and what we saw was that higher levels of cannabis exposure were associated with increased risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes," first study author Dr. Torri Metz, an associate professor of obstetrics and gynecology at University of Utah Health, told Live Science in an email; using cannabis in the first trimester still carries risk, but the findings hint that stopping within the first trimester could help prevent that risk from growing larger. Urine screening flagged 610 participants who had used cannabis in pregnancy — nearly 200 used cannabis only in the first trimester, while the rest tested positive both in the first trimester and later in pregnancy. Participants who used cannabis had a higher risk of experiencing at least one of the poor pregnancy outcomes, nearly 26%, compared with 17.5% in the group that did not use cannabis; the risk was highest for those who used cannabis late into pregnancy and also appeared to rise with the overall concentration of THC byproduct in a person's urine. The researchers initially considered the pregnancy outcomes as a group rather than separately because, as Metz put it, "we knew we did not have a large enough sample to examine all of these outcomes independently," but a secondary analysis zoomed in on specific outcomes and found cannabis use linked to a higher chance that a baby would be born small, while not linked to a higher risk of high-blood-pressure disorders in pregnancy. "I think this lends more evidence to the idea that there are potential risks, and really talk to patients about alternative methods of treating whatever it is they're hoping to treat with cannabis," Metz told JAMA in a podcast interview.
