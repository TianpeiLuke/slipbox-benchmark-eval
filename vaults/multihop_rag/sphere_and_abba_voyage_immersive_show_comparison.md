# Sphere And ABBA Voyage As Two Models Of Immersion

The Sydney Morning Herald's 19 October 2023 report sets out a two-part model of immersive concert design: ABBA Voyage in London pushes its effects out toward the audience, while Sphere more wholly and subtly envelops it — the only approximate comparison available for either.

The relation holds because both are wholly enveloping experiences where lights, sound and special effects are blended to push the audience's senses to the limit; they differ in one key way, which is direction. ABBA's effects project outward, largely to play smoke and mirrors with the on-stage simulacrums; Sphere's surround the viewer. A second shared structural condition is the artist: "They are both legacy projects made by artists who have the career and track record to be able to look at these things objectively in a way that a younger performer perhaps wouldn't because it takes an entire lifetime of experience to make those kinds of choices and make it work," Williams says.

A third link is institutional. The two shows share the British music stage architecture firm Stufish, whose work ranges from the Queen's Platinum Jubilee to tours for Beyoncé and Jay-Z, Michael Bublé, Elton John and Madonna, as well as the ABBA Arena in London; Stufish partner Ric Lipson collaborated on the U2 project for Sphere along with artist Es Devlin and producer Brian Eno. Lipson also marks the limit of the model — the venues are not locked to one show: "With the ABBA Arena, it's absolutely designed for that show, but you could also imagine that building having another avatar show in there, or indeed a live performance where people would use the technology that's in the building as an immersive environment."
