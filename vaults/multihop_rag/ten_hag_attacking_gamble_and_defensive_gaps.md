# Ten Hag's Attacking Gamble And The Gaps It Leaves

The Guardian argues that Ten Hag is enacting a high-wire act — a daring gamble that United will gain more going forward than they lose going back — and that the Chelsea win is not yet evidence the gamble works.

The grounds are structural: if you commit a midfielder to McTominay's advanced role, you leave gaps elsewhere. One reason Chelsea found it so easy to play through United was that, with the forwards pushed high and McTominay as the secret sauce in the final third, there were open spaces every time they played through the press; Chelsea's goal was a case in point, two simple vertical passes straight through the centre via Mykhailo Mudryk to Cole Palmer, passes McTominay could see coming but could do precious little to stop. The warrant is that the result depended on finishing quality United cannot count on: on another night Chelsea score from one of these counterattacks, and in another game United face a better finisher than Nicolas Jackson, who for all his talent looks like a man playing football in a hall of mirrors. The bet is that lawless abandon will begin to resemble a sustainable attacking blueprint once Lisandro Martínez and Raphaël Varane come back, once Ten Hag brings the defensive line up 10 yards, once Rasmus Højlund hits some form and once Mason Mount gets up to speed — that he can shout down the noise in the short term while building something in the long term. In the raptured afterglow of a big win it felt like a gamble being won, but the writer's own caveat is to come back and check in a few days.
