# Smart Glasses Privacy And Bystander Consent

The Verge's reviewer argued that the privacy problem with camera glasses is not the camera but the missing social cue: we do not have the same signals for smart glasses that we have for phones. If you hold your phone up in a certain way, people know it means you are recording; if you hold your finger up to the temple of your glasses, you could be adjusting the fit. Meta's answer has been the LED capture light it added to the original Ray-Ban Stories — most people did not think it was enough, so this time the LED cannot be disabled and pulses in a pattern meant to be more noticeable. Polling co-workers, friends, and family produced a mixed bag: some said it was easily seen indoors, others disagreed, and most agreed that while you can see the light outdoors, it is also easy to ignore or mistake for light reflecting off the lenses. Her own evidence was blunter — she took pictures and filmed many times in public, and no one ever noticed.

The context has shifted since the backlash of the Glass era, when a pair of Google Glass got ripped off a person's face, wearers were dubbed glassholes, and some public spaces banned the device entirely. Now people wear Quest 3 headsets to coffee shops, TikTok is full of normal people and content creators vlogging their lives, and passersby hold very private conversations out loud on FaceTime or through AirPods; for better or worse, the smartphone has made everyone comfortable with the idea that there are cameras everywhere. Meta supplies privacy guidelines and etiquette tips at setup plus a link to its privacy page in the app, which boils down to: do not be a glasshole. That is nice, the reviewer wrote, but glassholes will use the device however they please, and the rest of us have to figure out how not to be one on the fly.

The unresolved cases are the argument's real payload. Invited to an outdoor wedding, she felt it would be wrong to decide unilaterally and asked her friend for permission; it was weird to explain, but her friend agreed, and she got to protect her eyes from the sun, discreetly record the ceremony for the couple, keep her phone in her bag, and stay present — and afterward the friend was glad to have the footage since there was no videographer. None of it appears in the review because the ceremony was private. Her question is whether anyone in 2023 would feel compelled to ask that with a phone, given how often people photograph weddings and post straight to their own feeds. The same ambiguity spreads: is filming concert footage being a glasshole; should a house hunter recording a property alert the real estate agent; what happens if someone wearing them as headphones enters a public restroom and a bystander freaks out; is asking permission from fellow commuters potentially opening yourself to physical harm; is a face-mounted bodycam an invasion of privacy or a safety tool — a co-worker bought a pair partly so that if someone tries to murder them while walking the dog, they can grab footage of the assailant. She has no real answers, and ends on a deflating one: a part of her wishes she would be the wary privacy stalwart, but she already lives as if everything in her life is surveilled, so what do a few hundred extra smart glasses cameras matter.
