# Discogs Seller Livelihoods

Discogs is now the source of many people's full-time employment. A European Discogs seller who has been on the platform since 2008 and requested anonymity for fear of retribution by the company told The Verge he does 80 percent of his business on the platform; he does not operate a brick-and-mortar storefront but has four employees and nets around €20,000 a month on Discogs. His sales, he said, have shrunk by half over the past year, and he is in the process of building his own site to try to move away from the platform.

A Connecticut seller, who also does the majority of his business on Discogs and requested anonymity for the same reason, said: "I've made my living with this company for the past decade. It's just the frustration that you have no control over what they're doing, and it doesn't even make any sense." Because the vinyl renaissance occurred in tandem with the growth of Discogs, the site is fairly integrated into any record business, whether or not that business has a brick-and-mortar storefront — so a major change to the site could mean a major shift in the record market as a whole.

Underlying the sellers' complaints, as reported on October 7th, 2023, is a kind of dismay: the feeling that what had previously been a safe haven for nerds to buy and sell $2 records is being threatened, and that one more corner of the internet that was not yet a glossy behemoth designed to subsume and capitalize on your personal information was about to collapse. "When you get any kind of community built around a business, and you tweak that a little bit, you're gonna make a lot of people upset," says Doug Martin. "This is their Discogs, they built it."
