# How Scorsese's Late Films Revisit His Earlier Themes

Polygon's model of Scorsese's late period, set out on 2023-10-26, is that in all his late-period films — along with the passel of rock documentaries made during this era, including two on Bob Dylan and one on George Harrison — he is consciously returning to certain pet subjects and periods in his work, not to repeat himself but to revise and add greater perspective.

The relations are specific and paired. 2002's Gangs of New York, about how organized crime was woven into the operation of Scorsese's native city from its inception, is a prequel — or ancestor — to his classics Goodfellas and Casino. 2019's The Irishman, about a man aging and dying with his guilt and sins unresolved at the end of a life of crime, is intended as a bookend to his crime trilogy with Robert De Niro and Joe Pesci. 2016's Silence is in conversation with, if not a synthesis of, the ideas explored in The Last Temptation of Christ and Kundun; all three are searching, anti-commercial religious films Scorsese struggled for years to get made.

The effect of the pattern is tonal as well as structural: there is a sense that he is trying to adjust his positions with the clear eye of an older person who has seen more of the world, dropped his pretensions, and lost his capacity for bullshit. His later films return to his old themes and obsessions, but in the process they have become more contemplative.
