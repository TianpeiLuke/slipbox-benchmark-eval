# "Pay Or Okay": The Cookie Paywall Model Meta Borrowed

The "pay or okay" model Meta is seeking to impose on EU users was not its invention. It was pioneered in Austria by the daily newspaper Der Standard, after which copycat cookie paywalls quickly sprang up on a raft of news publishers in Germany and elsewhere in the EU. noyb has been challenging this approach to GDPR consent since 2021, filing complaints with a number of data protection authorities and arguing the model forces newspaper readers to "buy back their own data at exorbitant prices."

The publisher case and the Meta case diverge on one feature. Some DPAs appear to have been sympathetic to local newspapers' use of cookie walls, seeing it as a way to support the production of journalism. When it comes to Meta, TechCrunch argues, that argument evaporates: Meta is definitely not in the journalism business, and moreover the adtech giant does not even need to produce content to pump around its social networks — it gets all that filler for free from the same users it is now demanding pay a fee if they want to use its services without being tracked and profiled for behavioral advertising, which makes Meta's model feel like even more of a rip off.

The legal boundary of the model remains unsettled. Back in April 2023, a decision by Austria's DPA on a noyb complaint about cookie paywalls said users must have the ability to say yes or no to specific data operations, meaning blanket consent is not an option. But the result left it unclear how cookie paywalls might be operated in a way that is GDPR compliant, and the privacy rights group vowed to fight the decision in court, predicting at the time that "the final decision on 'pay or okay' may be made by the European Court of Justice (CJEU) in the long run."
