# The Internet As A Global Endocrine System

Buck's model of what we have actually built is a collective body that runs on data but does not behave computationally: the thing we are jacked into and collectively creating seems more like a global endocrine system than anything we might have visualized in the years while "cyber" was a prefix.

The proposal begins from a mismatch with the 1960s inheritance. The ecology and spirituality of the 1960s, which shaped and structured much of what we see as energy democracy and the good future today, told us we were all connected — globally networked, a fevered dream from the 1980s or 1990s with roots in the 1960s and before. Media theorist Geert Lovink reflects on a 1996 interview with John Perry Barlow, Electronic Frontier Foundation cofounder and Grateful Dead lyricist, in which Barlow described cyberspace connecting each and every synapse of all citizens on the planet. As Lovink writes: "Apart from the so-called last billion we're there now. This is what we can all agree on. The corona crisis is the first Event in World History where the internet doesn't merely play 'a role' — the Event coincides with the Net. There's a deep irony to this. The virus and the network ... sigh, that's an old trope, right?" Read through one cultural history it seems obvious we would reach this point of being globally networked, and that the Internet would not just play a role in global events like COVID-19 or climate change but shape them.

The relation Buck proposes is that the connectedness arrived, but manifesting late and not at all as imagined: our global body is neither a psychedelic collective consciousness nor an infrastructure for data transmission comprising information packets and code. We have made a collective brain that does not act much like a computer at all — it runs on data, code, binary digits, but acts emotionally, irrationally, in a fight-or-flight way, and without consciousness, operating as an emotional toddler rather than with the neat computational sensing capacity that stock graphics of "the Internet" convey. Thinking of it as data or information is the same as thinking that a network of cells is a person.

The observation may seem banal — Marshall McLuhan was talking about the global nervous system more than fifty years ago, and we have had enthusiasm about cybernetics and global connectivity over the decades plus a more recent revitalization of theory about networks and kinship and rhizomes. The irony is that with fifty years of talk on "systems thinking" we still have responses to things like COVID-19 or climate that are almost antithetical to considering interconnected systems, dominated by one set of expertise and failing to incorporate the social sciences and humanities. So: globally connected, yet divided into silos, camps, echo-chambers. Social media platforms act as agents, structuring our interactions and our spaces for dialogue and solution-building — and authoritarians know this, which is why they have troll farms that can manipulate the range of solutions and the sentiments about them.
