# The Commission's Defence Of Political Ad Targeting And The Channels MEPs Say It Bypassed

The dispute at the LIBE hearing resolved into three fixed positions that did not meet: Johansson held that the Commission may legitimately promote its own legislative proposals and that the detail of the ad campaign belonged to her "service", MEPs held that political microtargeting is anti-democratic because privileged channels for talking to co-legislators already exist, and the committee held no lever to force an answer within the session. TechCrunch reported the exchange on 25 October 2023.

The first relation is deflection by attribution. The LIBE committee repeatedly pressed Johansson to provide detail about the microtargeted ad campaign, but she declined to do so, saying she did not have any information about it and that it was for her "service", who she suggested had been responsible for the campaign, to answer. The consequence within the hearing was a specific unanswered question: there was no explanation about why, for instance, Christians had been explicitly excluded from the Commission's microtargeting.

The second relation is defence by generality and by precedent. She avoided giving a direct response to accusations by MEPs that the use of political microtargeting by the Commission was anti-democratic, opting instead to mount a general defence of its right to promote its proposals, and she listed a number of other departments within the Commission she said had previously used ads to promote separate legislative proposals. "I think that the commission should defend and explain and promote our proposals. We do that and we have done that. And I think it's a good practice to do so. Because we are we are taking stance and we should defend our stance," she told the committee.

The third relation is the counter-principle offered by MEPs, which does not deny the Commission's interest in its proposals but denies that behavioural ad targeting is an admissible means of pursuing it. A number of MEPs pushed back, including by pointing out that there are more appropriate channels for the Commission to engage directly and transparently with co-legislators than opaque behavioral ad targeting on platforms like Twitter/X. "One principle of democracy is that we have procedures because the end doesn't justify the means," opined MEP Sophie in 't Veld. "And European Commission has the right to be very attached to its legislative proposals but there are privileged channels for the European Commission to communicate with the two legislators and others — not an ad campaign on Twitter."

The range over which this structure held is the hearing itself, and it ended without resolution. Despite a lot of pushback, the committee was unable to extract any other lines from the commissioner on the ad campaign, though at the end of the session she did agree to respond to it in writing with some missing answers "as soon as possible" — while avoiding agreeing to do so by the end of the week, as one MEP had asked.

The ad campaign was not the only line of questioning. While many of the questions directed at her over the 1.5-hour long hearing focused on the controversy that had sprung up around the ad campaign, parliamentarians also pressed the commissioner on a number of other issues, including concerns about the extent of commercial lobbying around the CSAM-scanning proposal. That had been a topic of intense interest, especially following a report by investigative journalists published the previous month by BalkanInsight which looked at close contacts between Johansson's department and companies with CSAM-scanning and other child safety tools to sell.
