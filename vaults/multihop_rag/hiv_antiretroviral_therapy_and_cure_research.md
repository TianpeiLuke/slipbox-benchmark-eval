# HIV Antiretroviral Therapy And Cure Research

HIV has gone from a near-fatal prognosis to a chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy, and antiretroviral therapy works by limiting viral replication at several levels of cell development. The first ART started with azidothymidine in the late 90s, which belongs to the nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor class; the current regimen is a combination of NRTIs, nonnucleoside NRTIs, protease and integrase inhibitors, and casts a heavy pill burden apart from side effects. The relations between drug class and stage are specified: NNRTI and NRTI act at the cellular level, protease inhibitors work at the maturation phase, and integrase inhibitors at the integration phase of the viral lifecycle. Truvada is the first approved pre-exposure prophylaxis treatment for those at risk of viral exposure, working at a very early stage of exposure with high success when taken regularly in pill form. Treatment resistance to ARTs from prior exposure is an increasing concern in HIV patients, and Gilead's Lenacapavir, approved last year, is a single-pill treatment regimen for patients with multi-drug resistance that also addresses the pill burden.

The model's boundary is explicit: by limiting viral replication, ARTs by design cannot cure or lead to remission of HIV. The cure route runs elsewhere. By pinpointing a cohort of patients whose viral load was under control with limited treatment, researchers have identified broadly neutralising antibodies, or bNAbs, as a potential cure; antibodies deal with viruses but fail to do so in HIV infection, so by engineering bNAbs in a manner similar to mABs for cancer, or to vaccines where antibody reaction to a neutered virus generates immune protection, an HIV cure may be on the horizon. Gilead has several Phase-2 programmes with ART combinations in progress, and studies as recently as February 2023 presented proof of concept, with 90 per cent of participants showing virological suppression at 26 weeks. Incidentally, Gilead's Sovaldi and Harvoni were the first cures for Hepatitis C, a virus the World Health Organization aims to eradicate by 2030.
