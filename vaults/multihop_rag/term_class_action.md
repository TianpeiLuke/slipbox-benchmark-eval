# Class Action

## Definition

A class action, as it appears across this corpus, is a single lawsuit pursued on behalf of a group of similarly affected people rather than filed by each person individually.

The corpus documents several such suits from 2023: FTX customers against crypto promoters including Paradigm, a news publisher against Google, consumers against PayPal, and the Authors Guild against OpenAI.

It also shows the mechanism can be blocked by contract, as with Uber's terms of use barring class actions in sexual-assault cases.

## Context

The corpus shows the mechanism used across very different disputes: FTX customers brought a class-action lawsuit accusing crypto investment firm Paradigm and other promoters of defrauding customers by promoting the exchange, publisher Helena World Chronicle filed a class action antitrust suit against Google and Alphabet under the Sherman Act alleging AI tools like Search Generative Experience and Bard siphon off publishers' content and ad revenue, and consumers represented by law firm Hagens Berman filed a class action against PayPal over anti-steering rules that allegedly stifle competition from cheaper platforms like Stripe and Shopify.

The Authors Guild of America, representing Stephen King, John Grisham and 134 other authors, likewise filed its own class-action suit against OpenAI over unauthorized use of copyrighted books in training data, run in parallel to (but distinct from) comedian Sarah Silverman's separate, non-class suit.

Apple's earlier MacBook Pro keyboard class-action settlement is cited in the corpus as a business driver — the corpus notes the settlement cost probably factored into Apple's decision to redesign the keyboard, a change introduced alongside separate updates like a new camera and the removal of the Touch Bar.

## Key Characteristics

- **Collective standing** — a class action lets one or a few plaintiffs (e.g., Helena World Chronicle, the Authors Guild, unnamed FTX customers) litigate on behalf of an entire group with the same underlying grievance, rather than requiring each affected person to sue separately.
- **Can be contractually blocked** — the corpus shows companies can preempt this mechanism: under Uber's terms of use, class action lawsuits cannot be filed against the company in sexual assault cases, forcing survivors to litigate individually and preventing them from advocating for themselves collectively.
- **Used across dispute types** — the corpus applies the same mechanism to fraud (FTX), antitrust (Helena World Chronicle v. Google, PayPal anti-steering), and copyright (Authors Guild v. OpenAI) claims, showing it is a general procedural tool rather than one tied to a single area of law.
- **Settlement exposure shapes corporate behavior** — Apple's keyboard class-action settlement is presented in the corpus as a concrete cost that influenced a subsequent product redesign decision.
- **Defendants, not just plaintiffs, can be named as a group** — the FTX-related suit accuses Paradigm "and others" collectively of defrauding customers, showing the mechanism can target a class of co-defendants as well as aggregate a class of claimants.

## Background (external)

Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute (Wex) defines a class action generically as "a procedural device that permits one or more plaintiffs to file and prosecute a lawsuit on behalf of a larger group, or 'class'" (Cornell Law School, Legal Information Institute, Wex: "class action," accessed 2026-09-02, https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/class_action).
