# Defense Opening Statement: The Plane Metaphor

Defense attorney Mark Cohen answered the prosecution's opening not with evidence but with a metaphor: working at a startup, he told the jury with the energy of a patient father telling his obnoxious children a bedtime story, was like building a plane while flying it, and FTX the plane had flown right into the perfect storm of the crypto crash. The premise this attacks is the prosecution's claim of intentional theft — Cohen's counter was that Bankman-Fried "acted in good faith and took reasonable business measures," that FTX's and Alameda's business relationships were "reasonable under the circumstances," and, in the common white-collar defense form, that Bankman-Fried as CEO was simply too busy to oversee what everyone did every day. He also blamed Caroline Ellison and Binance CEO Changpeng "CZ" Zhao for the debacle.

The Verge's reporter judged the counter-argument self-defeating, because Cohen volunteered a fact the prosecution had not raised: FTX "didn't have a chief risk officer, which became an issue when the storm hit." That admission cuts against the good-faith premise — the lack of a risk officer kept elbowing her in the ribs as Cohen argued reasonableness, and the too-busy-to-supervise line made her more suspicious, since delegating oversight is exactly why you hire a risk officer. Cohen's own characterisation of his client worked the same way: Bankman-Fried was a math nerd who did not party, which paints a picture of someone deliberate, especially given that he left MIT immediately and went to work on Wall Street; a party-hardy trainwreck might plausibly overlook hiring a risk officer, but a deliberate person would not.

The context was that the defense had no plea to fall back on: the reporter had learned the day before that Bankman-Fried had never been offered a plea deal, since he and his attorneys had told the government they would not negotiate, which made her curious what evidence had made him so confident. The answer, she wrote, was instead a metaphor — and it is no good when your defense lawyer has just made you sound worse than the prosecution already did.
