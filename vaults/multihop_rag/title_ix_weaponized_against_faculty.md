# Title IX Weaponized Against Faculty

The Verge argued that Title IX, a well-intentioned Obama-era policy to protect students, has had unintended side effects: it has empowered victims of harassment and sexual violence but has also weakened due process, and has in some cases been manipulated and weaponized against people on campuses who are from marginalized and vulnerable groups, including faculty. The grounds are a pattern of documented false accusations; the warrant is that a complaint process built to believe and support accusers gives an accuser leverage regardless of the truth of the claim, and that leverage lands hardest on those the surrounding systems already disadvantage.

The policy history sets the scope. In 2011, citing the alarmingly high rates of rape on campuses, the Obama administration began calling for colleges and universities to investigate accusations of assault with greater urgency and rigor; two years later Obama signed the Campus Sexual Violence Elimination Act, which strengthened civil rights under Title IX, the federal law enacted in 1972 to prohibit gender-based discrimination in educational institutions and programs. That move required institutions to use more stringent methods to investigate and judge, and offered more guidelines for believing and supporting those who allege rape, assault, or sexual harassment. Under the Trump administration some of these policies were rolled back and changed, allowing accused individuals more due process protections. Many universities have historically mishandled student allegations of rape and sexual misconduct, and Title IX became a crucial tool in curbing discrimination and harassment against students and employees based on sex.

The evidence that the saga of S. is far from an anomaly comes from other academics. Professor and journalist Sarah Viren detailed the false sexual harassment accusations against herself and her wife filed with the Title IX Office at Arizona State University, and in a follow-up podcast interviewed other academics who reached out after she shared her story, including a Mexican American professor accused of sleeping with students and a professor up for tenure accused of harassment by a student she had never met. In one glaring example, at least 20 people, many of them academics from various states and schools, said they were harassed, threatened, called racial slurs, and stalked by an individual who apparently also threatened to throw acid, chop off hands, murder, and mutilate some of them. Shantel Buggs, an assistant professor at Florida State University whose research centers on culture, race and racism, gender, and work inequity in academia, tweeted: "We know that being openly queer, not white, a woman, among many other social positions can set one up for excess surveillance, for questioning." Sociologist Victor Ray, an associate professor at the University of Iowa, said he was stalked by the same individual, who "has harassed me and my family—including death threats and lying about my background—for years," adding: "When I didn't respond to threats, they targeted my family," and "They target marginalized scholars because marginalization makes support more difficult and isolates their targets. I'm talking about this now because ignoring it hasn't worked. They are committed to violent harassment like it is their full-time job." Buggs put the conclusion plainly: "An accusation against someone the systems we all live within already disadvantages can be ruinous."
