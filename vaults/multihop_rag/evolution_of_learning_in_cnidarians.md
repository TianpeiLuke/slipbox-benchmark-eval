# The Evolution of Learning in Cnidarians

"Maybe learning does not need a very complex nervous system," says Jan Bielecki — maybe any creature with even a simple nervous system can learn, and if so, this new insight might help point to how learning evolved in animals.

The supporting ground comes from a second cnidarian. Gaëlle Botton-Amiot has studied sea anemones at the University of Fribourg in Switzerland, and her work hints that, like jellyfish, those sea creatures can learn; since sea anemones and jellyfish both belong to the group called cnidarians, "Showing that cnidarians that are so different [can both learn]," she says, "means that it's probably super widespread within them" — perhaps both inherited the ability from a common ancestor. The competing explanation is left open: it is also possible that the ability to learn arose in different animals independently, Nagayasu Nakanishi says, and finding out just how nerve cells in jellyfish or sea anemones accomplish learning could settle it, for instance by looking at what chemicals play a role in how different animals learn. "If there's a lot of similarities in the mechanism of how they learn, then that would be suggestive of common ancestry," he says. "But if they evolved independently, then you would perhaps expect very different mechanisms of learning."
