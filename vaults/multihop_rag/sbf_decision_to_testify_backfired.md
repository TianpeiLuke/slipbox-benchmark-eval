# Bankman-Fried's Decision To Testify Backfired

The Verge's trial reporter argued that Bankman-Fried's choice to take the stand was a net loss for his defense: like Theranos' Elizabeth Holmes before him, he got on the stand to speak directly to his state of mind in a way no other witness can, but telling your side of the story opens you to a great many questions you might not want to answer on cross-examination — and, having watched a lot of crosses, she called Sassoon's the nastiest she had ever seen.

The warrant is what the charges turn on. The case hinged on conspiracy and intentional deceit: just losing $8 billion is not a crime, though it is very embarrassing, but if Bankman-Fried lied to customers and lenders about what he was doing and how safe FTX was, that is a crime. Because state of mind was the contested issue, his own testimony became the evidence, and it did not hold: Sassoon had established the day before that he has a long history of dishonesty, and on October 31st, through a set of questions about what he did and did not do, she established that the story he told on direct examination was absurd. After a brief redirect by his own lawyers, resplendent with word salad, Bankman-Fried stepped down and the defense rested.

The direct examination had already supplied grounds. Beyond the two obvious lies the reporter counted him telling on the stand — about Alameda Research's name and about "allow_negative" — she was struck by how little he seemed to know about his own companies: apparently Singh, Wang, and Ellison were out there just doing whatever their little hearts desired, because Bankman-Fried was a CEO, but definitely not the kind that pays any attention to money at his crypto trading firm and futures exchange.
