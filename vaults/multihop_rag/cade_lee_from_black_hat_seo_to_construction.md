# Cade Lee, From Black Hat SEO To Construction

Cade Lee — the SEO who had warned the writer on the phone before the alligator party that SEO was "modern-day pirate shit" — now runs a construction company, having left the guideline-violating work behind. "That's what happened with SEO for me — it was becoming bullshit," he says. "I was not feeling good about customer meetings and about what I was saying, and I was like, 'I shouldn't be doing this.'"

Lee is among the SEOs who have spoken publicly, on panels, about violating Google's guidelines. He is also an ex-con who used to trade penny stocks and served time for securities fraud, and his entire body is covered in tattoos, from his scalp to his legs to his fingers. Meeting for beers in Denver at a bar outside an escape room, he said his probation officer in the economic crime offenders unit has never tried to stop him from violating Google's terms of service: "I was transparent about it, and they approved it." They even approved "some pretty questionable things, like in regards to adult sites," specifically involving what he had thought were ads for consensual sex workers. Later an activist reached out and showed him how certain websites he had built were supporting human trafficking; horrified, he shut the whole thing down, and even then helped the activist with her website.

His view of business is that any way you make money is essentially a con or a scam of some kind — a framing his probation officer hates. "The good con is like, you actually delivered, and you came through and made a profit," he said, illustrating it: "We're gonna take that old lady's money to build her a brand new patio. There's that scam, and then there's, 'Hey, let's take her deposit and run.'" Lee has spent a lot of time thinking about his place in the world — he was in the Marines, sold mortgages in the lead-up to the 2008 crash, went to prison, did SEO — and understands he needs to make money to survive but would like to do so in a way that is minimally harmful, so he prefers the good kind of con.

His scepticism about moral standards is aimed at expectations, not at Ray. While Ray herself said she has left behind the guideline-violating tactics of her past, choosing instead to do as Google asks and make high-quality websites that will "make the internet a better place," that kind of moral standard can be a lot to ask of someone running a business, and the reality is that most people running a company will break whatever rules they are able to get away with breaking. "They want this wholesome thing, and I can understand that. That'd be neat," Lee said. "But that's maybe in a world where we don't have money and greed and things, you know?"
