# Three Forces: Climate, Authoritarianism And The Internet

Holly Jean Buck frames the twenty-first century as three overlapping stories — climate change, authoritarianism, and the Internet — whose interaction is the real subject.

The first is the energy transition. Burning hills and glowing red skies, stone-dry riverbeds, expanses of brown water engulfing tiny human rooftops: this is the setting for the twenty-first century, and for many working on climate and energy the plot is about making the energy transition happen — completely transforming both energy and land use in order to avoid the most devastating impacts of climate change, or failing to.

The second is authoritarianism, and it is even more urgent. About four billion people, or 54 percent of the world, in ninety-five countries, live under tyranny in fully authoritarian or competitive authoritarian regimes. In this narration the century began with a wave of crushed democratic uprisings and continued with the election of authoritarian leaders around the world who began to dismantle democratic institutions; any illusion of the success of globalization, or of the century representing a break from the brutal twentieth, was stripped away with Russia's most recent invasion of Ukraine. The plot is less clear given the failure of democracy-building efforts in the twentieth century, leaving only a faintly discernable storyline of general resistance and rebuilding imperfect democracies.

The third is the penetration of the Internet into every sphere of daily, social, and political life. Despite turn-of-the-century talk about the Information Age, we are only beginning to conceptualize what this means; the current plot is about the centralization of discourse on a few corporate platforms, whose rise brings potential to network democratic uprisings as well as to buoy authoritarian leaders through post-truth memes and algorithms optimized to dish out anger and hatred. This is a more challenging story to narrate because the setting is everywhere: it unfolds in our bedrooms while we should be sleeping or waking up, filling the most quotidian moments of waiting in line in the grocery store or while in transit, and the characters are us, even more intimately than with climate change. While we are increasingly aware of the influence that shifting our media and social lives onto big tech platforms has on our democracy, less attention is devoted to the influence this has on our ability to respond to climate change.

Put the three together and the expected combination is optimistic: climate activists using the capabilities of the Internet to further both networked protest and energy democracy — in particular, advocacy for a version of "energy democracy" that looks like wind, water, and solar, decentralized systems, and local community control of energy.
