# Parent-Led Counterfeit Pill Awareness Campaigns

For the parents in the suit against Snap, litigation is only one iron in the fire: Perla Mendoza works to spread awareness of the risks of fentanyl to Spanish-speaking families, while Amy Neville travels to schools to share her son Alexander's story and hosts monthly online meetings that empower young people to do peer-to-peer youth outreach.

The lawsuit itself has a cost for those doing this work. Even for Neville, who is a plaintiff, going through the process of filing has been re-traumatizing, forcing her to relive horrifying moments again and again. "I don't know what is worse, getting out and talking about it, or not talking about it," she said.

What the outreach has taught her is where the information gap lies: many parents and young people alike do not know that counterfeit pills exist — something she desperately wishes she had known while her son was alive. The night he died, Alexander told his parents he had been taking Oxycontin he got online and that he wanted help; Neville and her husband immediately called a rehab facility and made plans to take him there the following day, but did not think to take the pills away. "We thought we were doing everything right," Neville said. "A little bit of information would have gone a long way in our household."
