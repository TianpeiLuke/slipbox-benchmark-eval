# Apple's Double Tap User Research And Naming

Apple built the double tap gesture on a large internal data collection and testing programme. "We collected data over hundreds of users, thousands of instances of these gestures internally to create models that represent all the different use cases we think [double tap] will be used in," explains David Clark, senior director of Apple Watch software engineering, noting that Apple cataloged all the potential user experiences that could use double tap and then solicited feedback from testers about how intuitive the feature was and whether it behaved as they expected. "There was an exhaustive list of screenshots, what those screens were, how a user could get to them, and then a detailed discussion through each and every one of those areas." The answers were iterated multiple times across different types of testers: some complete novices, others more familiar with the gesture, and others experts in human interaction engineering.

That feedback process is also why the gesture is no longer called double pinch, its name in Assistive Touch. "We did a lot of testing internally, and one of the things we learned is that when you tell someone to pinch, they hold the lower part of the gesture more than they release. Tap evokes a release," says Eric Charles, senior manager of Apple Watch Product Marketing, who explained that the simple name change led to testers performing the gesture more accurately. The Verge, reporting on October 25th, 2023, noted it would still be unofficially calling it the pinchy pinch.
