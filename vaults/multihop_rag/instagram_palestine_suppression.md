# Instagram Palestine Suppression

Through October 2023, as the humanitarian crisis in Gaza grew more dire, Instagram users, creators and Palestinian digital-rights groups reported that Meta was restricting, hiding, mislabelling or removing content about Palestine — including content that does not support Hamas and content that adheres to Instagram's Community Guidelines. The reports, gathered by TechCrunch and published 19 October 2023, span account restrictions, "sensitive" and "inappropriate" flags, hidden comments, collapsed Story reach, and disproportionate flagging of Arabic-language material.

Palestinian-American filmmaker Khitam Jabr provides an example that predates the current escalation. Earlier this year she posted a handful of Reels about her family's trip to the West Bank — short travel vlogs sharing snippets of Palestinian culture, from decadent meals to dancing at her niece's wedding. "I hadn't been in a decade, so it's just like, life abroad," she told TechCrunch. She received anti-Palestine comments and then could not respond to them or use her account for 24 hours, despite not posting anything about the occupation; she suspects Instagram restricted her because other users reported her Palestine travel videos. "Fast forward to now and the same shit's happening," she said.

The belief that Meta shadowbans — limits the visibility of — content about Palestine is not new. In an Instagram Story last year, supermodel and activist Bella Hadid, who is of Palestinian descent, alleged that Instagram "disabled" her from posting content on her Story "pretty much only when it is Palestine based," said she gets "immediately shadowbanned" when she posts about Palestine, and reported her Story views dropping by "almost 1 million." Leen Al Saadi, a Palestinian journalist based in Jordan and host of the podcast "Preserving Palestine," said she is used to "constantly being censored"; her Instagram account was restricted last year after she posted a trailer for the podcast's first episode, which discussed a documentary about Palestinian street art under occupation. "Palestinians are currently undergoing two wars," Al Saadi said. "The first is with their legal occupier. The second war is with the entire Western media landscape, and when I say the entire landscape, I mean social media."

Creators report that engagement on their posts tanked specifically after they publicly condemned Israel's response to the Hamas attack as excessively violent. Some, like Jabr, say they were restricted from posting or going live; others say Instagram flagged their content as "sensitive," limiting its reach, or flagged it as "inappropriate" and removed it even when it adhered to the Community Guidelines. A user based in Egypt, who asked to stay anonymous for fear of harassment, said her posts usually get around 300 views, but after she began posting pro-Palestine content following the Hamas attack earlier that month her Stories would get only one to two views; she found that posting a random picture first, then a pro-Palestine post, raised the views, and said the same happened to her friends. Another anonymous user based in the United Kingdom said his view count returned to normal when he posted a cat photo: "My stories went from 100s of views to zero or a handful. I've had to post intermittent non-Gaza content in order to 'release' my stories to be viewed again."

The pattern extends beyond Stories. The Arab Center for Social Media Advancement (7amleh), which documents Palestinian digital-rights violations and works directly with social media companies to appeal them, told TechCrunch it has received reports of Instagram inconsistently filtering comments containing the Palestinian flag emoji, with users reporting that such comments are flagged as "potentially offensive" and hidden. The organisation has also received countless reports of Meta flagging and restricting Arabic content, including content posted by news outlets: 7amleh advocacy manager Jalal Abukhater said it has documented multiple cases of journalists on Instagram reporting the same news in Arabic, Hebrew and English but only being flagged for the Arabic version. "It's literally journalistic content, but the same wording in Hebrew and English does not get restricted," Abukhater said. "As if there's better moderation for those languages, and more careless moderation for Arabic content." As the Intercept reported, Instagram and Facebook flagged images of the al-Ahli Hospital, claiming they violate Meta's Community Guidelines on nudity or sexual activity.

Enforcement also runs inconsistent in the other direction. Al Saadi tried to report a comment saying she should be "raped" and "burned alive," left in response to her comment on a CNN post about the conflict; in screenshots reviewed by TechCrunch, Instagram replied that it did not violate the platform's Community Guidelines against violence or dangerous organizations. "The restrictions on content, especially the content that relates to Palestine, is heavily politicized," Abukhater said. "It feeds into the bias against Palestinian narrative genuinely. It really takes the balance against Palestinians in a situation where there's a huge asymmetry of power." Similar claims circulated publicly, including an October 15, 2023 post alleging that the singer Kehlani was shadowbanned after becoming vocal and changing her profile picture to "i stand with Palestine," just as Bella Hadid had been.
