# HIV Treatment Access and Cost Barriers

The tools to end the AIDS epidemic by 2030 will work only if they reach the people who need them: "The science is a first step, but access is what will translate its true potential and value," Quarraisha Abdool Karim of CAPRISA told Live Science — and cost is the sharpest of those access barriers.

Uptake of PrEP pills rose more than tenfold from 2019 to 2022, but cabotegravir, described as a potential game changer, is not yet widely used because of its high cost of $3,700 per dose in the U.S. The drug's maker recently told the South African news outlet Bhekisisa that its nonprofit price will be around $30 a dose, and generic versions will be manufactured in coming years, but the current high price means HIV programs have yet to fold cabotegravir into their budgets, Dr. Raphael Landovitz said. "There's still not a drop of cabotegravir to be had anywhere in Africa," where some of the highest rates of new HIV infections occur, Abdool Karim said. Monitoring costs bite too: regardless of the type of ART a patient takes, their viral load should be checked regularly, and 21 million people underwent routine viral-load testing in 2022, up from 6 million in 2015 — but viral-load tests are expensive, so proxy measures such as a urine test Dr. Monica Gandhi and colleagues designed to track ART levels could serve the same purpose cheaply. An estimated 25% of people stop ART treatment, sometimes for six months or more, often because they face stigma, cannot get to the clinic or cannot afford treatment, and these individuals — many from vulnerable populations — represent a growing proportion of the AIDS cases seen in hospitals. "That is preventable and avoidable and really represents a failure on many levels," Dr. Anjali Sharma said. "But the failure isn't really the drug itself" — it is a failure of the support system that could keep people on ART.
