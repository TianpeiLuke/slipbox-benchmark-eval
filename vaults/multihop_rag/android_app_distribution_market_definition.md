# The Market Definition Fight In Epic v. Google

Market definition is the structural question on which the case turns: Epic claims Google has illegal monopolies in "Android app distribution" and "Android in-app payment processing," arguing that if you buy or develop for Android phones you are stuck paying the Google tax, while Google will say the real competition comes from Apple because people can choose to buy an iPhone instead.

The relation between definition and outcome is direct. If the court decides the relevant market is phones and app stores, not specifically Android ones, then Google is on the road to victory; if it accepts that Android apps are their own market, Epic will be in better shape. A third path exists — the court could pick a different market definition entirely, as the judge in the Apple case ended up doing. It is pretty tough to argue Google has a monopoly on app stores in general, which is why the narrower Android framing matters so much to Epic.

Two observations sit outside the formal model but bear on how the fee looks. Google charges up to ten times more per transaction than you pay with PayPal or a credit card, which does seem like a lot; and though Epic cannot argue it in court, Apple was not able to explicitly justify a 30 percent fee to that case's judge. On the other hand, it does kind of seem like Epic wants something for nothing — Epic has not stated what kind of fee would actually be reasonable for Google to charge in exchange for placement in its store, and CEO Tim Sweeney seemingly suggests there should not be a Google fee at all if a developer wants to use its own payment system.
