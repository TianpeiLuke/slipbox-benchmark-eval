# The University Response Gap To Faculty Online Abuse

The Verge's reporter argued that universities are failing faculty who are harassed online, and that the gap is not only procedural but attitudinal: deans and chairs are often unaware of how online abuse is affecting their faculty, and where campuses do respond they rely on policing and a punitive model rather than the well-being and mental health of the targets. "Deans and chairs are often unaware at all of how online abuse is actually affecting their faculty," Victoria O'Meara told her. "A lot of the attacks — while they may graduate to things like emails or even, in a horrible instance, people showing up on campus — they often start on social media."

The reporter grounded the claim partly in her own experience teaching journalism in academia for over a decade, watching threats to teachers grow worse. In 2015 she became concerned about a student who professed having a crush on her despite knowing she is married and who told a colleague about his sexual feelings toward her; she reported her concerns to the school, and his behavior escalated. He already had a criminal record including charges of sexual violence, talked about killing people and writing a book about it, and his reading responses turned dark, discussing rape and describing ideas of criminal activity along with a lack of empathy toward murder and disaster victims; frightened students reported his conduct as well. Her department, program directors, and humanities dean supported her, but when other officials got involved — the school police, the counseling center, and a campus dean — she was made to feel like an alarmist, and her own self-doubt crept in as she asked whether she had been too nice to him. "I don't find him scary," one campus official on the case said, and she was informed a plan would be made to help the individual graduate. She and her teaching assistant kept up instruction; though he was not allowed to attend class in person, she spent those weeks checking door locks and plotting how she might handle an attack on her class — an agonizing exercise for a journalist who has covered the immediate aftermath of college massacres such as the one that killed 32 people at Virginia Tech in 2007. Her assistant, a graduate student in the MFA program, said, "There were about two weeks where I couldn't sleep. I would have really bad nightmares. I was worried about the students, but also for myself and for you," and the experience helped her decide she did not want full-time university employment again. Neither of them realized at the time that, as employees, they could have reported the situation themselves to the Title IX office; instead, to avoid the student showing up unannounced, they moved their class of around 50 undergraduates to a secret, unlisted location — prevention spearheaded by program directors on the ground, not from the powers above.

The warrant is that the job now requires the exposure that produces the risk. Academics use Twitter, now known as X, and Facebook to collaborate and connect with researchers in their field, and some use TikTok or Instagram to promote their research; "It's not really possible anymore to be an active member of your research community without being on social media," O'Meara said, and accessibility online has only heightened scholars' visibility and vulnerability — at a time when funding agencies increasingly ask researchers to do more public engagement. Yet existing workplace harassment policies have not figured out how to prevent or protect faculty and staff from abuse, especially if the culprit is not someone under the authority of the institution. O'Meara's proposed remedies mark what she says institutions could do: more conversations among faculty about stalking and harassment, stronger digital protections for all employees, paying for services like DeleteMe that scrub the web of private information such as home addresses, and more training for staff on online abuse. "A lot of the people we talk to," she said, "have their hands in the air."
