# Fine / Penalty

In the corpus, a fine or penalty is the recurring instrument through which financial-market, antitrust, data-protection, and platform-governance enforcement is actually made to bite against a confirmed or admitted violation.

## Definition

Fine / Penalty is the label the corpus uses for the monetary sanctions regulators and prosecutors impose on companies and individuals after a confirmed or admitted rule violation — sized as a percentage of global annual turnover under the EU's DSA and CPC regimes, or as a fixed dollar amount tied to a settlement or guilty plea under U.S. agency actions such as the DOJ/Treasury/CFTC resolution with Binance and CZ.

## Context

The corpus shows two dominant penalty designs. First, EU digital regulation ties fines to a percentage of a company's *global annual turnover* rather than a fixed sum: the Digital Services Act (DSA) allows fines of up to 6% of global annual turnover for confirmed breaches, and a separate Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) enforcement track lets national consumer-protection authorities impose fines of up to 4% of global annual turnover for conduct pursued as a consumer-law violation. The Commission invoked the 6% DSA figure against X twice — once as a warning in October 2023 alongside its formal request for information into Israel-Hamas disinformation concerns (before any investigation had been opened), and again in December 2023 when it opened a formal DSA infringement proceeding against X over risk management, content moderation, and deceptive design. Second, U.S. enforcement in the corpus produces fixed-dollar or admission-linked penalties: Binance agreed to pay about $4.3 billion to resolve DOJ, Treasury (FinCEN), OFAC, and CFTC investigations into anti-money-laundering and sanctions violations, with CEO Changpeng Zhao separately recommended for a $150 million CFTC fine as part of his guilty plea. Outside the DSA/antitrust cluster, Norway's Datatilsynet used a *daily* fine (up to roughly $100,000 per day) to compel Meta's compliance with a consentless-ad-tracking ban, illustrating that penalties can be structured as recurring pressure rather than a one-time sum. Historical fines also inform present conduct: the corpus notes that Google only invested in improving search quality in Europe after being hit with a €5 billion EU antitrust fine in 2018, and the U.S. government cited that history in the DOJ's antitrust trial to argue Google lacks intrinsic incentive to improve without competitive or regulatory pressure.

## Key Characteristics

- **Turnover-percentage basis (EU model)** — DSA fines scale with a company's global annual turnover (up to 6%), and the parallel CPC consumer-protection track caps at 4% of the same base, so penalty exposure grows with company size rather than being fixed.
- **Fixed-sum or negotiated basis (US model)** — U.S. agencies in the corpus (DOJ, CFTC, SEC) reach specific dollar penalties, often as part of a guilty plea or settlement, as with Binance's $4.3B resolution and CZ's $150M recommended fine.
- **Recurring/daily penalties** — some regulators (Norway's Datatilsynet against Meta) impose a fine that accrues per day of continued non-compliance, functioning as ongoing coercive pressure rather than a settled sanction.
- **Penalty as leverage before conviction** — the EU explicitly warned X of possible fines and blocking *before* opening a formal investigation, using the threat of a penalty as a compliance-forcing signal.
- **Downstream behavioral effect** — a fine already levied (Google's 2018 EU antitrust fine) can be cited years later as evidence a company's improvements were penalty-driven rather than voluntary.

## Background (external)

The corpus uses "fine" and "penalty" as familiar terms without defining them itself. Per the Cornell Law School Legal Information Institute (LII), a fine is "a monetary penalty that is issued for failing to comply with one's duties," and it falls under the broader category of penalty, which can be imposed by a court or an administrative agency in either a criminal or a civil context (LII, *Fine*, accessed 2026-09-02). In criminal law a fine may be imposed alongside or instead of incarceration for violating a statute, while in civil law it commonly follows a breach of contract or comparable duty (LII, *Fine*, accessed 2026-09-02).
