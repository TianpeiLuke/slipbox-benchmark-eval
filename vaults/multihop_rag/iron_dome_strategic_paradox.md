# The Iron Dome's Strategic Paradox

The Iron Dome was built with the strategic vision of giving decision-makers breathing room — allowing them not to react immediately to rockets but to have the flexibility to react at the right time, in the right place, in a range of ways — yet its success was paradoxical: it was so successful that it not only supported the decision-making process but fundamentally changed it.

Prof. Eviatar Matania puts the paradox as follows: up until October 7, the system "served as a very successful plaster to threats to a sovereign state, because what legitimacy would a state leader have for going to war when no citizens were harmed?" Brigadier General Eran Ortal, until recently commander of The Dado Center for Interdisciplinary Military Studies, also believes technological measures such as the Iron Dome and the barrier influenced decision-making in Israel, and in his estimation helped the political echelon conduct a policy of military restraint towards the Gaza Strip, which in turn allowed Hamas to strengthen and gain offensive capabilities. But even after Iron Dome, Israel found it difficult to stand up against significant disruption of daily life on the home front: "Despite the clear threat posed by Gaza, the decision makers preferred to avoid a policy that would mean returning a large part of the country's population to the reality of air raids and disrupted daily routines. The bottom line: the Iron Dome did not change the essential strategic situation; as firepower from the Gaza Strip developed, so did the terrorist organizations' ability to control daily life in Israel's hinterland, hold it hostage, and restrain Israel from taking proactive measures against them." Ortal asks rhetorically why the relative success of the Iron Dome did not translate into restoring Israel's freedom of action against Hamas, and names three factors: first, like any operational system, Iron Dome is not perfect — the enemy studies it and develops its own challenges against it, and decision makers recognized that Israel is not hermetically protected; second, the enormous cost of deploying and operating batteries significantly impedes a policy based on continuous rounds of violence; and third, as long as rockets are intercepted in the skies over Israel and not over Gaza, the Israeli home front still experiences sirens, security rooms and shelters, and disrupted routine living — so "even the complete success of the Iron Dome left daily life on the home front in the hands of the terrorist organizations."
