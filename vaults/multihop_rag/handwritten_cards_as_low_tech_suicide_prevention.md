# Handwritten Cards As Deliberately Low-Tech Suicide Prevention

Schalley suspects that the "intentionality" and deliberately low-tech communication between social workers and teens is another reason the program has worked so well -- the medium, not just the message, is doing the work.

The card restores a relationship that used to end at discharge. "It recognizes the impact of a child or adolescent telling their story to a stranger in an emergency room during a time of crisis," she said. "Before, it was unlikely that the patient would hear from that social worker again." Now that same social worker will send personal notes to the patient for a year, "offering unconditional caring and support along the way of their mental health journey."

Because the cards are physical objects, they persist and are re-encountered: "The cards are tangible and many of the patients report hanging them up in their rooms, keeping them in their cars or backpacks, and reading them aloud to their friends or family," said Schalley.

That persistence is what connects the cards to the timescale of a crisis. The cards could also work to save lives, as "most kids who make a suicide attempt go from thought to action in 10 minutes or less," said Schalley. "Imagine the power of having a card to reread ... telling you that you are worthy from someone you met once," she said. "That's life-changing." She added, "In our high-tech world, written words from a caring adult stand out and make a difference."
