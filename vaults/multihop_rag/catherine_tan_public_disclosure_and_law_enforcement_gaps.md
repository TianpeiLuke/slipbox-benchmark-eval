# Catherine Tan's Public Disclosure And Law Enforcement Gaps

On November 9th, 2022, after S.'s letter reached Vassar and S. began publicly replying to her tweets with insults, Catherine Tan decided to stop letting a platform that had enabled her harassment get away with it and went public. "I have a stalker," she tweeted. "Recently, she contacted my employer in effort to get me fired. She is racist, and has begun contacting ASIAN ACADEMICS connected w/ me on this platform. So, if that is you, there's a chance she will send a similar letter to your employer. If this happens…please contact me immediately and I will put you in touch with my investigator." She added: "This has been going on for almost a year. I have NEVER met this harasser. I have NEVER engaged with this harasser. This is my first time publicly acknowledging this person." The tweet was shared 4,350 times and received more than 15,000 likes, and she received messages from other users and academics with stories about their own stalkers.

Tan refused to be scared off the platform. She still posts regularly — photos from dinners with friends or of her horseback riding, tweets about her syllabus, her outfits, her husband, class prepping and grading ("I did it. 50 papers graded across 13 days") — and told The Verge, "I'm not going to stop tweeting. I'm not going to adjust my life for this. I have my book coming out at the end of this year. I don't want this person to be in the back of my head. And most of the time, she isn't." Now and then she learns of the latest threat, post, or racial slur, and it upsets her again. Being a collateral victim of a cyberstalker who started out obsessed with someone else went from irritating to infuriating, and the racial taunting especially hits a nerve: "I'm Vietnamese. I was born in the US. Growing up in the '90s and early 2000s, at that time, American culture wasn't as welcoming." Some of S.'s comments claimed Tan "wants to be white"; Tan says she does not, but did struggle to embrace her Asian American identity in her younger years, and dealing with such comments repeatedly in adulthood was at times depleting. She also situated the harassment in academia's own dynamics: "When you publish or you become more public, you're always going to encounter haters, people who are ready to discredit you, people who are ready to undermine your legitimacy. That's true for everybody, but especially for scholars of color." The Supreme Court recently struck down race-based admissions on college campuses in cases that used Asian Americans as plaintiffs and pawns, arguing affirmative action discriminated specifically against them; yet some universities, like one where Tan previously worked, enroll a student body made up predominantly of people of color while the faculty is still overwhelmingly white, which can leave professors of color feeling, as Tan put it, that "we only belong here because we were given special admission, some sort of affirmative action." That makes harassment more intimidating to report: "It's definitely hard. And the people who will really ruin you won't be the stalkers. They're going to be your peers."

Law enforcement offered little. By winter, Sinha had submitted a complaint about S. to the FBI via an online portal and also tried calling; he did not hear back. Sinha, Umamaheswar, and Tan filed police reports in their local jurisdictions, but as Sinha explained, "The local police here will take a complaint from us, but they won't go over there to arrest." The Southern Connecticut State University Police Department had previously arrested S., who was later released, and the harassment did not stop. Tan asked the Poughkeepsie Police Department in New York whether it might reach out to the Hamden Police Department in Connecticut, where S. lives, for assistance, but Poughkeepsie declined. Sinha contacted the New York Division of Human Rights as a hate crime resource, and since he and Tan both live or work in New York they wanted help filing hate crime complaints with New York State Police. "We're all in different states," Tan said. "It's not easy to arrest somebody. Unless she tries to physically harm us, there's not much we can do." Every state has different laws, Sinha said: "It depends on where you are and where the perpetrator is." As a lawyer he believes there is no question S. is breaking various laws — defamation, aggravated harassment, disorderly conduct, stalking, hate crime motivations — some of which could rise to the level of felonies, yet even with all his knowledge, efforts, documentation, and research he has been stonewalled: "If I can't get some traction here, I don't know who could." It is extraordinary how indifferent police have been, he added: "It's a real struggle. You just need commitment from the law enforcement side, and you need a very clear and easy-to-prove violation." For Tan the open question is danger: "There's the uncertainty of what's gonna happen next because we know that she is confrontational. We know that she's not afraid to take action. It's not just a Twitter diary. Is she capable of violence?"
