# X's Moderation Capacity After The Musk Takeover

X's capacity to act on content reports is best understood as a set of substitutions made since Musk's takeover the previous year: in-house enforcement capacity was cut, legacy policies and verification were removed, a crowdsourced mechanism was installed as the main response to disinformation, and the EU's voluntary disinformation Code was exited. TechCrunch's reporting in October 2023 describes each element and how they connect.

The first element is headcount. X's ability to respond internally to reports of content problems was drastically pared back after Musk's takeover, which saw major layoffs, including in content moderation and human rights, as part of the new billionaire owner's bid to improve the platform's profitability. Musk also ripped up a bunch of legacy content moderation policies and slashed in-house enforcement teams. The relation here is direct: the profitability motive is what drives the reduction in enforcement capacity.

The second element is signal quality. Musk ended legacy account verification and turned the Blue Check system into a game of pay-to-play, one of a series of changes that, in TechCrunch's account, make it harder for users to locate quality information on X — which is why the publication says Musk has painted the largest target on X when it comes to DSA enforcement.

The third element is the replacement mechanism. X has been pushing Community Notes — which crowdsources additional context to add to questionable tweets — as its main response to disinformation risks, promoting this decentralised, crowdsourced alternative in place of the in-house teams it cut. TechCrunch's reading of that substitution is sharply critical: it essentially outsources responsibility for dealing with tricky issues like disinformation to users, in what looks suspiciously like another gambit to eke out extra engagement and farm confusion by applying a philosophy of extreme relativism, so that culture warriors are encouraged to keep forever fighting for their own "truth" in the comments.

The fourth element is the regulatory posture around all of this. Earlier in 2023 Musk pulled X out of the EU's Code of Practice on online disinformation — in TechCrunch's words, a very clear thumb of the nose to EU regulators. That move did not change the binding position, and Breton said so in response to X leaving the voluntary Code: "Obligations remain. You can run but you can't hide." The range over which the model holds is therefore bounded on both ends — it describes X from Musk's takeover the previous year through the October 2023 reporting, and it applies to voluntary commitments only; the statutory DSA duties survive the exit.
