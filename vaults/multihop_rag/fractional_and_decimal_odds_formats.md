# Fractional And Decimal Odds Formats

Fractional odds show two numbers separated by a slash (/) or a dash (-) — 5/1 or 5-1, say — where the first number, akin to a numerator, represents potential profit and the second, akin to a denominator, indicates the amount you need to stake. Put differently, the denominator tells you how much you must bet to earn a profit equivalent to the numerator. With 2/1 odds you would earn $2 for every $1 wagered; with 4/9 odds a bet of $9 would yield a $4 profit, so a winning $9 bet at 4/9 returns $13 in total — the $9 stake plus the $4 profit. These odds turn up occasionally, especially in contexts like horse racing, and are straightforward once you get the hang of them even if they initially look like a math problem.

Decimal odds are to betting what the metric system is to measurements: straightforward and user-friendly, with fewer intricacies than their American and fractional counterparts — though, just as the metric system is not universally embraced in the US, decimal odds can be overlooked there. To determine a bet's payout you multiply the decimal odds by the amount you are betting. If an NHL betting site has the Edmonton Oilers at 2.53 to beat the Detroit Red Wings, a $10 bet would yield a $25.30 return, comprising the $10 wager and $15.30 of profit.

The scope condition worth keeping is what the decimal calculation returns. It gives the total payout, not just earnings, so anyone tracking betting profits must subtract the original stake from the payout to get an accurate figure.
