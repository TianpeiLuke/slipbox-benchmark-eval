# Christmas Hope Amid War and Suffering

The argument answers a question it first puts sharply: how can there be "Joy To the World" when there is so much sorrow on Earth — how can there be thoughts of sleigh bells jingling, chestnuts roasting on an open fire or Jack Frost nipping at our noses when war rages in Israel and Ukraine, and deep hatred of God's chosen people continues seething in our nation's streets? The answer offered is that hate is strong, but God's love is stronger.

One anticipated objection is that the case is dated: to an age of AI and computer technology, a little story about a 19th century poet may seem anachronistic. The reply is twofold. First, human nature does not change, as bestselling author and nationally syndicated columnist Cal Thomas notes from his standpoint: "You can change hairstyles, clothes, styles, modes of transportation. You can even change politicians. But nothing really seems to change … A lot of that has to do with human nature." Second, Longfellow's poem still bears the truth because it was based on divine reality.

From that warrant the conclusions follow: wars do bring death — but God through Jesus Christ has conquered death; and because God is now "with us," there is hope even with a broken heart, and triumph even amid tragedy. Hence the closing instruction: this Christmas Day, strain your ears, push aside the din of the culture, the city, the war, the pain — and hear the bells.
