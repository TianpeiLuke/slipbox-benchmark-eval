# Editorial Judgment

## Definition

Editorial judgment is the term used in the Texas/Florida social-media Supreme Court case for social media companies' own claimed First Amendment right to decide what user expression does and does not appear on the platforms they own — a right belonging to the companies, not their users (doc_0256).

## Context

The term appears in the corpus as the crux of the First Amendment question the Supreme Court agreed to decide in the consolidated Texas and Florida cases over state laws (Texas's HB 20 and Florida's SB 7072) restricting how social media companies moderate content: "Do social media companies have a First Amendment protected right to exercise what you might call editorial judgment... in sorting out what expression does and does not appear on the platforms that they own?" (doc_0256).
NYU adjunct law professor Paul Barrett, quoted as the case's explainer, treats editorial judgment and content moderation as two names for the same activity — "what you might call editorial judgment — or what you also might call content moderation" — which is why the case is framed as testing whether content moderation itself counts as constitutionally protected speech (doc_0256).
Barrett is explicit that the underlying question is unsettled: "it's just not clear right now what precisely the First Amendment says about that question" (doc_0256).

## Key Characteristics

- **The platform's right, not the user's** — the case is counterintuitively about whether social media companies (not their users) hold a First Amendment right, since it is the companies' own curatorial decisions that the state laws restrict (doc_0256).
- **Equated with content moderation** — Barrett presents editorial judgment and content moderation as interchangeable labels for the same platform activity of "sorting out what expression does and does not appear" (doc_0256).
- **Framed as a subset of a larger question** — content moderation, and by extension editorial judgment, is described as "a really big and important subset" of what social media companies do, within the broader question of how the First Amendment applies to social media companies (doc_0256).
- **Legally unresolved** — as of the case being taken up, whether this judgment is constitutionally protected speech remained an open question rather than settled doctrine (doc_0256).
- **Consequential if rejected** — if courts find no such protected right, states could compel platforms to carry content (including misinformation and hateful content) and to give individualized explanations for removals, reshaping how moderation is practiced at scale (doc_0256).
