# Remedies Sought Against Google And The Ripple Effect On Big Tech Cases

The government's argument in US v. Google is that Google uses its platforms and its deals with partners to block out any competition in search or advertising, thereby hindering competitors from accessing the data they would need to improve their products. The grounds offered for this is a data asymmetry: the DOJ has said Google gets 16 times more data than Bing does every day, so exclusion from distribution compounds into a permanent quality gap for rivals.

The warrant joining the claim to the remedy is that if the deals are what starve rivals of data, then unwinding the deals and redistributing the data restores competition. On that logic, if Judge Amit Mehta rules against Google, the search giant may have to change its behavior and share its APIs with third-party developers; it may also be banned from making anticompetitive and exclusive deals with smartphone and computer manufacturers and wireless carriers; and Google might end up having to turn over all or most of the data it has collected to other search engines so they can improve their products and attract more users. Enforcers want to show that antitrust law remains relevant and that even though Google is basically the God of the internet, it is still no match for U.S. law.

The Google outcome could also have a ripple effect on other Big Tech cases. The FTC sued Amazon in September for using anticompetitive and unfair strategies to illegally maintain its monopoly power. The DOJ has been investigating Apple for years over the company's policy for third-party apps on its devices and whether it unfairly favors its own products. And there is an ongoing case between the FTC and Facebook, in which the agency calls on Facebook to sell Instagram and WhatsApp.
