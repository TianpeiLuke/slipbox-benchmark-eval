# John William Polidori And The Vampyre

Polygon's verdict on John William Polidori, author of The Vampyre (1819), is: not a vampire, but Byron — vampire all the way.

The grounds start with the book's origin. While the concept of vampires goes back at least as far as Balkan folktales of the middle ages or earlier, its history in the English-speaking world began in earnest with Dr. Polidori's novel at the turn of the 19th century, and the book has as high-class an origin as any bloodsucker book could ask for, born at a Swiss chalet in the same spooky story contest where Mary Shelley hatched the idea for Frankenstein. Another participant, Lord Byron, contributed the tale of an aristocratic Englishman who is secretly an ancient, unkillable force of evil, and being too lazy to write the story himself, passed the idea to his personal physician and party guest Polidori, who spun it into his novel three years later. The evidence for the verdict is the portrait: Polidori had always had an adorably puppy dog-ish devotion to Byron, that mercurial, temperamental, but undeniably sexy man of the late-night hours, so it is unsurprising that his vampiric main character Lord Ruthven takes on a certain erotic charge — cruelly sadistic, mockingly contemptuous of others, yet desperately desired by those around him for reasons even they cannot entirely explain. Ruthven is, in other words, a pathetically obvious stand-in for Byron himself, and Polidori's almost unwilling attraction to his questionable friend marks him out as a classic human thrall of an undead overlord rather than a vampire in his own right.
