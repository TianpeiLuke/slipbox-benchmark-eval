# Discount and MSRP

## Definition

In the corpus, a discount is the gap between a product's stated list price (which deal coverage also labels MSRP or the "usual"/"asking" price) and the lower sale price a retailer is currently charging, and deal-roundup articles cite that gap as the reason to call an offer a good buy.

## Context

The corpus's Cyber Monday and Black Friday deal-roundup articles repeatedly frame a sale item by naming its list price and then stating how far below that price the current offer sits, e.g. Engadget describes the third-generation AirPods as carrying "a list price of $169 but often sell for closer to $150, so this is $10 off the usual" once marked down to $140 — the discount is measured against the price the item "often" sells for, not only the official MSRP.
Outside deal journalism, the FTC's antitrust complaint against Amazon (doc_0002) treats discounting as a contested practice: Amazon's algorithm flagged seller Fred Ruckel's Ripple Rug as ineligible for the buy box after Chewy ran "a discount promotion" and dropped its price a few dollars below Amazon's $43 listing, illustrating how a single small discount elsewhere can trigger Amazon's price-parity enforcement.
Price-matching coverage (doc_0403) treats the same underlying concept — the difference between the listed price and a lower price found elsewhere — as the trigger for a retailer's price-match refund, though the article notes Amazon itself does not offer price matching.

## Key Characteristics

- **List price / MSRP as the reference point** — deal writers quote the item's list price (e.g. AirPods 3rd gen at $169, Apple Watch SE at $249) before stating the discounted price, so the discount is always expressed relative to that baseline.
- **"Usual" price can differ from MSRP** — Engadget distinguishes the official list price from what an item "often" or "regularly" sells for, and measures "off the usual" against the latter, meaning the advertised discount can be smaller than the gap from MSRP alone.
- **Discounts as leverage in enforcement** — in Amazon's marketplace, a competitor's discount (even just a few dollars) can cause Amazon's pricing algorithm to demote a seller's listing from the buy box, per the FTC's complaint.
- **Discounts as consumer protection** — price-matching and liberal return policies exist so a shopper who missed a discount at time of purchase can still recover the difference, though Amazon opts out of price matching specifically.
- **All-time-low framing** — deal coverage marks some discounted prices as new record lows (e.g. AirPods Max at $450), a stronger claim than an ordinary discount off list price.

## Background (external)

The corpus itself never formally defines "MSRP" or "discount"; it only uses the terms while reporting specific deals.
For general background, Wikipedia's "List price" article explains that a list price — also called MSRP, RRP, or SRP — is the price a manufacturer "notionally recommends that a retailer sell the product," and that such suggested prices are "sometimes unrealistically high, so the seller can appear to be offering a discount" (Wikipedia, "List price", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_price, accessed 2026-09-02).
