# Rising Game Development and Marketing Budgets

To keep up with live-service expectations, games as a whole are getting longer and more expensive, Polygon reported on 2023-10-19. It takes multiple studios to keep yearly live-service franchises like Call of Duty afloat, as noted in the CMA's report on Activision Blizzard; that same report says the development budget of a AAA game can range anywhere from $80 million to $380 million, and that seasonal updates in particular require anywhere from $50 million to $65 million to produce. For publishers, keeping up with the competition requires nearly as much money as making a big-budget game in the first place — for contrast, a modern Tomb Raider game had a budget between $75 million and $100 million, not counting marketing.

Marketing numbers are eye-popping too: a 2018 report noted that marketing budgets alone for some titles can equal 75% to 100% of what was spent on development, effectively doubling total production cost. The channels publishers pursue are varied, but Twitch streamers command a percentage, and recent years have seen a shift toward personality-based promotion, with more publishers eschewing traditional press at launch in favor of coverage they can control. Blevins by himself apparently commanded a $1 million payday for streaming Apex Legends, a game whose popularity can be attributed to its enormous Twitch-focused launch.

Who can actually compete in this environment? Giant publishers who make live-service games, mostly: all 10 of the most-watched games on Twitch in 2022 were live-service games with regular updates.

The cost goes beyond money. As Fortnite took off, developers at Epic Games said they experienced months of crunch requiring 70-to-100-hour weeks to produce that industry-shaping content, despite the publisher's deep pockets — an egregious work week that often still requires the cheap benefits of outsourcing and contractors who are routinely laid off. Things have not changed much since 2019 at some major studios: Diablo 4's developers say they also underwent crunch to ship the game, and the action role-playing game had Twitch integration from the jump alongside a detailed content roadmap. Polygon sees little incentive for any of this to change, even as big-budget games buckle under their own weight and studios close, because publishers know the longer a player is hooked, the more likely they are to spend money.
