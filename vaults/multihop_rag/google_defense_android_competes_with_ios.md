# Google's Defense: Android Competes With iOS

Google's defense denies the premise that Android app stores are a market of their own: "You cannot separate the quality of a phone from the quality of the apps in its app store, and that means Google and Apple compete against each other," began Google lead attorney Glenn Pomerantz. If Google can convince the jury of that, it could be a winning argument, because obviously Google does not have a monopoly on app stores or phones in general.

Pomerantz's commonsense line was that Google cannot possibly have a monopoly on Android app stores when "every single Samsung phone comes with two app stores right on the homescreen," and that the download charts reflect user preference rather than exclusion: "When they show these charts that show all these downloads from Play and not from the Galaxy Store, that's what the Samsung phone users are choosing. They're touching Play. Nothing's keeping them from touching the Galaxy Store; it's just what works for them."

Google's second plank is openness relative to Apple. Epic promised to "show that Google has closed off each and every other option" to the Play Store during this trial, but Google points to the simple fact that it allows alternate app channels at all — something Android rival iOS does not. Pomerantz boasted that over a billion people have gone through the process Epic portrays as needlessly onerous to get apps outside the Play Store; Google told The Verge over email that this refers to how many users have enabled the Android sideloading flow, not necessarily followed through with an install. "A billion people have done it after getting notified of the potential risks," Pomerantz said. "That's because Android users have a real choice."

Google also argues about consequences: if Epic wins, according to Google, it could make Android phones less safe by dismantling basic protections against sideloaded apps, and damage Android's ability to compete with the iPhone because it arguably cannot run a competitive app store by giving it away for free. Google's execution was uneven, though — it wound up spending much of its opening statement attempting to explain away its seemingly bad behavior as normal business practices and did not always succeed out of the gate.
