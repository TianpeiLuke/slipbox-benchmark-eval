# Middle East Crude And Shipping Chokepoint Disruption

The disruption model has two legs, oil supply and maritime trade routes, each with a stated magnitude. On the oil side, even before the Hamas attack Saudi Arabia and Russia had further cut oil production into an already tight supply market; Iran is a big incremental supplier contributing 3 per cent to global supply, so any escalation risks taking energy prices higher, and for security reasons Israel has already told Chevron to shut down its offshore energy platform. The historical reference point is the Arab oil embargo of 1973, when OPEC cut oil to supporters of Israel and accounted for 50 per cent of oil production; although non-OPEC production has since picked up and OPEC's contribution is lower at 32 per cent, that share is still sizeable enough to take oil prices higher. The quantified transmission link is that a 10 per cent rise in crude price will result in global inflation being 0.4 percentage points higher next year.

On the maritime side, the war may impact Suez Canal trade, a key route between Asia and Europe that accounts for 12 per cent of global trade by value and much larger by volume, plus 5 per cent of world oil trade. As things stood, trade may not yet be impacted through the Strait of Hormuz, which lies between Oman and Iran, but a spread of the crisis to the whole Middle East can risk trade through this route, which accounts for 20 per cent of global oil trade passage. If Iran is embroiled in the conflict, then trade with landlocked Central Asia is disrupted; Central Asia is a big agricultural producer and a source of raw materials and minerals like copper, cotton, uranium, gold and natural gas, and it also hosts a network of road, rail, and air routes linking China, Europe and South Asia. The relations therefore hold over an escalation range: the Hormuz and Central Asia legs are conditional on the crisis spreading beyond its October 2023 boundaries.
