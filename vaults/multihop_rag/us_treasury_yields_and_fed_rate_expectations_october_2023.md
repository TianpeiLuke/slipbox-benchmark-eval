# US Treasury Yields And Fed Rate Expectations In October 2023

The mechanism linking data to yields to stocks ran in both directions during October 2023. On 4 October, the yield on the 10-year Treasury, the centrepiece of the bond market, pulled back from its highest level since 2007, down to 4.73 per cent from 4.80 per cent late on the Tuesday, with shorter- and longer-term yields also easing to allow more oxygen for the stock market. Yields fell following reports indicating a slowing US economy, the first suggesting that hiring by employers outside the government was much weaker than expected; on Wall Street that is currently good news because a cooling job market could mean less upward pressure on inflation, which in turn could convince the Federal Reserve to take it easier on interest rates. After already raising its main interest rate to the highest level since 2001, the Fed had indicated it may keep its overnight rate higher next year than earlier expected, and Treasury yields had correspondingly snapped higher as traders accept a new normal of high rates for longer; the Fed pays particular attention to the job market because too much strength there could drive wages much higher, which it fears could keep inflation well above its target of 2 per cent.

By 12 October the same mechanism was running the other way. The stock market has largely been taking its cue from the bond market recently, and weak results announced in the afternoon for an auction of 30-year Treasury bonds sent yields of all kinds higher; higher yields can knock down prices for stocks and slow the economy by making borrowing more expensive. Yields had already been rising in the morning after a report showing consumer-level inflation a touch higher than economists expected, raising worries about the Fed keeping its main interest rate high for a long time as it tries to undercut inflation by knocking down investment prices and slowing the economy. The report also had encouraging nuggets: after ignoring prices for food and fuel, which Fed officials see as a better predictor of where inflation is heading, prices consumers paid were in line with expectations and continued to decelerate from earlier months. A second report said slightly fewer US workers than expected applied for unemployment benefits, indicating a job market with few lay-offs and therefore strength for the overall economy, though it could also add fuel to upward pressure on inflation. After the reports the 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.7 per cent from 4.56 per cent late Wednesday, and the two-year Treasury yield, which more closely tracks expectations for the Fed, climbed to 5.06 per cent from 4.99 per cent, with yields still down slightly for the week after regressing from more-than-decade highs following speeches that investors saw as hints the Fed may not raise its main overnight rate further. The big jump for the 10-year yield since the summer has pulled mortgage rates to their highest levels since the turn of the millennium, and such moves could slow the economy without requiring more Fed hikes; Seema Shah, chief global strategist at Principal Asset Management, said nothing in Thursday's inflation report should sway the Fed one way or the other on what it will do on 1 November, calling the data "reassuringly uneventful".
