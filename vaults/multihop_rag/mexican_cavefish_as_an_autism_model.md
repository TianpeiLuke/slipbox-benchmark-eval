# Mexican Cavefish as an Autism Model

For Masato Yoshizawa, geneticist and neuroscientist at the School of Life Sciences at The University of Hawai'i Mānoa, the Mexican cavefish is a fascinating model system: while these fish have unique adaptations for the harsh, lightless conditions of limestone caves in northern Mexico — such as the loss of their eyes — they also share several basic features with vertebrates, including the brain region involved in memory and emotion as well as many genes and the signalling pathways used to control them, which makes experimental results with them applicable to other species.

Humans and other vertebrates have more complexity in these systems, and experiments with the cavefish offer a way to strip that complexity away to investigate fundamental questions of biology. While working with cavefish, Yoshizawa noticed similarities between their behaviour and that of patients with autism: the fish avoid their peers and engage in repetitive behaviour, such as swimming in circles. "Many people first doubted that the fish have an autism-like state; I also doubted it at first," said Yoshizawa — but he soon found that even patterns of gene regulation resembled those of autistic patients. The boundary case is stated plainly: the cavefish are not an exact model for human autism, but this work shows how such experiments shine light on the biology and evolution of these behavioural conditions and offer new directions for researchers to follow.
