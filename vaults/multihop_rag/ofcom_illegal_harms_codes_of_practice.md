# Ofcom's Recommended Practices And Enforcement For Illegal Harms

Ofcom recommends that in-scope platforms implement policies like not allowing strangers to send direct messages to children, using hash matching to detect and remove CSAM, maintaining content and search moderation teams, and offering ways for users to report harmful content. Ofcom estimates that around 100,000 services may fall under the wide-ranging rules, though only the largest and highest-risk platforms will have to abide by the strictest requirements.

The premise is consistency rather than novelty. Large tech platforms already follow many of these practices, but Ofcom hopes to see them implemented more consistently. "We think they represent best practice of what's out there, but it's not necessarily applied across the board," Whitehead says. "Some firms are applying it sporadically but not necessarily systematically, and so we think there is a great benefit for a more wholesale, widespread adoption."

The regulations also cover how sites should deal with other illegal harms like content that encourages or assists suicide or serious self-harm, harassment, revenge porn and other sexual exploitation, and the supply of drugs and firearms. Search services should provide "crisis prevention information" when users enter suicide-related queries, for example, and when companies update their recommendation algorithms they should conduct risk assessments to check that they are not going to amplify illegal content.

Enforcement runs through complaint and penalty. If users suspect a site is not complying with the rules, Whitehead says there will be a route to complain directly to Ofcom. If a firm is found to be in breach, Ofcom can levy fines of up to £18 million (around $22 million) or 10 percent of worldwide turnover — whichever is higher — and offending sites can even be blocked in the UK.
