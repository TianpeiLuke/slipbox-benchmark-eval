# Tyler Mitchell's Case For Lightly Staged Black Leisure Photography

Speaking to The Age in New York before the NGV Triennial and published on 27 November 2023, Tyler Mitchell argues that photography should not merely record how things are but imagine new realities — his "lightly staged" scenes of Black families at leisure are a deliberate challenge to the "visual regimes" that photojournalism reinforces.

His grounds are both art-historical and political. "I think that I am part of a longer lineage of black artists who have considered how they respond to the Western historical art canon, but also it's 2023, and it's a new day and I think beyond just responding to that canon, we're trying to use the limited language of pictures to try and imagine what personal and unencumbered freedom looks like for young black men and women today," he says. In one of his favourite works, A Glint of Possibility, a teenage boy hangs over a river on a tyre swing studying his own reflection — pensive and peaceful at once, relatable to anyone who has ever been a child on a tyre swing on a hot summer day, yet speaking to the turmoil and tension inherent in the experience of Black American teenage boys, whose lives can be destroyed or irrevocably altered even while doing unremarkable things such as walking home, playing in the park or talking to their crush. His work exists in the tension between what is possible and how things are; he describes it as "a consideration of joy, perseverance and self-determination against the backdrop of history". The Triennial's theme, "Magic, Matter, Memory", makes his pieces a logical inclusion: "A lot of my work meditates on being between a heightened reality, or potentially even a magical reality and an actual reality."

The warrant joining method to claim is his account of staging: "I'm creating what I call lightly staged moments… I'm setting up scenarios where these families are enjoying real moments of leisure in outdoor spaces, usually open pastures and fields, and those moments are meant to stand in for a meditation on what it means to enjoy leisurely outdoor nature as a black person." The context he names is the pandemic and the rise of the Black Lives Matter movement in the summer of 2020, when Black artists and activists sought to reframe a public debate about Black life and force new perspectives of peace, luxury and safety into the picture. He wants to challenge assumptions about certain people or cultures that are reinforced over and over: "I think that [photojournalism] is a well from which we all culturally – consciously or unconsciously – construct ideas of how people live around the world. My work is really about responding to, and questioning, and challenging those ideas." As a 20-year-old film student at New York University he became enamoured with still photography after signing up to a documentary photography course in Havana, Cuba, drawn to its accessibility after being frustrated by the time and money films required.
