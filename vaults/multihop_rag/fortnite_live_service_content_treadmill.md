# Fortnite's Live-Service Content Treadmill

Polygon's model, published 2023-10-19, is that around 2018 Twitch and its personalities turned Fortnite into a juggernaut so powerful that much of the gaming industry started molding itself in its image — and that what kept Fortnite from being a mere fad was not its approachable aesthetic and surprising gameplay but its model: where content expansions and adjustments previously took months or years and often cost money, Fortnite set a more cutthroat standard of free weekly content updates.

The conditions that made this possible were unique to Epic. It could swing ceaseless delivery because the bulk of its profits at the time came from its engine, which many games — including Fortnite's competitors — license; those deep pockets made Fortnite possible in the first place, since the bubbly shooter was initially a flop before Epic perfected the formula. The cadence also let Epic quickly adapt Fortnite to whatever was trending or novel, whether Among Us or Splatoon, and much like Roblox it became a game that could give you anything — and if Epic did not emulate it, the players would. "You can't replicate Fortnite's growth from, like, the [content creator] standpoint and also definitely from the developer standpoint," says a Twitch streamer turned game developer who requested anonymity because he is not authorized to discuss the popular AAA live-service shooter he works on.

Those conditions were immaterial to publishers who could only imagine profits: suddenly "live service" was everywhere, and games like Overwatch, which could not achieve a similar pace, were posed as disappointments by content creators.

The limit of the model is the audience it assumes. Developers say getting half their players to actually finish games is unusual and worth celebrating, because most people will never finish a game they start, and 22% of purchased games on Steam are unplayed outright — affected by factors like sales or Game Pass, but still telling. Per Statista, a reported 25% of players between 18 and 34 enjoy games between three and six hours a week, 28% play two hours or less, and 19% play between 13 to 24 hours or more; as fans get older they apparently spend less time in games, and in the most engaged age group half of all players only log a couple of hours or so per week in their favorite games.
