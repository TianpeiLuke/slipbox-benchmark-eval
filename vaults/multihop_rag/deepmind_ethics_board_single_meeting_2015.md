# DeepMind's Ethics Board And Its Single Meeting In 2015

When Musk invested in DeepMind he broke his own informal rule that he would not invest in any company he didn't run himself, and the downsides were already apparent when, only a month or so after his birthday spat with Larry Page, he again found himself face to face with his former friend. The occasion was the first meeting of DeepMind's ethics board on 14 August 2015, a board set up at the insistence of the startup's founders to ensure their technology did no harm after the sale. The members convened in a conference room just outside Musk's office at SpaceX, but that is where Musk's control ended: when Google bought DeepMind it bought the whole thing, and Musk was out, financially ahead but unhappy. Three Google executives now firmly in control of DeepMind were there, Page, Sergey Brin, a Google co-founder and Tesla investor, and Eric Schmidt, Google's chair, along with other attendees including Reid Hoffman, another PayPal founder, and Toby Ord, an Australian philosopher studying "existential risk".

The DeepMind founders reported that they were pushing ahead with their work but were aware the technology carried serious risks. Mustafa Suleyman gave a presentation called "The Pitchforkers Are Coming", telling the board that AI could lead to an explosion in disinformation and fretting that as the technology replaced countless jobs in coming years the public would accuse Google of stealing their livelihoods, so Google would need to share its wealth with the millions who could no longer find work and provide a "universal basic income". Musk agreed, but it was pretty clear the Google guests were not prepared to embark on a redistribution of their wealth: Schmidt said he thought the worries were completely overblown, and Page, in his usual whisper, agreed, arguing AI would create more jobs than it took away. Eight months later DeepMind had a breakthrough that stunned the AI community and the world, when a DeepMind machine called AlphaGo beat one of the world's best players at the ancient game of Go, a match streamed over the internet and watched by 200 million people across the globe, though most researchers had assumed AI needed another 10 years to muster the ingenuity to do that; the South Korean professional Lee Se-dol played Go against AlphaGo in March 2016.
