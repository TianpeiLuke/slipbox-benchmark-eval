# AI Startup Defensibility

Startup-focused AI work can be made defensible, but only at the application layer and only on conditions: to prove defensible in the rising climate of AI integration, industry-specific startups that tune generative AI models for a specific niche must prioritize collecting proprietary data, integrating a sophisticated application layer and assuring output accuracy. The claim is made against a market where costs are only one open question — TechCrunch's 13 October 2023 survey of venture capitalists also asked how startups should approach building tools for AI technologies, how defensible startup-focused AI work will prove, and how upstart tech companies should charge for AI-powered tooling.

The grounds are a four-part assessment framework for the defensibility of application layers of AI companies. First, the application must address a real enterprise pain point prioritized by executives. Second, to provide tangible benefits and long-term differentiation, the application should be composed of cutting-edge models that fit the specific and unique needs of the software — it is not enough to simply plug into OpenAI; rather, applications should choose their models intentionally while balancing cost, compute, and performance. Third, the application is only as sophisticated as the data that it is fed: proprietary data is necessary for specific and relevant insights and to ensure others cannot replicate the final product, and to this end, in-house middle-layer capabilities provide a competitive edge while harnessing the power of foundational models. Fourth, due to the inevitable margin of error of generative AI, the niche market must tolerate imprecision, which is inherently found in subjective and ambiguous content, like sales or marketing.

The warrant is that the layer a startup occupies determines whether these conditions can be met at all. Most of the opportunity lies in the application layer, and within that layer the best applications will harness their in-house expertise to build specialized middle-layer tooling and blend them with the appropriate foundational models — "vertically integrated" or "full-stack" applications. For startups this approach means a shorter time-to-market: without negotiating or integrating with external entities, startups can innovate, iterate and deploy solutions at an accelerated pace, and this speed and agility can often be the differentiating factor in capturing market share or meeting a critical market need before competitors. The middle layer alone is a weaker position, since it acts as a conduit connecting the foundational aspects of AI with the refined specialized application layer — model fine-tuning, prompt engineering and agile model orchestration — where the emergence of foundation model providers expanding into middle-layer tools heightens commoditization risks, established market leaders venturing into the space further intensify competition, and despite a surge in startups within this domain, clear winners still need to be discovered.

The incumbent-encroachment objection is answered by historical pattern rather than by denial. LLM observability falls within the middle-layer category, acting as a catalyst for specialized business applications to use foundational models, and incumbents like Datadog, New Relic and Splunk have all produced LLM observability tools and do appear to be putting a lot of R&D dollars behind this, which may curtail the market area in the short term. However, as seen before with the inceptions of the internet and cloud computing, incumbents tend to innovate until innovation becomes stagnant; with AI becoming a household name that finds use cases in every vertical, startups have the chance to come in with innovative solutions that disrupt and reimagine the work of incumbents. It is still too early to say with certainty who the winners will be, as every day reveals new gaps in existing AI frameworks, and therein lie major opportunities for startups. A companion TechCrunch survey of 10 investors on the future of AI and what they believe might be the next big thing touched on the same terrain — where startups still stand a chance, where oligopoly dynamics and first-mover advantages are shaping up, and the value of proprietary data.
