# The Republican Holdouts' Case Against Jim Jordan

Jim Jordan's third attempt at the gavel essentially collapsed in large part because more centrist Republicans revolted over the nominee and the hardball tactics being used to win their votes — an attack not on his politics alone but on the legitimacy of the pressure campaign. They had been bombarded with harassing phone calls and even reported death threats. "One thing I cannot stomach or support is a bully," said a statement from Rep. Mariannette Miller-Meeks, R-Iowa, who voted against Jordan on the second ballot and said she received "credible death threats."

The second premise they rejected is that Jordan was suited to the office at all: many view Jordan, a founding member of the hard-right House Freedom Caucus, as too extreme for a central seat of U.S. power, second in line to the presidency. The 20 Republicans who rejected his nomination on the first day were a surprisingly large and politically diverse group, many resenting the hardball tactics seeking to enforce support and viewing the Ohio congressman as too extreme.

Flexing their independence, the holdouts are a mix of pragmatists — ranging from seasoned legislators and committee chairs worried about governing, to newer lawmakers from districts where voters back home prefer President Joe Biden to Trump. Some Republicans resent being pressured by Jordan's allies and say they are being threatened with primary opponents if they don't support him as speaker; others are simply upset at the way the whole process has dragged out. They cast their ballots for Kevin McCarthy, Majority Leader Steve Scalise — who had been the party's first nominee to replace McCarthy — and others, one vote even going to the retired John Boehner.
