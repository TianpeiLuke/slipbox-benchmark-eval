# The Construction Of The Gaza Border Barrier

The fence that until October 7 separated Israel and the Gaza Strip was completed in December 2021 — 60 years after construction of the Berlin Wall began — and although it was intended to protect against a major security threat, in practice it was much leaner than that: almost unmanned by soldiers, relying mainly on sensors above and below ground, surveillance cameras, and vehicle patrols, with no surrounding minefield.

Its design answered a specific earlier threat. After Operation Protective Edge in 2014 revealed that Hamas forces could penetrate the Gaza border settlements at several points via underground tunnels, it was decided in 2016 to build a defense line focused on that threat — "the anti-tunnel barrier" — to replace the existing fence. The upper section was based mainly on a fence called the "sand clock," which had only proved itself in stopping illegal immigrants on the Israel-Egypt border, the same fence then-US President Donald Trump enthused over and wanted to duplicate on the US southern border. The cost of the barriers was NIS 3.5 billion, mostly for the below-ground section. It was presented as a high-tech project and a great technological achievement that would be an additional layer of protection from the Gazan threat alongside the Iron Dome; the Ministry of Defense boasted that "the amount of concrete invested in it could pave a road from Israel to Bulgaria, and the amount of iron and steel could equal an iron bar from here to Australia." At the time the Ministry of Defense claimed it would be integrated into the "Smart and Lethal Border" project being tested along the northern border of the Gaza Strip, which it said included mobile robots and military drones for carrying out defense missions without endangering soldiers' lives.
