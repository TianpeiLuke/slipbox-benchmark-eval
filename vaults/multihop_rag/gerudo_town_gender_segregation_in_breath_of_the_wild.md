# Gerudo Town's Gender Segregation in Breath of the Wild

Gerudo Town is a gendered and raced space unlike the other settlements on the periphery of Hyrule, as Polygon described on 2023-10-19: alongside the bird people, fish people, and rock people, the Gerudo are a vaguely Arabic or North African-coded, all-female, matriarchal society whose culture is a site of friction and humor in both games. The Gerudo are at once obsessed with and scornful of men, found on pilgrimages to find their one true love — a monogamous myth perpetuated by their own teachings — or else behind the walls of their single-gender desert city.

The rule is that women of all races, and the apparently genderless Gorons, are welcome in Gerudo Town while men are prevented from entering by armed guard. In Breath of the Wild many still try their luck, with several characters' whole personalities being a one-note joke about how it is funny to watch men chase after women who wish to be left alone.

But the discriminating feature Polygon stresses is that the Gerudo do not themselves police gender. Vilia, who can be read as a trans woman, is said to be welcomed in the city, and Link is welcomed in when he dons their attire, seeming comfortable if bashful when he first puts on the clothes. When Link reveals himself at the city's health-replenishing spa, the innkeeper Romah implores him to relax — "We're all vai here," she assures, using the fictional language's word for "woman" — and many more compliment Link's appearance in strictly feminine terms. Since both the Gerudo chieftain Riju and armor shopkeep Greta address Link as if he is in disguise, one can infer that many perhaps see Link as a non-conforming person moving through the city.

A boundary case reinforces the point: the presence of a black-market armor shop denies gender essentialist notions like the idea that Vilia is a man dressed as a woman. In Breath of the Wild, the shop's covert dealings of Gerudo armor for men suggest there must be men in the all-female race, leaving one to assume trans men may be born of the Gerudo and, while not sanctioned in the city, are integrated into their material culture. Tears of the Kingdom, by contrast, does away with almost all of the queer subtext brimming just beneath Breath of the Wild's surface.
