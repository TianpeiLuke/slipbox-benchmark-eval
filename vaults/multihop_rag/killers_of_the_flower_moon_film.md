# Killers of the Flower Moon (Martin Scorsese)

Killers of the Flower Moon is Martin Scorsese's 206-minute adaptation of David Grann's 2017 account of the reign of terror that plagued the Osage in the 1920s, starring Leonardo DiCaprio, Lily Gladstone, and Robert De Niro. Polygon ranked it 4 on its best movies of 2023 list of 2023-10-07 and described it as an act of lamentation — a cinematic wail against one of the many foundational horrors of the United States, a crime still in living memory whose ill-gotten gains still prop up any claim to prosperity the nation has today.

Ernest Burkhart loves money; it is one of the first things he says when he arrives in Oklahoma, fresh off the Great War's killing fields and into the arms of his manipulative uncle William Hale, with whom he will help turn this land, home of the Osage Nation, into another killing field. Scorsese's reverent, methodical film pares back the sprawling story to focus on Burkhart, played by DiCaprio, Hale, played by De Niro, and Mollie Kyle, played by Lily Gladstone, the Osage woman who would marry Burkhart and pay for it with the lives of her entire family. Through the Burkhart marriage, critic JR writes, Scorsese renders the crimes against the Osage in miniature — three and a half hours of rumination on the theft and bloodshed that watered the prosperity of white America, endured in stifled silence for a century.

Polygon's 2023-10-26 piece on Scorsese's late career places the film among his work: it is perhaps most closely related to The Aviator as a film that appears to be a recognizable, industry-friendly form of awards-season prestige, and it is a return to Scorsese's core themes and characters — another cast of avarice-fueled, violent white American dumbasses gaining ill-gotten wealth, failing up, consolidating power, and shading the country more evil. It adapts a major literary work of historical true crime from a New Yorker writer about an obscure but monstrous historical incident that is a cosmic truth in miniature, and on screen it is Scorsese's first true Western, filled with gorgeous vistas and Oklahoma horizons that would bring a tear to the eye of John Ford, one of Scorsese's many idols.

The same piece stresses that the film is much stranger than it sounds on paper: Scorsese's take largely operates outside the text, centering the story on a peripheral character we know little about from Grann's book, and it also has funny elements, incorporating The Departed and The Wolf of Wall Street's nervy energy, with long and zany improvisational exchanges on grim subject matter, sudden wild swings in tone, and one major decision that should spark more debate and differences of opinion than any narrative device Scorsese has ever employed.
