# Scorsese's Late Career Refutes Tarantino's Claim That Directors Do Not Get Better

Polygon's 2023-10-26 piece attacks the premise behind Quentin Tarantino's 2021 defense of his intention to make "just" 10 movies and retire: "I know film history, and from here on in, directors do not get better." The counter is that this view of both artistry and film is squarely at odds with Martin Scorsese's historic body of work.

The specific premise under attack is the claim of inevitable late-career decline. Scorsese had already made his 10th feature-length narrative film by 1986; he is now 80 years old, and his 27th, Killers of the Flower Moon, is a strong argument that directors are still capable of groundbreaking greatness late in life — and, in Scorsese's case, of continued exhilarating experimentation and discovery.

Polygon presses the point further: since the year 2000, Scorsese has curated a 23-year career within a career, and the quality and variety of his films have come not in spite of his age and experience but because of it. His movies have never been more relevant than in the past two decades, and with each subsequent release, as his career winds down, critical appreciation for him grows — Scorsese worship being the one remaining form of gerontocracy America is perfectly content with, and with good reason.

The mechanism the piece identifies is autonomy. This century Scorsese has zigged and zagged as always, but because he is following his own varied interests rather than moving from compromise to compromise as he once had to in order to keep working: an artist who spent the first 30 years of his career desperate for autonomy finally got it, and now takes full advantage — telling difficult stories, setting his own mega-budgets, and demanding every minute of run time he feels each picture demands. He has used that autonomy to revisit his work and revise the record, creating great new films and adding crucial addendums to past work while blazing new trails and adding layers to his style. Remarkably, the control that eluded him when he was younger has made his films more consistently collaborative and creative, more fluid and alive than he was often allowed to be before the 2000s, with the earned confidence of an artist who knows he will find something great in post. Polygon concludes that this should give Tarantino cause to reconsider, alongside any artist or critic who thinks the creative spark has an expiration date.
