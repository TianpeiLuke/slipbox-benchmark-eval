# Vascular Plants, Weathering and the Paleozoic Oxygen Rise

The emergence of plants with roots, stems and leaves midway through the Paleozoic, around 420 million years ago, drove two coupled changes: it accelerated chemical weathering, which may have led to a slow, steady decrease in atmospheric CO2 through the late Paleozoic, and it created a new source of buriable organic matter, which pushed atmospheric oxygen concentrations as high as 35%, almost twice the current concentration of 21%.

The weathering link works because these plants produce acids that dissolve rock, helping cycle water from the soil back into the atmosphere. The oxygen link works because the spread of vascular plants supplied organic matter that could be buried on land or carried off in rivers for ocean burial, and increased burial of organic matter causes rising atmospheric oxygen levels: that organic matter is the product of photosynthesis, which splits up oxygen and carbon atoms, and the carbon, once buried, is no longer available to cannibalize the liberated oxygen.
