# Whose First Amendment Rights Are At Stake: Editorial Judgment

The case revolves around First Amendment rights — but, counterintuitively, it is the rights of the social media companies that are in question, not the rights of their users. The claim being litigated is that platforms themselves hold a constitutionally protected right to exercise editorial judgment over what appears on the services they own.

The grounds are how the question is actually posed. As Barrett framed it: "The question here is: Do social media companies have a First Amendment protected right to exercise what you might call editorial judgment?" — and, in the fuller version, "what you might call editorial judgment — or what you also might call content moderation — in sorting out what expression does and does not appear on the platforms that they own?"

The warrant joining the two halves is the equivalence Barrett draws between editorial judgment and content moderation. "These cases are about the First Amendment and how the First Amendment applies to social media companies. And then more specifically, what the First Amendment has to say about content moderation, which is obviously a subset, although a really big and important subset of what social media companies do," Barrett said. If moderation just is editorial judgment, then a platform's decisions about what to carry are the platform's own expression, and the users' speech rights are not what the case turns on.

The scope condition is that this remains an open question rather than a settled doctrine. In Barrett's words, "it's just not clear right now what precisely the First Amendment says about that question."
