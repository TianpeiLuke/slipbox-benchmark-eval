# Carols Express Both Joy and Sorrow

The claim is that it is only in carols that we truly understand Christmas — of the joy at the birth of Jesus, and also the somberness of what Jesus was born to do: to die. Popular Christmas songs have their place, including wonderful tunes like "Silver Bells," "White Christmas" and "The Christmas Song (Chestnuts Roasting On An Open Fire)", and even songs about Rudolph, Frosty the snowman and walking in a winter wonderland warm our hearts during the holidays — but they are not what carries the double meaning.

The supporting case is textual. "What Child Is This" brings joy and sorrow poignantly together as it sees the beauty and innocence of a tiny babe asleep on His mother's lap — "What child is this who lays to rest on Mary's lap is sleeping" — yet still sings of the future sorrow: "Nails, spears, shall pierce Him through, the cross be born for me for you. Hail, hail the Word made flesh, the babe, the Son of Mary." That Jesus was born to die for our sins, to breach the chasm between God and humanity, is the joy and sorrow of this season: there is joy because He left His glory aside to live among us, abide with us, live the life we couldn't live; and there is sorrow because our sin, the curse, was and is so great that God Himself took the wrath of his own judgment — and died the death we deserve.

The warrant joining the textual reading to the claim is that this double register is what lets carols account for a broken world: carols make sense of why there are still wars and suffering at Christmas, expressing that the world isn't the way it should be while giving us tremendous hope in what it could be. "O come O come Emanuel" is a mournful tune, almost funereal, yet its refrain pleads, "Rejoice, Rejoice!" — because "Emanuel, shall come to thee, oh Israel." Emanuel means "God with us", which means that because of Christmas a new, category-busting power has come into the world, one not available for the tens of thousands of years of human existence before; grace and a love that surpasses all understanding is what became available because of Christmas.
