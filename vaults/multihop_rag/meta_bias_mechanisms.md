# Meta Bias Mechanisms

Meta's disproportionate suppression of Palestinian and Arabic content is produced by a stack of interacting mechanisms rather than any single fault: thin Arabic language resources, categorical policies that cannot handle nuance, a blanket prohibition on support for terrorist organizations, government partnerships that shape policy decisions, automated enforcement backed by "minimally trained humans," and an allocation of moderation resources that tracks regulatory pressure rather than need. TechCrunch set out this account on 19 October 2023, drawing chiefly on the Electronic Frontier Foundation.

The linguistic layer comes first. Meta struggles to navigate the cultural and linguistic nuances of Arabic, a language with over 25 dialects, and has been criticized for neglecting to adequately diversify its language resources. On top of that, the company's black-and-white policies often preclude it from effectively moderating any nuanced topic — content that discusses violence without condoning it is the example given.

The policy layer compounds it. Meta forbids support of terrorist organizations, as most U.S.-based social media companies do, but struggles to moderate content around that prohibition, from user discourse to journalistic updates. Combined with the company's partnership with Israel to monitor posts that incite violence, the rule complicates matters for Palestinians living under Hamas' governance: as EFF points out, something as simple as Hamas' flag in the background of an image can result in a strike. Advocacy groups have separately raised concerns that Meta's partnerships with government agencies, such as the Israeli Cyber Unit, politically influence the platform's policy decisions.

The enforcement layer supplies the inconsistency. Jillian York, EFF's director for international freedom of expression, blames automation and decisions made by "minimally trained humans," and says Meta's zero tolerance policy and imprecise enforcement often suppress content from or about conflict zones. The range of the effect is wider than Palestine: the site's moderation issues have negatively affected multiple non-English speaking regions, including Libya, Syria and Ukraine. "These rules can prevent people from sharing documentation of human rights violations, documentation of war crimes, even just news about what's happening on the ground," York said. "And so I think that is what is the most problematic right now about that particular rule, and the way that it's enforced."

The resourcing layer explains why the effect is unevenly distributed across languages. York says Meta has made more contact with regional Palestinian organizations since 2021 but has been slow to implement recommendations from EFF and other advocacy groups, and that it is "very clear" Meta is not putting the same resources behind Arabic and other non-English languages compared with the attention it gives countries that exert the most regulatory pressure. Moderation of English and other European languages tends to be more comprehensive, for example, because the EU enforces the Digital Services Act.

Meta contests the picture. In its response to the report, human rights director Miranda Sissons said the company was "assessing the feasibility" of reviewing Arabic content by dialect and that it has "large and diverse teams" who understand "local cultural context across the region," including in Palestine; responding to the escalating violence earlier that month, Meta said it had established a "special operations center" staffed with fluent Hebrew and Arabic speakers to monitor and respond to violating content. Despite those apparent efforts, Arabic is still disproportionately flagged as violating — as in the case of journalists reporting news in multiple languages.
