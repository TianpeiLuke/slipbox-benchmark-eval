# Terms of Service

## Definition

Terms of Service (also called T&Cs or "terms and conditions") is the corpus's label for a platform's or vendor's own rulebook, as distinct from external regulation such as the DSA, which compels how that rulebook must be written and enforced (doc_0024).

The corpus applies the same label to two different domains: platform content-moderation rules — paired with, but not identical to, a separately named "Community Guidelines" document in Discord's warning system (doc_0505) — and the commercial fine print that online sportsbooks attach to sign-up and bonus offers (doc_0062, doc_0072).

## Context

In the corpus the term recurs wherever a platform's own rulebook — rather than a law — is the thing being disputed or regulated. The EU's DSA obliges platforms to "clearly communicate their T&Cs to users and be able to demonstrate proper enforcement," which is the legal hook the Commission uses against X over illegal-content handling (doc_0024). Breton's letter to X separately flags a change to X's "public interest policy" — the sub-rule governing when rule-breaking posts stay up for newsworthiness — as having left users "uncertain" about how X applies its own rules, illustrating that ToS content itself, not just its existence, is subject to DSA transparency requirements (doc_0025). On Meta's platforms, the operative rulebook is branded "Community Guidelines": Instagram removed and restricted Palestine-related posts that users say complied with those Guidelines, and Meta's own newsroom statements point back to the Guidelines and an appeals process as the mechanism for contesting enforcement (doc_0106). Snapchat's Community Guidelines are described as prohibiting pornographic, terrorism-advocating, or gratuitously violent content — a similarly-scoped rulebook to Meta's (doc_0124). Discord's new warning system links violators directly to its "Terms of Service, Community Guidelines and appeal mechanism," pairing the rulebook with a visible enforcement record (doc_0505). Outside content moderation, "terms and conditions" also names the commercial fine print — wagering requirements, banking limits, expiry windows — that online sportsbooks attach to sign-up and bonus offers, which users must accept before registering (doc_0062, doc_0072).

## Key Characteristics

- **Platform-authored, not statutory** — the rules originate with the service, distinguishing ToS from the regulations (e.g. the DSA) that constrain how it must be written and enforced.
- **Related but distinct rulebooks share the "terms" label** — "T&Cs" and "terms and conditions" name a platform's or vendor's own rules generally, while Meta and Snap label the content-moderation slice specifically "Community Guidelines"; Discord's warning system links to "Terms of Service," "Community Guidelines," and an appeal mechanism as three separate, companion items rather than one document (doc_0505).
- **Sub-policies can shift independently** — X's "public interest policy" changed without broader ToS changing, showing a rulebook is composed of adjustable sub-rules (doc_0025).
- **Transparency and enforcement is a regulatory target** — the DSA specifically requires clear communication of T&Cs and demonstrable enforcement, not merely that terms exist (doc_0024).
- **Disputed, uneven enforcement is a recurring failure mode** — users on Meta's platforms reported content removed despite compliance with Community Guidelines, and inconsistent enforcement (e.g. across Arabic vs. Hebrew content) became a trust and bias issue (doc_0106).
- **Domain-general usage** — the same "terms and conditions" label also covers non-moderation commercial conditions, such as wagering requirements attached to sportsbook bonuses (doc_0072).

## Background (external)

The corpus uses "Terms of Service," "T&Cs," and "terms and conditions" throughout without ever stating a general definition for the term.

More broadly, terms of service — also called terms of use or terms and conditions — are the legal agreement between a service provider and its users: a person must agree to follow them to use the offered service, and for some websites they function simply as a disclaimer (Wikipedia, *Terms of service*, accessed 2026-09-02).
