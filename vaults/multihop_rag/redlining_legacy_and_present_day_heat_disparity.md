# Redlining Legacy and Present-Day Heat Disparity

The researchers compared their findings with historical data from 177 cities and found that today's heat disparity tracks 1930s lending discrimination: neighbourhoods generally graded "D" by lenders had a higher heat index than those graded "A."

The chain begins in the 1930s, when the US government rated neighbourhoods according to their investment worthiness. Many of the neighbourhoods housing poor and minority populations, especially Black and African American residents, were considered riskier investments and therefore received less funding for development and housing programmes. Today those marginalised neighbourhoods suffer far worse environmental conditions than other parts of their respective cities: they have less tree cover and experience higher surface temperatures than neighbourhoods that were not originally redlined. A 1938 Home Owners' Loan Corporation map of Brooklyn, New York shows neighbourhoods colour-coded by loan value, with areas in red marking places where banks and other lenders generally considered residents unworthy of inclusion in lending and home ownership programmes; those redlined districts had a disproportionate number of Black residents. "The disparities were pervasive," Chakraborty said, adding: "This result was very interesting — that this level of inequality and segregation correlated so strongly with the levels of heat disparity."
