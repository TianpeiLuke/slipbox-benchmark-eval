# Haigui And Guihai: The Returnee Founder Cycle

Haigui are Chinese founders who "return from overseas" — a homophone of "sea turtles." Many in the current generation of globalizing Chinese founders studied or worked overseas, and, captivated by the Chinese internet's rapid growth, returned home in the late 2010s to join the likes of Tencent, Baidu, Alibaba and ByteDance; having gained an insider's look into Chinese tech titans, they set out on their own entrepreneurial journey with the hope of becoming the next Jack Ma, the founder of Alibaba.

Guihai are the same people going the other way — those who "return overseas." Their dreams started to crumple following the fall from grace of Ma, whose Ant Group and Alibaba became prominent targets of China's tech crackdown; they soon realized that China had entered a new era, where the regulatory hurdles for running a startup have significantly heightened. Not every startup has the resources to pursue a dual-market strategy, so many "sea turtles" wound up leaving China again. While foreign markets present their own sets of challenges — competition and skepticism toward outsiders — these entrepreneurs perceive a broader, more predictable opportunity in AI in the West. That reversal of their trajectory earned them the moniker guihai.

This wave differs from earlier ones in when the expansion happens. Chinese startups have a long history of going abroad, and every wave has assumed its own approach: previously, many companies would venture out only after succeeding in China, whereas these days more are eyeing global expansion from day one, sometimes even skipping their home market.
