# The Clock Change, Circadian Disruption And Immunity

The health case against the clock change runs through sleep: moving off daylight saving time interferes with the natural circadian rhythm, the 24-hour biological cycle influenced by morning light and evening darkness, and research shows that even a small reduction in sleep affects the immune system — which matters because a good night's sleep during the winter months is essential protection against the seasonal increase in colds and flu.

Giulia Guerrini, lead pharmacist of the digital pharmacy Medino, sets out the grounds and the practical implication. "It's hard to understate the importance of a good night's sleep," she says. "Having too much or too little sleep can have adverse side effects, so you should aim to get between seven and eight hours of sleep as consistently as possible." She names the clock change as a leading disruptor: "One of the biggest disruptors to our sleep is when UK clocks go forward one hour at 1am on the last Sunday in March and go back one hour at 2am on the last Sunday in October, which is rapidly approaching on 30 October." Her advice follows from the premise that consistency matters most: "One of the most important aspects of a healthy sleep schedule is waking up around the same time each morning, so try to limit yourself to a one-hour window if you need to adjust when you get up in the morning and when you go to sleep at night. One hour sounds like an insignificant amount of time, but it can completely throw our circadian rhythm, or more simply known as internal clock, off balance."
