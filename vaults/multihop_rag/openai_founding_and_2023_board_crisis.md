# OpenAI's Founding And Its 2023 Board Crisis

The Rosewood hotel dinner led to the creation of a startup called OpenAI later that year; backed by hundreds of millions of dollars from Elon Musk and other funders, the lab promised to protect the world from Larry Page's vision. Thanks to its ChatGPT chatbot, OpenAI has fundamentally changed the technology industry and introduced the world to the risks and potential of artificial intelligence, and it is valued at more than $US80 billion, about $A123 billion, according to two people familiar with the company's latest funding round. Musk and Sam Altman's partnership did not survive, and the two have since stopped speaking. Altman's own gloss on such splits: "There is disagreement, mistrust, egos. The closer people are to being pointed in the same direction, the more contentious the disagreements are. You see this in sects and religious orders. There are bitter fights between the closest people."

The month before the 9 December 2023 article, that infighting came to OpenAI's boardroom: rebel board members tried to force out Altman because they believed they could no longer trust him to build AI that would benefit humanity, and over five chaotic days OpenAI looked as if it were going to fall apart until the board, pressured by giant investors and by employees who threatened to follow Altman out the door, backed down. The drama gave the world its first glimpse of the bitter feuds among those who will determine the future of AI, but years before the near meltdown there was a little-publicised but ferocious competition in Silicon Valley for control of the technology now quickly reshaping the world, from how children are taught to how wars are fought. That earlier story begins with DeepMind: five years before the Napa Valley party and two before the cat breakthrough on YouTube, Demis Hassabis, a 34-year-old neuroscientist, walked into a cocktail party at Peter Thiel's San Francisco town house and realised he had hit pay dirt, because there in Thiel's living room, overlooking the city's Palace of Fine Arts and a swan pond, was a chessboard; Hassabis had once been the second-best player in the world in the under-14 category, and said "I was preparing for that meeting for a year. I thought that would be my unique hook in: I knew that he loved chess."
