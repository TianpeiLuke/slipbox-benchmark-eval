# Thanksgiving's Black Friday Opening As Consumerism Critique

Eli Roth argues that the Black Friday sale that opens Thanksgiving is not just spectacle but a critique of a rigged economy — the frenzy, he told The Age on 15 November 2023, is driven less by greed than by people who are "paid shit" and can only afford Christmas gifts on one sale day a year.

The grounds are in the film's construction. The idea gives Thanksgiving its raucous opening, what The Age calls possibly the most horribly enjoyable 13 minutes of a film all year — a sick and clever movement that launches the action amid the craze of a Black Friday sale. In the great tradition of seasonal horror classics, the move lets Roth critique the gross commercialisation of the holiday, tap into the era's increasingly stoked class divisions, and evoke the horror genre's rich legacy: the hordes of rampaging shoppers rushing the shopping mall feel like a direct nod to George Romero's anti-capitalist screed Dawn of the Dead (1978). "I love Dawn of the Dead, zombies running the mall, and very much in line with Romero's views on consumerism run amok. That's what it's all about," says Roth. "There's a real darkness underneath it and it's not just people's greed that's behind [the frenzy of these] Black Friday sales. People are paid shit now so there's no middle class any more so they desperately need these Black Friday sales in order to get all the Christmas gifts for their kids, otherwise they can't afford them. So then you have the very few at the top sitting in their mansions watching all the rats fighting over these crumbs, and there's something really sick about that, that everyone's been reduced to fighting for televisions and electronics because the only way they can afford them is on this one sale day, while the few at the top get richer and richer."

The warrant is the horror genre's long history of reflecting the tumult and anxieties of the wider culture that produced it — the opening scenes of crazed shoppers pressed against the jagged glass of the mall also evoke images from the US's January 6 riots, while later scenes of armed school students anxiously and awkwardly defending themselves against a crazed killer feel like they engage with US gun control rhetoric. Roth himself resists over-reading it: "Look, I want it to be where we're in the world of the movie, we're not in the real world. I want people to escape into a rollercoaster ride and have a fun time with the movie. But certainly, if you want to write a film school thesis about it, everything's there." For such a horror tragic, he says, the first goal is always "great kills".
