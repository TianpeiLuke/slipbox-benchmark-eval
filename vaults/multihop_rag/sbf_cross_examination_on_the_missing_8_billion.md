# Bankman-Fried's Cross-Examination On The Missing $8 Billion

On October 31st, 2023, prosecutor Danielle Sassoon walked Bankman-Fried through his reaction to the FTX software bug fixed by Adam Yedidia — a bug that overestimated the amount Alameda owed FTX by about $8 billion — and to the discovery that, even after the bug was fixed, Alameda still owed FTX about $8 billion. His response had been to direct alleged co-conspirator Caroline Ellison to repay third-party loans and to go on making investments. Bankman-Fried said he found out about the fiat@ftx account, the one that tracked how much Alameda owed FTX, in June 2022 while his senior staff was focused on fixing the software bug, but did not discover what the account was for until October.

Simple questions, The Verge's reporter wrote, nailed him to the wall. Had he given his employees at Alameda the direction not to spend FTX customer deposits? Had he put in any policies to prevent Alameda employees from spending FTX customer money? Did he put in place any measures at Alameda to protect FTX customer money? No, no, and no — even though he was testifying to Congress about keeping customer funds safe. Asked who was making decisions to spend $8 billion of customer funds, he could not recall knowing anything about it; asked whether there were rules or requirements for how money borrowed from FTX would be returned, or rules for risk management, he said, "I was concerned with overall risk management." Asked whether he had called anyone in to ask who spent $8 billion, he said, "I wasn't trying to build out blame for it," saying he was focused on solutions; he fired no one. The court also saw an Alameda balance sheet from June 13th, 2022 that included the money it was borrowing from FTX as "ftx borrows," on which he seemed pretty hazy.

The June 2022 testimony resonated most with the reporter. Asked whether he had asked what "fiat@ftx" was, he said he did — but his employees told him "they were busy and I should stop asking questions because it was distracting," words she heard uttered aloud in a court of law. Yedidia — Bankman-Fried's college friend, Bahamas roommate, and FTX employee — had testified that he asked Bankman-Fried about the $8 billion hole on a padel tennis court in their luxury complex in June or July, and on the stand Bankman-Fried seemed to be trying to deny the conversation had ever taken place; it was not until Judge Lewis Kaplan intervened to ask whether Yedidia had ever told him about that money, in words or in substance, that Bankman-Fried admitted he had been told. "So it's your testimony that your supervisees told you to stop asking questions?" Sassoon asked, in a tone so level she could have been filing her nails. His parents were not there; the jurors watched attentively. Phrases like "hole isn't really the word I would use," and answering a question by saying he wanted "a few more qualifiers and scoping on it," led the reporter to conclude that if you know the meaning of the word "epistemology," you absolutely should not testify in your own defense.
