# Digital Engagement Protects The Ageing Brain

The Guardian's 10 October 2023 piece sets out a model in which grappling with technology — not being rescued from it — protects the ageing brain, because novel and difficult tasks force the brain to work in new ways.

The structure has three linked parts. First, the difficulty itself is the active ingredient: a recent study from the University of Texas involving more than 200 elderly adults found that those who engaged with technology had better cognition and memory than their non-engaging peers, and the study authors concluded that digital engagement — whatever the device — protects the brain; even the stress experienced while battling with a computer can be helpful, sharpening mental acuity. These findings reflect earlier studies that found older people who made active use of technology were less likely to get dementia. The implication is that the well-meaning helpfulness of children who "delight in parenting" their parents on all things digital "might not be as beneficial as we think".

Second, novelty generalises beyond devices. Dr Eamon Laird, researcher in health and ageing at Limerick university, agrees that older parents should be encouraged to try new things, and the further out of their comfort zone they feel, the better: "It's always good to keep the mind active and fresh. New challenges can help build and maintain new brain connections and can be good for brain and overall health." Third, sociability compounds the effect: if older people use technology to keep in touch with extended family and friends, "that's a double whammy" — when the author's 84-year-old mother recently joined Instagram she both expanded her grey matter and found an effective means of staying in touch with her grandchildren. Being social, says Laird, is as important as trying new things: "Research shows that those who are more social are also more resilient to chronic conditions and often have a stronger immune system."
