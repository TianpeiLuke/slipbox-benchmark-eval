# The Regulatory Gap Around Unapproved Aircraft Parts

Mary Schiavo's argument is that the regulatory perimeter stops in the wrong place: while plane and engine manufacturers, pilots and aviation companies are heavily regulated, distributors are not — and she pushed for tighter rules in the 1990s but was ignored.

Her grounds are her own casework and its stakes: "I've had cases where counterfeit parts have caused deaths. What's at stake is the safety of the lives of the flying public." The scope of the exposure, in her words, is total: "Literally, if it's on an aeroplane, it could be fake." Following her investigation various reforms came in, including a warning system to flag suspicious components — but not regulation of distributors. The current case is still running: the High Court case against AOG began on September 21 and is ongoing, and the UK's Civil Aviation Authority has said it is supporting the US Federal Aviation Administration and European Aviation Safety Agency in investigating the matter. A spokesman for CFM said: "We are working collaboratively with operators, so they can promptly remove the unauthorised parts from their engines in accordance with the recommendations issued by the regulatory agencies. We remain united with the aviation community in working to keep unapproved parts out of the global supply chain."
