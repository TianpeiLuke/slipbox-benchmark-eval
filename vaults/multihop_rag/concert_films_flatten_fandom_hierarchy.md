# Concert Films Flatten The Fandom Hierarchy

Polygon argues that attending a concert in person does not make you a bigger fan, and that concert films are levelling the field by dropping some of the barriers of price, location, and accessibility — which is why they increasingly look like the future of pop music fandom.

The grounds start with what fans go for. The writer says she goes to a BTS concert to see BTS perform but also to be among other BTS fans who already understand why she cares so much about a Korean boy band: they trade freebies and photocards, help each other set up lightsticks, and share favorite songs or members. That sense of belonging is available whether in a stadium in Korea or a cinema at her local mall. The second ground is who actually gets in: fans able to attend the stadium and arena concerts of the biggest musical artists in the world are not necessarily more passionate — they just have a combination of luck and privilege that others lack — so using in-person attendance to enforce a fandom hierarchy misreads what it measures. She notes that BTS, Taylor Swift, and Beyoncé, whose concert film Renaissance: A Film by Beyoncé hits theaters in December, all have massive fandoms that are geographically scattered and often stigmatized by a mainstream culture that belittles female-driven fan culture, and that in a time when in-person community is harder to come by and can mean pushing people's financial or physical limits, a filmed concert can feel like a gift. The conclusion is qualified: concert films may not be the future of pop music for everyone, but they are increasingly the future of pop music fandom — and that is a good thing, because pop music fandom is a way to find collective joy and connection in art, and anyone who wants in should be able to join the party.
