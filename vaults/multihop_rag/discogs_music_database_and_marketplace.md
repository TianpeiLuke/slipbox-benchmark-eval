# Discogs As Music Database And Marketplace

Discogs is the internet's foremost database of recorded music and one of the most extensive marketplaces available for physical music media, with every bit of it generated and offered by users. On it you can catalog your collection, look up information about even the most obscure artists, cross-check record store prices to see whether your local shop has a markup, and purchase records, typically at something close to their "market rate." For a devoted vinyl collector or an obsessive music fan — as is often the case, both — it is very nearly a lifestyle.

What distinguishes it is the way the database is built. Founded in 2000 by Intel programmer Kevin Lewandowski, it was initially conceived as something of a Wikipedia for recorded music, which it predates by a few months, and it has not changed a great deal since its conception besides the introduction of the marketplace in the mid-aughts. Sellers have to submit a record's information if it is not already in the database in order to sell it, which is how the database became so complete; many entries, even for deep obscurities, are fastidious, with album covers and liner notes scanned for inclusion and album credits fleshed out with hyperlinks that are almost more useful and thorough than the Wikipedia equivalent, plus reviews by devotees, and a lively forum where all these details get litigated. The Verge's example, from October 7th, 2023: the entry for a Japanese pressing of Thelonious Monk in Italy includes photos of the record jacket and both sides of the vinyl. Doug Martin, who started selling on Discogs in 2020, described the users as different from casual buyers: "Some people just buy records for the album art hanging on the wall... These were real fans listening to real music who cared about the format and the medium. That's what attracted me in the beginning."

Its boundary case is its own position between two internets. Discogs is a fairly clunky, definitely old-fashioned website devoted to even older technology — a vestige of an earlier, more idyllic internet that has spent the last decade walking the record-needle-thin line between 2020s algorithmically driven tech monolith and niche unprofitable obscurity. It became a central part of the music internet, surviving physical media's replacement by MP3s and then streaming and rebounding as interest in vinyl, CDs, and tapes did throughout the 2010s. It is an insular community in many ways, but it is also, and has always been, a money-making endeavor for both Discogs and the sellers who use it — and sellers say the site's old tech has started to wear on them while new fees and restrictions have made it harder to do business.
