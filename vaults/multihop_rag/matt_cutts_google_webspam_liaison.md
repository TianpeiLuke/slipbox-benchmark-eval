# Matt Cutts, Google's Webspam Liaison

Matt Cutts was a celebrity among SEOs, constantly mobbed with questions and complaints; before he left Google he determined that he had sent about 50,000 emails to people outside the company during his decade and a half there.

The conference dynamic around him ran one way. Several SEOs described trying to get Cutts to drink at conferences so he would "spill secrets," as one put it, but what generally ended up happening was that all the SEOs would get drunk instead while Cutts stayed sober, jotting down the latest SEO methods on a small notepad, sitting quietly in the corner at the bar. "My favorite question to ask an SEO," Cutts said, was "What's the worst thing you've ever done?" — which prompted responses that felt like "a cross between showing off and a confessional." So many SEOs were tempted to reveal the vulnerabilities they had discovered in Google's algorithms, even when they were talking to the one person they really should not have been talking to, the guy who was planning to go back to his office and make those vulnerabilities disappear.

Cutts, as an individual, seemed to be doing his best within an expanding corporate behemoth to remain approachable. "One thing I learned early on was that even when someone was shouting at you, there's a kernel of something you needed to hear in the other person and listen to and respect and integrate and incorporate," he said. Most SEOs said they appreciated his efforts. When Google released the 2011 Panda update that devastated a generation of SEO businesses, Cutts openly recognized the impossible task of achieving the kind of epistemological neutrality Google's founders had initially promised, telling Wired at the time, "[T]he only way to be neutral is either to randomize the links or to do it alphabetically."

The hostility was still real. Some blamed him personally for "killing" companies that had relied on the previous iteration of the algorithm, and during his time at Google he regularly received death threats and hate mail. When SEOs would send, say, a fruit plate or a brownie cake addressed to him at Google's offices, he said, "We'd take it down to the kitchen with a note warning: possibly poisoned."
