# Bail

## Definition

Bail is the term the corpus uses for Sam Bankman-Fried's pretrial release from custody: a $250 million bond that let him live under house arrest at his parents' home in Palo Alto instead of in jail while awaiting trial, and which a U.S. judge revoked in August 2023 (doc_0010, doc_0226).

## Context

The corpus's clearest illustration is Sam Bankman-Fried's case: after being arrested in the Bahamas in December 2022 and extradited to the U.S., he was released on a $250 million bail bond and confined to house arrest at his parents' home in Palo Alto (doc_0010). That release was not permanent — a U.S. judge revoked his bail in August 2023 after he was accused of intimidating former Alameda CEO Caroline Ellison by leaking her private diary, leaving him in custody by the time his fraud trial began that October (doc_0010, doc_0226). Bail is also treated as something a court can simply refuse to grant: one recap of the FTX saga notes flatly that "SBF has been denied bail" (doc_0030). The concept resurfaces around Bankman-Fried's co-defendants, where house arrest — the condition often attached to bail — is described as a lighter alternative to imprisonment that cooperating felons like Gary Wang, Nishad Singh, and Caroline Ellison might still face even without prison time (doc_0121).

## Key Characteristics

- **Conditional, not unconditional freedom** — release on bail came bundled with restrictions (house arrest at a fixed address) rather than free movement (doc_0010).
- **Financially secured** — Bankman-Fried's release required a $250 million bail bond, tying his liberty to a monetary guarantee (doc_0010).
- **Revocable** — a judge can revoke bail mid-proceedings for conduct that violates its terms, as happened after the Ellison intimidation allegations, moving the defendant from house arrest to detention (doc_0010, doc_0226).
- **Discretionary and sometimes denied outright** — courts can decline to grant bail at all, as they did for Bankman-Fried at least once during his case (doc_0030).
- **Distinct from post-conviction outcomes** — bail concerns pretrial release, while house arrest can separately appear as a possible post-conviction alternative to imprisonment for cooperating defendants (doc_0121).

## Background (external)

The corpus never defines "bail" itself — it only reports how bail applied in Bankman-Fried's case.
Per Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute, bail is "the money a defendant pays as a guarantee that they will show up in court at a later date," and release can be "conditioned on certain behavior of the defendant," such as staying away from an alleged victim (Cornell Law School LII, *Wex: bail*, accessed 2026-09-02).
A defendant's failure to comply "triggers the bond obligation and allows the court to keep any money given as security" (Cornell Law School LII, *Wex: bail*, accessed 2026-09-02).
