# Relative Valuation: Definition And Prevalence

Relative valuation is the art of attempting to determine the fair value of a stock based on how it is valued relative to many factors. If the PE of a stock is 18, whether that is cheap or expensive depends on many things: the only way to assess a single multiple, whether 15 or 18 or 30, is by analysing that one number against its growth prospects, peer company multiples, the industry trading range, its historical average PE, prevailing interest rates and so on. Whenever an investor decides to buy or sell a stock based on its PE, P/B, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue or even EV/subscriber ratio, they are engaging in relative valuation, and doing so is entirely acceptable provided it is approached the right way with adequate risk management; the broader the assessment, that is the more parameters a multiple is checked against, the better the perspective.

Findings from research done a decade earlier by Aswath Damodaran indicated that around 85 per cent of valuation done by equity research analysts and 50 per cent of corporate M&A transactions were based on relative valuation. The discriminating contrast with absolute valuation, covered in the previous week's edition, is put as a formula: absolute valuation is made up of an interesting story that is backed by credible and validated numbers, while relative valuation is made up of an interesting number backed by a credible and validated story. Structurally, every number in relative valuation is the result of a number A divided by a number B, as PE is the price of the stock divided by its earnings, and these ratios exist to be compared against similar metrics of other stocks. The first branching point before using relative valuation to pick stocks is that it can be done using either equity multiples or firm/enterprise multiples.
