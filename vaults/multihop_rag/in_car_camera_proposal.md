# In-Car Camera Proposal

Uber's in-app safety features cannot work for the riders who need them most, so survivors are asking for in-vehicle surveillance and more extensive background checks instead — the argument attorney Rachel Abrams put to TechCrunch on 2023-10-13. Abrams argues that Uber's safety features are insufficient because they're all app-based and only potentially useful if a rider has access to their own phone.

The grounds are the circumstances in which the assaults she has handled actually occurred. "The majority of clients I represent and the women I've spoken to who have been sexually assaulted have experienced: (A) someone else ordered the ride for them; (B) their phone is dead or they can't locate their phone to use the safety shield; or (C) if they could use their phone, they're incapacitated," said Abrams. She says she has interviewed upward of 5,000 survivors over the course of several years and has seen no difference in the number of attacks before or after implementation of these in-app features.

The demands go beyond cameras. Aside from in-vehicle surveillance and more extensive background checks, the survivors are asking Uber to implement driver training on interactions with passengers, a zero-tolerance policy for drivers, sexual harassment education and training, and a more adequate system to encourage customer reporting and monitor customer complaints. On screening, Uber has lobbied against additional background requirements for drivers, which has given the company authority to conduct its own background checks with little or no oversight, unlike most taxi operators. Uber and Lyft say smudged fingerprints can lead to inaccurate results and that fingerprint checks reference historical arrest records, which can have discriminatory effects on some minority communities that face disproportionately high arrest rates; an Uber spokesperson told CNN arrest records are incomplete and often lack information about whether a person has been convicted of a crime.

The evidentiary record available to test the claim is partial. Uber hasn't released safety data for 2021 and 2022, and won't say when it plans to do so, though older data backs up some of Abrams' claims. Uber has published two safety reports — one containing data from 2017 to 2018 and another with data from 2019 to 2020 — and the ride-hail company claims that the rate of sexual assault reported on the app decreased 38% between its first and second reports.
