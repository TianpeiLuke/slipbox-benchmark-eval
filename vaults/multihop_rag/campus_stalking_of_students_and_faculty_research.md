# Campus Stalking Research On Students And Faculty

Students are historically the most vulnerable population at risk of being stalked on college campuses: in one study across eight universities in the southwest, 17 percent of students reported being stalked since enrolling, with women, transgender or gender-nonconforming, and sexual minority students more likely to be victims, and reported perpetrators including strangers, acquaintances, friends, former partners, other classmates, and non-students. Many college protective measures arose in response to inappropriate relationships between students and teachers, and in some cases professors have become harassers — Donna Freitas detailed in her 2019 memoir Consent: A Memoir of Unwanted Attention a two-year ordeal of being stalked by her professor, and at the University of Central Florida a professor was arrested after sending a student over 800 messages a day, one of which read: "You should be happy that someone likes you this much to stalk you."

The much smaller body of research on faculty who are stalked is only now being strengthened. A 2009 study on student stalking of faculty in the Journal of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning found that the question of whether professors are at risk from their students had received little attention; yet the 52 faculty members interviewed reported 87 concerning incidents, ranging from repeated unwanted messages, following, obsessive watching, and sexually coercive behavior toward the faculty member to endangerment, threats, and attempts to harm or kill them. Academics in that study said things like, a "student would have to injure me to be taken off campus… someone has to get hurt before something is done," and "There is a tendency to immediately take the student's side over the professor…[the] professor has no rights in this process." Others failed to report incidents at all, describing embarrassment, helplessness, and a personal sense of responsibility for the student's behavior: "Made me question what I was doing to promote this. What would make them think they could do this to me?" Victoria O'Meara, a post-doctoral research fellow at Royal Roads University who has been interviewing scholars in the US and Canada for a study on online abuse of faculty, told The Verge there has been "an increasingly organized attack on academia," and that scholars say their universities remain ill-equipped to respond to it or support faculty, let alone protect them.

The danger is not new, and the record includes killings: in 2002 three nursing professors at the University of Arizona were killed by a student who had harassed and stalked them for a year; four years later a student at Loyola University spent a year making harassing phone calls to a professor before attempting to burn down his house; a University of Southern California psychology professor was stabbed to death on campus in 2016 by a student, despite warnings to police and university administrators about threats made by the same person over a year earlier; and in 2022 at the University of Arizona an expelled student shot and killed a professor after various faculty had reported a history of threats, harassment, and abuse to the university and police. Over the last two decades US colleges and universities have emphasized policies to protect students, but some within academia now call on institutions to do more to defend professors and staff, who are also commonly targeted. Today's academics have become public figures online and in the media amid rising political polarization, racism and misogyny, and attacks on intellectualism; many threats now come from individuals anywhere in the world, making harassers harder to track down or punish. Scholars appear regularly in the press, maintain personal webpages, post on social media, and are encouraged to write for broader audiences — expectations of a job once largely confined to campus and field. The Professor Watchlist, launched in 2016, has grown from an original roster of 200 to nearly 1,000 scholars including Angela Davis, Ibram X. Kendi, and Noam Chomsky, regularly posting photos and information about those deemed radical professors "advancing leftists propaganda in the classroom," and as attacks on critical race theory, Black history, and books or courses addressing gender identity have exploded, many educators feel under scrutiny and at risk of extremist threats. Even for less famous academics, the substance of the work makes them targets: Umamaheswar's publications included research into "policing and racial (in)justice in the media," Sinha's included titles on "racial discrimination in the United States," and the two co-authored a paper on wrongful imprisonment. Both come from South Asian backgrounds, and it was not lost on either of them that S. is white — based on their own knowledge of the criminal justice system, it would not have been implausible for law enforcement not to treat her behavior as a serious threat in the first place.
