# Scorsese's Late-Career Genre Departures

Alongside the films that revisit his old themes, Polygon's 2023-10-26 piece identifies a set of radical departures in Scorsese's late era — films whose relation to his body of work is contrast rather than continuation.

2004's The Aviator is his most conventional movie to date, the most traditional, Oscar-baity, Old Hollywood biography he has made, and is best understood as a director-for-hire palate-cleanser after the odyssey of making the perpetually troubled Gangs of New York. 2010's Shutter Island is horror and suspense, an expert exercise in pure genre, part Hammer Films and part Alfred Hitchcock, and it also contains some of the most inventive, moving, and painterly visuals he has ever committed to film.

2011's Hugo is perhaps the hardest Scorsese film to imagine a younger Scorsese making: a 3D Robert Zemeckis-style family film that is also about the end of life and about immortality in art. He made it as a love letter to Georges Méliès, Harold Lloyd, and his own then-12-year-old daughter Francesca, and it seems designed to remind fathers of all the times they tried to introduce their kids to their favorite old films. Hugo is explicitly about Scorsese's legendary cinephilia in a way his earlier films are not, and it is sentimental in a way his work has never been before or since.
