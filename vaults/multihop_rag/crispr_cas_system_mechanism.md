# The CRISPR-Cas System Mechanism

CRISPR works by targeting and cleaving specific sequences of DNA from the genome: a "guide" leads CRISPR's famous "molecular scissors" to the targeted gene, which either deactivates the gene or allows it to be removed and swapped for different DNA.

Although made famous as a powerful gene-editing tool, CRISPR technology was actually adapted from an immune system found in many bacteria, CRISPR-Cas. The key components of that immune system are molecular scissors known as Cas proteins, and a memory bank of DNA snippets that a bacterium has collected from phages that once infected it; by tapping its memory bank, CRISPR-Cas can guide its lethal scissors to a precise point in an invading phage's DNA and snip it like a piece of ribbon. In the laboratory version, a Cas enzyme cuts through a target DNA strand and is told where to cut by an RNA strand. The property that makes the system attractive against HIV is that it can target the viral DNA embedded in the host genome rather than only stopping replication, which is why research groups believe the strategy could be effective in removing latent HIV infections.
