# Why Phage Therapy Resists Widespread Resistance

Unlike with antibiotics, bacteria are unlikely to gain widespread resistance to phage therapy, argues Paul Turner, director of the Center for Phage Biology and Therapy at Yale University: "if phage therapy were used at a global scale, that it would not lead to the same problem of widespread resistance to it, the way that antibiotic use has led to that problem," he told Live Science, a conclusion he says he and other experts have reached.

The grounds are twofold. First, antibiotic resistance has been dramatically accelerated by the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, especially broad-spectrum antibiotics that work on a variety of bacteria, whereas phages can have much narrower targets than even narrow-spectrum antibiotics — for instance, targeting a protein found in only one or a few strains within one bacterial species. Second, although the target bacterium can still evolve resistance to an individual phage, by picking the right combination of phages scientists can make it so that the bacterium's evolution comes at a cost, Turner said; that cost might be a decrease in virulence or an increased vulnerability to antibiotics. The warrant is that resistance spreads widely only when selection pressure is broad and cost-free, and phage therapy satisfies neither condition.
