# Cancer Immunotherapy And Checkpoint Inhibitors

Immunotherapy is a pharmaceutical way of using the body's immune system to eradicate cancer cells, the latest step in a progression from plain chemotherapy to surgery, radiation and now immunotherapy. The mechanism works through a switch on T-cells: the PD-1 protein on T-cells is deactivated by the PD-L1 protein found on the surface of a cancerous cell, so drugs that target PD-1, namely Keytruda from Merck and Opdivo from Bristol-Myers Squibb, and Tecentriq, a PD-L1 inhibitor by Roche, can stop T-cells from switching off by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction between a cancer cell and a T-cell, which allows normal immune action on cancer cells.

Pembrolizumab, branded Keytruda and approved in 2014, is the first major monoclonal antibody or mAB from Merck; it has raked in approvals for several cancers and generated $20 billion in sales in 2022, working by helping T-cells find and attack cancer cells. The range over which the mechanism holds is limited: mABs have not had a high success rate, with treatment-resistant patient cohorts standing considerably high at 70-80 per cent.
