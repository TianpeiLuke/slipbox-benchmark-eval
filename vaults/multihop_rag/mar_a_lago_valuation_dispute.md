# Mar-a-Lago Valuation Dispute

Mar-a-Lago is the sharpest of the valuation disputes: Trump valued the Palm Beach estate as high as $739 million — more than 10 times a more reasonable estimate of its worth, Fortune reported in September 2023 — on the idea that the property could be developed for residential use, when deed terms prohibit that, Attorney General Letitia James said. The Age's November 2023 comparison put Trump's 2021 valuation at $US612.1 million, Bloomberg's 2023 valuation at $US240 million, and New York's 2021 valuation at $US27.6 million.

The gap turns on how the property may be used. Trump made a series of agreements with Palm Beach County and the National Trust for Historic Preservation for Mar-a-Lago to be used only as a club, giving up development rights, so Palm Beach values the property as a business on its tax rolls and in 2021 assessed it at $US27.6 million, lower than comparable residential properties; New York State argues this is the appraisal Trump should have been using. That figure drew objections: New York's estimated value "was a shock to the real estate community and anybody with any understanding of the island and its values," said Liza Pulitzer, a real estate agent at Brown Harris Stevens and a Palm Beach native. "It could easily fetch $US500 million or more." That assumes a buyer views it as a single-family residence; it is currently not zoned for such use, though Trump is able to reside there through a loophole designating himself an employee. Trump's legal team engaged an expert witness who argued a future buyer could do the same thing, and could reduce the club to a membership of one, though it is unclear whether Palm Beach would allow that without a change to zoning status.

The property's economics have also shifted. Historically Mar-a-Lago never made much money for the Trump Organisation, but it took in about $US41 million in revenue in 2022 according to Trump's most recent ethics disclosure, compared with $US21 million in 2019. Bloomberg values it at $US240 million based on a combination of comparable residential property sales in the area and its value as a business, reflecting uncertainty around its future status — uncertainty not shared by everyone: if the city and county allowed it, "there is no reason why it wouldn't be able to converted to a full-time private residence," said Eli Beracha, director of the Hollo School of Real Estate at Florida International University. The Age also notes the estate has been at the centre of publicised post-presidency scandals, from classified documents kept in one of its bathrooms to being the location where Trump is said to have shared nuclear submarine secrets with Australian businessman Anthony Pratt, which Trump denies.
