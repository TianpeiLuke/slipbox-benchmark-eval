# Google's Defense: Android Openness And Sideloading

Google plans to push back at Epic's claim that it does not have competition in the app store business — the premise on which Epic's monopoly theory rests. It will point out that not only does the Play Store compete with Apple's App Store, which the Ninth Circuit ruling in the Apple case agreed upon too, but Android also supports competitive app stores like Samsung's Galaxy Store and Amazon's Appstore.

Google will also attack the foreclosure premise directly. It will note that, unlike Apple, it allows apps to be sideloaded on Android devices — something Epic takes advantage of today — and that this process is streamlined and helps keep users safe. Epic will claim in response that the process is made to be overly difficult for end users, with multiple steps involved and "scare screens" that warn users of the security risks involved with sideloading.

On discovery, Epic will suggest that Google hid items by allowing execs and employees to have "off the record" chats; Google will counter this as well, saying it has provided the court with thousands of chat logs and millions of documents. Google will ultimately push the notion that this case is not about competition but about money — that is, that Epic Games wants to reach the Play Store's 2.5 billion users without having to pay. As a Google blog post by Wilson White, VP, Government Affairs & Public Policy, put it: "Android's choice and flexibility work well for consumers and developers of all sizes. We look forward to making our case in court as we fight to keep our users safe from harm, partner with developers to grow their businesses, and keep the Android ecosystem thriving and healthy for everyone."
