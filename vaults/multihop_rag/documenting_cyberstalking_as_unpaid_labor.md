# Documenting Cyberstalking As Unpaid Labor

Alex Sinha became obsessive about documenting S.'s tweets: not a week went by without him routinely checking them at night and again as soon as he woke up. "It has just become part of the rhythm of my day," he told The Verge. "I'll be waiting at the bus stop for my kids to get off the bus, and I'll be like, 'Okay, let me take out my phone and take screenshots.' Or I'm at the airport waiting for my flight or at a restaurant waiting to pick up my food." He captured the images for legal reasons and so his wife would not have to read them. "On one level, it has become so ordinary, just part of my day, to read her outrageous, racist views. But at the same time, it never stops being outrageous," he said. He pulled back from using social media himself and rarely tweeted anymore. Though S. had never acted on any of her outlandish threats, he had no idea what she was capable of: "If I didn't see it, and I didn't prepare, and something happened, I would never forgive myself." At one point he read a tweet suggesting S. had been watching him and his wife in a public place, describing a specific spot where the couple often took walks together.

Platform reporting did not stop it. Sinha, Umamaheswar, and Tan filed complaints with Twitter for online abuse over and over; at first, according to Sinha, the company responded that S. had not violated Twitter policies. Tan's husband was especially persistent in filing complaints, and one of S.'s accounts would get banned only for another under a new handle to pop up in its place — a pattern that repeated while the tweets kept coming, sometimes trickling over to other professors online. Sinha screenshotted a tweet from S. on November 9th, 2022 aimed at an Asian American assistant professor of sociology then at the University of Chicago whom Tan follows, reading, "Hey can you stop stalking and sexually harassing me on Twitter," and a similar tweet from the same account on the page of an Asian American astrophysicist Tan also follows, accusing him of being abusive to women he had never met online and saying she had looked into reporting him. Tan felt obligated to reach out to her Twitter mutual contacts to warn them about S., now seemingly hunting for other Asian American academics connected to her. Sinha's later tweets from S. included lines such as "Reporting Asians for sexual harassment is a new hobby of mine," claims that the professors were "racist Asian supremacists" abusing white women, and a threat to beat an "Asian chick" until hospitalized.

The burden of proof and of pre-emptive disclosure falls on the targets. When Sinha co-authored a paper with a colleague from another university and they wanted to share the news on Twitter, he felt duty bound to text a warning: "There's a chance she's going to engage in some hostile way." Any colleague, collaborator, or friend who entered his orbit risked being targeted. Southern Connecticut State University officials and police had already dealt with S. and there were receipts to prove it — S. had even posted on social media an email she received from Detective William S. Rivera of the Southern Connecticut State University Police, along with his phone number — but officials at the professors' new institutions had no long record of the past issues, so the burden of evidence to alert new or prospective employers and to warn colleagues and social media friends falls on them for as long as S. is allowed to keep stalking. "I don't want to upset my employer," said Sinha, who does not yet have tenure; in an environment where academic positions are scarce and competitive, he wonders how the whiff of an accusation or the presence of a stalker might tip the scales in a job interview or performance process. "This has been hanging around my neck now for a while," he said, adding that he knows he has to be proactive: "I should get out in front of it." By then the tweets were coming by the minute from the account @janedoepow, announcing an intent to report the three to their schools for online sexual abuse and harassment, predicting they would get anxious when confronted by their employers, and stating that Vassar and GMU would each get an email. Sinha told his wife and alerted Tan; all three would need to notify their department chairs and administrations at Hofstra, George Mason, and Vassar about S. and any potential false allegations headed their way. "We have enough experience with her to know that this is probably not the end of the problem," Sinha said — and he was right. He falls asleep, as he does every night, the echoes of S.'s comments still in his subconscious.
