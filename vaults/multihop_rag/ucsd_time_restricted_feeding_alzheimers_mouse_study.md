# The UC San Diego Time-Restricted Feeding Study In Alzheimer's Mice

Participating in intermittent, time-restricted fasting could lead to a reduced risk of cognitive deterioration, a study published in the journal Cell Metabolism suggests: researchers at University of California San Diego School of Medicine adjusted the feeding schedule of certain groups of mice so that they only ate within six-hour windows each day, which the researchers noted would be equivalent to 14 hours of fasting for humans.

The framing question was about timing rather than content -- it has been proven that what people eat can help prevent or slow Alzheimer's disease, but the study asked about when they eat.

Compared to a control group of mice that ate on demand, the fasting mice showed improvements in memory, were less hyperactive in the evenings and had fewer sleep disruptions. They also were shown to have less buildup of amyloid proteins in the brain, which is a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease.

The size of the effect exceeded what the team expected. "We were hopeful to see some improvements in pathology, but did not expect such profound effects in reducing plaques and inflammation and improving memory," a study author said. "The effects of time-restricted feeding on lower amyloid pathology were also measurable in blood using markers found in the clinic, which was another important finding."
