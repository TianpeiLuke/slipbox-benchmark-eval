# Skepticism That OpenAI's Board Will Use Its Veto

TechCrunch's objection is not to the design of OpenAI's escalation process but to the assumption that the board will actually exercise the veto it has been granted. The process is meant to short-circuit anything like what was rumored to have happened before the big drama — a high-risk product or process getting greenlit without the board's awareness or approval. But the result of that drama was the sidelining of two of the more critical voices and the appointment of some money-minded figures, Bret Taylor and Larry Summers, who are sharp but not AI experts by a long shot.

That yields the central question: if a panel of experts makes a recommendation and the CEO makes decisions based on that information, will this friendly board really feel empowered to contradict them and hit the brakes? And if it does, will anyone hear about it? Transparency is not really addressed outside a promise that OpenAI will solicit audits from independent third parties.

A second gap concerns disclosure of critical-risk findings. Suppose a model is developed that warrants a "critical" risk category. OpenAI has not been shy about tooting its horn about this kind of thing in the past — talking about how wildly powerful its models are, to the point where it declines to release them, is great advertising. But there is no guarantee that such disclosure will happen if the risks are so real and OpenAI is so concerned about them; maybe it would be a bad idea, but either way it is not really mentioned.
