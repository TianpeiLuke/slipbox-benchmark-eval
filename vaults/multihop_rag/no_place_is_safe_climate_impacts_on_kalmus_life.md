# "No Place Is Safe": Climate Impacts on Kalmus's Life

Kalmus concluded that "no place was safe" after a 2020 heat wave — considered nearly impossible if not for the influence of climate change — seared the Pacific Northwest for three days, killing hundreds of people, buckling roadways and sending overheated baby birds jumping from their nests to their deaths, in the very region where he had dreamed of making a life he thought might be buffered from the worst of climate change.

"It feels like it's worse than I thought it would be in 2023," Kalmus said, pointing to record-low Antarctic sea ice and record-high sea surface and land temperatures as signs that Earth systems could be shifting more quickly than the scientific community can grasp. In recent years climate change has begun to sting his personal life: in summer 2020 he sickened himself hiking in a California heat wave, then watched a wildfire burn a few miles from his California home, spewing smoke that left his voice raspy and his head aching, while the searing temperatures killed a dogwood tree in his front yard and his productivity slowed so that he could not focus on science. His family relocated to North Carolina for his wife Sharon's work, but the Pacific Northwest experience planted a seed: if he could not stop climate change and could not avoid it, could he at least find a better way to survive it? He knew what would not work — "I very quickly eliminated the idea of being a prepper — you know, hoarding beans and ammunition," he said — but he found himself daydreaming of a simpler life where he could keep bees, grow vegetables and press cider to drink on a Friday night, living closer to the land.
