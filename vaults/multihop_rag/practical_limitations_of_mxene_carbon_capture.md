# Practical Limitations of MXene Carbon Capture

Against the enthusiasm for MXenes as carbon absorbers, the article notes that because they are still fairly new, MXenes today are only used in research, and scientists have to iron out several kinks before they will be ready for real-world use — which will take several years at least.

The specific weaknesses attack different premises of the case. Chemically, MXenes can break down and transform into other solids called oxides in the presence of oxygen, so water and oxygen make MXenes less reactive, though scientists think they can overcome this by tweaking MXenes' structure — capping the materials' edges with bulky salts, for instance, could shield them from damage by oxygen. In manufacturing, it is hard right now to make large amounts: in the lab, researchers can make only several kilograms in a single batch, and several companies are working out how to boost batch sizes. Anupma Thakur points out one irony: making MXenes for carbon capture can have a big carbon footprint, because it takes a lot of electricity to get the heat needed to make them and much of that electricity is made using CO2-spewing fossil fuels, so it is important to make sure the energy used does not spew more CO2 than the MXenes can later sop up — otherwise their use in carbon capture is defeated. MXenes also cost a lot to make, and that high cost would matter less if they could withstand thousands of CO2 capture-and-release cycles so that new material need not keep being made; so far no one has tested MXenes' durability for stored CO2 removal and reuse, though it helps that they are mechanically rugged, with research showing certain types can be as strong as steel.
