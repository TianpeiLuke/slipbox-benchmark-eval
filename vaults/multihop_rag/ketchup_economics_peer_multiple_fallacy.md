# Ketchup Economics: The Peer-Multiple Fallacy

Because relative valuation is based on comparison with peer multiples, it inherits a defect in its own premise: an overvalued peer cannot be used to justify expensive valuation in one stock, so investors must take measures to check whether the peer multiples are reasonable as well. The attack is on the inference step that treats a peer group's prices as a valid benchmark, not on the arithmetic of the multiples themselves.

Economist Larry Summers termed this Ketchup economists or ketchup economics, a sarcastic take on some highly paid economists and finance professionals who are concerned only with inter-relationships between the prices of different financial assets and conclude from those relationships whether the price of an asset independently is efficient: if two bottles of ketchup sell for twice as much as one bottle, except for minor differences traceable to transaction costs, the market is called efficient. The risk is that this ignores whether one bottle of ketchup is priced rationally in the first place. Nobel Laureate Paul Krugman once explained how the US housing bubble that precipitated the global financial crisis reminded him of ketchup economists: before purchasing a house during the bubble, buyers carefully compared prices with prices of other houses, but ignored whether the overall level of a home price made sense.
