# No Evidence Cranberries Were At The First Thanksgiving

"There is no evidence the Pilgrims had cranberries at the first Thanksgiving," said Brian Wick of Massachusetts Cranberries — despite cranberries being one of the few fruits native to North America that grow in the northern United States — FOX News Lifestyle reported on 23 November 2023.

The grounds are documentary and botanical. The Pilgrims explored and then settled the Cape Cod and Plymouth area in 1620 and celebrated the first Thanksgiving the following year, and Richard S. Cox and Jacob Walker write in "Massachusetts Cranberry Culture: A History from Bog to Table" that "when the Pilgrims first set foot on Cape Cod, even before they saw Plymouth Rock, they may have well stepped on the American cranberry". But it "seems doubtful" the Pilgrims even noticed them: "For all its tasty charms, the cranberry is hardly the sort to attract attention." Pilgrim Edward Winslow provided the only contemporary account of the first Thanksgiving, and he mentions only fowl and deer and "our harvest being gotten in"; scholars believe the harvest certainly included corn, but the rest of what they feasted on is unknown.

The warrant offered for the omission is the plant's own inconspicuousness. Cox and Walker suspect it might simply have been overlooked, citing colonial settler John Josselyn, who wrote in 1672 that the cranberry is nothing but "a small trailing plant" and a humble vine "that grows over Salt Marshes that are overgrown with moss", and Benjamin Eastwood — touted by Cox and Walker as one of the cranberry's "greatest promoters" — who called it a "simple, insignificant-looking plant" and a "stunted barren thing". The conclusion is that the cranberry's native habitat around Plymouth and its ascension to a uniquely vibrant place on America's Thanksgiving table are something of a coincidence, and a 20th-century phenomenon.
