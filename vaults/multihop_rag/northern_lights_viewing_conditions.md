# Northern Lights Viewing Conditions

To maximise your chances of seeing the Northern Lights, work through these factors in order: check geomagnetic activity, pick an unobstructed northern horizon, go out at night away from city lights, allow for the moon, and time the outing to the hours around midnight in the September-to-March or April season.

Start with geomagnetic activity. When the geomagnetic field is active, the aurora will be brighter, stronger and further from the magnetic poles, according to the Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC). The best time to see the Northern Lights is when the planetary K index is at an eight or nine and the aurora moves toward the equator, the weather service site also said.

Then choose the site. There are many places across the world to view the vivid display, but you will want to make sure to find a location that has no obstructions — aim to find a place with minimal tree lines or hills so that the northern horizon can be fully seen. Go at night, away from the city lights, in order to capture the best view of the Aurora Borealis. Allow for moonlight as well: "The full moon will also diminish the apparent brightness of the aurora (not the actual brightness)," the SWPC continued.

Finally, time it. The Northern Lights are usually on full display from September to March or April, but the best time to see them is in the late evening or early morning. "Best aurora is usually within an hour or two of midnight (between 10 p.m. and 2 a.m. local time)," the SWPC reported. "There may be aurora in the evening and morning, but it is usually not as active and therefore, not as visually appealing."
