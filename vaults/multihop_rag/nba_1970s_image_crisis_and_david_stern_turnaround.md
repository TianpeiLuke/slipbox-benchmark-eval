# The NBA's 1970s Image Crisis And How David Stern Reversed It

The NBA's turnaround under David Stern is offered by Essentially Sports on 2023-10-25 as a worked example of how a league in disrepute rebuilds mass appeal. The starting state: if you were around in the 1970s — which would make you at least 50 years old today — you would know the NBA did not have the mass appeal it enjoys now. Average game attendance hovered around 8,000 a game in one of the darkest periods of the basketball league, marked by drug abuse and brawls on the hardwood court that sometimes spilled over onto coaches and players. As reported in the Washington Post, even though there are no reliable figures on cocaine use by players, "estimates by people in the game range from 40 percent to 75 percent." Racial hostility was part of the picture too: the New York Knicks became the first all-Black team in 1979, and their fans made their disapproval very apparent — "It wasn't so much New York attitudes, but fans around the league let you know how they felt about you as an opponent and as a Black man as well," Knicks star Earl Monroe told Newsday.

The intervention and its effects run in a chain. Billionaire entrepreneur Mark Cuban, who paid approximately $1.8 million in penalties on behalf of his team, the Dallas Mavericks, fondly remembered former NBA commissioner David Stern on his death in January 2020. Stern instituted an anti-drug agreement and even banned All-Star player Michal Richardson for life for drug abuse. The revitalized image of the NBA helped rake in the moolah from sponsors and made the league more appealing to fans. Stern also went against the tide to open up the Olympic basketball competition to NBA players, resulting in the famous "Dream Team" of 1992 — and America has never failed to win a medal in the 19 Olympic basketball games it has played since then.
