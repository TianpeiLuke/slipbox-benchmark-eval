# Banking-as-a-Service

## Definition

Banking-as-a-service (BaaS) is the label TechCrunch's coverage applies to platforms — Synapse and QI Tech — that let banks and fintech companies build financial services on top of them, rather than platforms that serve end customers as a bank would.

## Context

The corpus surfaces the term through two TechCrunch items: Synapse, covered in the fintech newsletter The Interchange as a San Francisco–based company whose platform "enables banks and fintech companies to easily develop financial services," and QI Tech, covered in the general Startups Weekly newsletter as a Brazilian "banking-as-a-service platform" that raised a large Series B.

Both appear as fintech infrastructure providers rather than consumer-facing banks — Synapse sits behind other companies' financial products, and QI Tech's raise is framed as backing for the same kind of platform business in Brazil.

## Key Characteristics

- **Platform, not bank** — the BaaS provider "operates a platform enabling banks and fintech companies to easily develop financial services" rather than serving end customers directly.
- **Infrastructure for others' products** — Synapse's role is to let other banks and fintechs build financial services on top of it, positioning it as embedded infrastructure rather than a retail brand.
- **Investor interest despite volatility** — QI Tech's BaaS platform drew a $200 million Series B, the largest Brazilian raise across all sectors that year, around the same time Synapse laid off 86 people, or about 40% of its staff.
- **Exposure to macro conditions** — an earlier round of Synapse layoffs (18% of its workforce, per a June blog post from CEO Sankaet Pathak) was attributed to "the current macroeconomic conditions" affecting its clients and platforms, indicating BaaS providers' fortunes are tied to the health of the banks/fintechs building on top of them.

## Background (external)

Wikipedia's entry on Banking-as-a-Service defines it more generally: BaaS refers to offering banking products — like deposit accounts, loans, and credit cards — to customers through partnerships with non-bank or non-financial third parties, where the bank handles balance-sheet management (capital, liquidity, credit risk) while the partner, often a fintech, engages the customer directly through its own app, typically connecting to the bank's systems via APIs.
