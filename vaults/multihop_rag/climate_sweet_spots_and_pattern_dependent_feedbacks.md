# Climate Sweet Spots and Pattern-Dependent Feedbacks

Global mean radiative feedbacks depend not only on global mean temperature but on the spatial pattern of surface warming: a degree of global warming spread out evenly will cause a different radiative response than if that same warming were concentrated in a climate sweet spot — a location where surface warming produces efficient radiative damping.

For decades, researchers assumed that global mean radiative feedbacks mostly depend on global mean temperature (Gregory et al., 2004). The article's analogy is that applying a force uniformly over someone's entire body elicits a very different reaction than tickling the soles of that person's feet. A wide variety of processes affect the evolution of surface temperature, from greenhouse gas forcing and regional aerosol forcing to natural oscillations involving the ocean and atmosphere to the continental boundary conditions and the extent of ice sheets and sea ice, and the pattern of surface temperature change over the past 40 or so years featured a pronounced spatial structure, with some locations even cooling in spite of global mean warming on the order of 1 K. Feedbacks involving clouds and the atmospheric temperature structure are most sensitive to spatial differences in warming: deep convection in the warmest tropical regions readily communicates surface conditions upward throughout the troposphere (up to about 10-15 kilometers) and then horizontally across much of the globe, making the western Pacific a climate sweet spot. That warmer air sitting atop the relatively cool waters in the eastern Pacific or Southern Ocean acts to stabilize the lowermost troposphere, allowing more extensive low-lying stratus and stratocumulus clouds to develop, and because of their location and structure these low clouds efficiently cool the planet and offset some of the initial warming. Observations over recent decades show strong warming in regions of deep convection such as the western Pacific communicated throughout the troposphere, leading to strong warming aloft that enhances radiative emission to space, while parts of the eastern Pacific have cooled; warming aloft with cooling at the surface enhances lower tropospheric inversion strength in the eastern Pacific and promotes extensive shallow cloud cover that reflects solar radiation and keeps Earth relatively cool. Coupled climate models, by contrast, create their own surface warming pattern that differs from the observed one — warming aloft is less pronounced, lower tropospheric inversion strength is decreased and cloud cover is reduced through time, both of which lead to less efficient cooling near Earth's surface in models than in nature.
