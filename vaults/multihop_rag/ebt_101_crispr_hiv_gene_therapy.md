# EBT-101 CRISPR HIV Gene Therapy

EBT-101 is a gene therapy that uses CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to treat HIV, employing multiple guides to target multiple sites in the genome and snip out large sections of latently integrated HIV DNA, which stops HIV from replicating.

Scientists have been experimenting with how to use CRISPR to treat and potentially cure HIV since the gene-editing tool was developed, and the EBT-101 strategy has been studied in animal models since the development of CRISPR-Cas9 in 2012 — but the ongoing clinical trial, which tests whether just one dose of the therapy is safe in humans, is the first time such a gene-editing treatment for HIV has been tried in people. Using CRISPR for HIV gene therapy has shown promise in several test-tube studies, as a 2020 review in The Journal of Clinical Investigation notes, and various groups have been working to bring the therapies from test tubes to human patients. The multiple-guide design was described in a recent presentation at the annual meeting of the European Society for Gene and Cell Therapy in Brussels. Kamel Kahlili, a professor of neurovirology and gene editing at Temple University and co-founder of Excision BioTherapeutics, has been working with the company to develop EBT-101 for a decade; in 2020 and 2023 Khalili and his team published reports showing that EBT-100, a precursor to EBT-101, safely targeted and removed HIV DNA in infected primates. The early-stage human trial primarily focuses on safety, and initial results from three treated patients showed no toxic effects or serious adverse events, with all of the patients' HIV currently suppressed with cART. The latest data suggest EBT-101 is safe at the doses tested, but whether it cures HIV is not yet known.
