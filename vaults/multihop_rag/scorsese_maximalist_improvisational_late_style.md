# Scorsese's Maximalist, Improvisational Late Style

Where aging has meant settling for more routine and less challenging stories and shots on a tight production schedule for certain older filmmakers — Polygon names Clint Eastwood — Scorsese went in the other direction, into a maximalist, improvisational late style. The account is Polygon's, published 2023-10-26.

The Departed is the first case. From the outside it appears to be a cynical, domestic remake of a Hong Kong cops-and-robbers shootout, but Scorsese makes it his own and breathes bizarre life and energy into it: it contains the continuity errors, quick cutting, both rhythmic and arrhythmic needle bombs, and goofball humor his movies had not featured since Goodfellas. That earlier film essentially birthed an entire genre of filmic language, and The Departed goes a step beyond the original, being constantly rewritten and adjusted with the actors on the fly — exhilarating seat-of-the-pants filmmaking that Scorsese and his longtime creative partner, editor Thelma Schoonmaker, wrangled into a coherent film.

He brought the same cokey, maximalist energy to The Wolf of Wall Street, a pointed comedy as wildly, masterfully off the rails and improvisational as anything he has done, with Jonah Hill perhaps smuggling in a touch of Judd Apatow's "keep the camera rolling" ethos. Established-filmmaker confidence — and a touch of "eh, why not?" thinking — shows as DiCaprio's Ferrari changes color mid-narration during a highway blowjob, and when DiCaprio addresses the camera directly urging the audience not to worry about specific plot points. Polygon calls it a miracle that Scorsese and Schoonmaker produced a masterpiece out of the chaotic pieces of that production, again in post and with ample voice-over, with the shoot taking on something of a religious quality in the participants' retelling.

What grounds the style is the framing. Scorsese is telling a familiar story audiences have come to expect from him, about criminals and the pleasure they take in committing crimes and reaping the benefits; the brilliance is how he mines filmgoers' long relationship with his work, repurposing the style and tone of his gangster classics and inserting it into the world of finance. As he has done effectively since Casino, Scorsese weaponizes postmodernism, using viewers' presumed existing familiarity with his films as context — you do not get the full gale force of the film, its pleasures, or its overarching point without him reaching back to his past work and relying on viewers to recognize his voice in the storytelling.
