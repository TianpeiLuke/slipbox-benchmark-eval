# How Antibiotic Resistance Emerges and Spreads

Antibiotic resistance arises when drugs pressure bacteria to evolve defensive strategies that eject or disable the drugs or alter their targets, and it spreads both through random DNA mutations and through the swapping of "resistance genes" between bacteria in a process called horizontal gene transfer.

The starting point is how the drugs work. Antibiotics either directly kill bacteria or slow their growth, leaving the immune system to finish the job, acting in several ways — by preventing bacteria from building sturdy walls or making copies of their DNA, for instance — while growth-slowing antibiotics usually disrupt ribosomes, the factories in which bacterial cells make proteins. Many antibiotics shoot for the exact same molecular targets, and so-called broad-spectrum antibiotics' mechanisms are so universal that they work on both major classes of bacteria, gram-positive and gram-negative, which are distinguished by the makeup and thickness of their cell walls. Broad-spectrum antibiotics in particular pressure both harmful and helpful bacteria in the body to evolve those defences. Once defences exist, horizontal gene transfer lets bacteria quickly spread such mutations to additional bacterial populations in the body and in the environment; a CDC fact sheet on "How Resistance Moves Directly Germ to Germ," adapted by Live Science, illustrates the several ways drug-resistant bacteria transfer resistance to others. The misuse of antibiotics in health care as well as in agriculture has given bacteria endless opportunities to develop resistance, raising the chance that once-treatable infections will become life-threatening.
