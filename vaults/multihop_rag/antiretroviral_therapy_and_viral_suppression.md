# Antiretroviral Therapy and Viral Suppression

Combination antiretroviral therapy (ART), today's standard HIV treatment, uses several drugs to disrupt HIV's ability to replicate and invade immune cells, slashing the amount of HIV in a person's blood until it is undetectable; if that "viral suppression" is maintained it extends a person's life span to about that of HIV-negative people and eliminates their chance of spreading HIV via sex.

ART is given as daily pills or as monthly or bimonthly injections. "People living with HIV, on treatment and undetectable, are not infectious — full stop, end of statement — to their sexual partners," said Dr. Raphael Landovitz, co-director of UCLA's Center for HIV Identification, Prevention, and Treatment Services. Viral suppression also nearly eliminates HIV spread to babies during pregnancy or childbirth, greatly reduces spread via breastfeeding and likely lowers spread from sharing syringes. The first, imperfect HIV treatment, AZT (azidothymidine), was approved in 1987; nearly four decades and more than 40 million AIDS-related deaths later, a vaccine and a cure are still being hunted, but treatments have dramatically improved. "We've had really powerful treatments, really, since 1996, but they just get better all the time," said Dr. Monica Gandhi, director of the University of California, San Francisco Center for AIDS Research and medical director of the HIV Clinic at San Francisco General Hospital. The boundary of the concept is that combination antiretroviral therapies (cARTs) are the mainstay of HIV treatment and limit the virus' replication, extending lives to near-normal lengths and cutting the chance of transmission, but they must be taken for a lifetime and fall short of being a cure.
