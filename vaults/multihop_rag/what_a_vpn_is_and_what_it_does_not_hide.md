# What A VPN Is, And What It Does Not Hide

A virtual private network masks your IP address and the identity of your computer or mobile device on the network, creating an encrypted "tunnel" that prevents your internet service provider from accessing data about your browsing history. It is not a one-size-fits-all security solution — it is just one part of keeping your data private and secure.

The boundary of what it covers is narrow. A VPN only hides information like your IP address, location and browser history; a common misconception is that VPNs can make you totally invisible online, but the VPN provider often still has access to all of that information, so it does not grant total anonymity. You remain vulnerable to phishing attacks, hacking and other cyberthreats, which you should guard against with strong passwords and multi-factor authentication. Roya Ensafi, assistant professor of computer science and engineering at the University of Michigan, told Engadget that VPNs do not protect against common threats like phishing attacks, nor do they protect your data from being stolen: much of the data or information is stored with the VPN provider instead of your ISP, which means using a poorly designed or unprotected network can still undermine your security. What they are good for is online privacy when connecting to an untrusted network somewhere public, because they tunnel and encrypt your traffic to the next hop.

Marketing claims are therefore the wrong test. Sweeping claims like military-grade encryption or total digital invisibility may not be totally accurate; instead Yael Grauer, program manager of Consumer Reports' online security guide, recommends looking for security features such as open-source software with reproducible builds, up-to-date support for industry-standard protocols like WireGuard (Consumer Reports' preferred protocol) or IPsec, and the ability to defend against attack vectors like brute force.

Are they safe? Generally yes — VPNs are a safe and reliable way to encrypt and protect your internet data — but like most online services the safety specifics vary from provider to provider, and you can use third-party audits, Consumer Reports reviews, transparency reports and privacy policies to understand your chosen provider. Engadget's guide tested nine of the best VPN services available and landed on five top picks. An update dated November 10, 2024 removed a mention of PPTP, a protocol that Grauer notes "has serious security flaws."
