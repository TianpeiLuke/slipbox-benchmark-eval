# Musk's Position On Disinformation

Musk's answer to the EU's warning did not dispute the regulator's authority so much as the premise underneath it: that "disinformation" is a category anyone can independently arbitrate. Replying on X to journalist Glenn Greenwald — who had attacked the DSA as a "censorship law" that he claimed would be used to "punish X" — Musk avoided expressing the same trenchant criticism of the EU's approach and instead invited relativism, writing: "Let the public hear exactly what this disinformation consists of and decide for themselves."

That reply attacks the EU's inferential step rather than its legal one. The bloc's demand that X be "effective" against disinformation presupposes that false content can be identified as such by some competent party, so that a platform's failure to act on it is a measurable compliance gap. Musk's move was to sow doubt that disinformation is something that can be independently arbitrated at all, implying that fact-checking is merely a convenient exercise for targeting different opinions — essentially echoing Greenwald's position — by claiming that "many times" the "official fact-checker" has been found making false statements, before adding, for plausible deniability, the rhetorically empty offer that "Maybe this is the case here, maybe not". If no arbiter is trustworthy, the adjudication the EU's obligation depends on cannot be performed, and the public becomes the only judge.

TechCrunch treats this as of a piece with a wider mismatch. A rule of law clash between Musk and the EU had looked increasingly inevitable in recent months, and had indeed been predicted by some industry watchers since rumours of the billionaire's plan to take over Twitter emerged the previous year; asking Musk to be "effective" against disinformation, the publication wrote, is a bit like asking the sea to stop moving. Musk's extremely iterative — or just plain erratic and arbitrary — management style is so far from the responsive and responsible qualities the EU's rulebook demands of digital leaders that TechCrunch found it hard to see how the clash could end well for either side. But this is how the regulatory dance must go, and after the dance comes the denouement: if a DSA breach is confirmed, enforcement follows, and actual penalties might be a bit harder for Musk to troll.

A second exchange illustrates the practical cost of the position. Musk chipped into a conversation in which an X user had commented on a screengrab of an apparent exchange of threats between Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Khamenei, and an official Israeli government account in relation to the war, writing: "Amazing to see this exchange!" The irony, TechCrunch notes, is that Musk's destruction of Twitter's legacy verification of notable accounts means you can't, at a glance, be sure whether the exchange really happened — so the "decide for themselves" standard is being applied on a platform whose own authenticity signals he removed.
