# Lucashenko's Argument Against The Trope Of The Dying Race

Melissa Lucashenko's claim is that Aboriginal people have survived colonisation and that the "dying race" trope has finally begun to lose its grip. "That was really the impetus for this book, to say, 'This is what was, and we've survived it,'" she says of Edenglassie. Her grounds are about pressure and its recent easing: "There's a very strong economic and social and psychological drive in non-Aboriginal people to see us as, if not dead, then very diminished. Until probably 10 years ago, I would have said the trope of the dying race was, ironically, immortal. But now there's so much about us in the media all the time, and in the education system to an extent, most Australians have to recognise that Aboriginal people haven't died out."

Census data supports the point: the number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people has risen from 548,368 in 2011 to 649,171 in 2016, then up 25 per cent to 812,728 in 2021 — an increase attributed in large part to people feeling more comfortable identifying as Indigenous, and also to the growing popularity of genealogical research. Lucashenko's own reading of Aboriginality is not primarily about descent or appearance: she says there is a "whole demographic of people who have an Aboriginal ancestor, who sometimes think they're Aboriginal, but actually, regardless of skin colour, they're white people and they have to go on a very long and different journey if they're going to become Aboriginal", and skin colour "is not irrelevant but it's almost irrelevant". What matters is "the lens you see the world through" — she illustrates it with the myna bird in a native fig tree in West End, which should be full of fig birds, magpies, butcher birds, cat birds and parrots, but where there have been no cat birds and parrots for a hundred years.

The warrant tying her novel to the claim is formal as well as thematic: "F---ing up the simple binaries of black and white is a lot of what I'm about", and the book's dual time-stamp structure is an attempt to render in words what she has called the "double-vision" of Aboriginal people, the layering of the structures of modernity on the ancient landscape of their ancestors. Survival was, she stresses, achieved against the best efforts of some colonists — one of the historical events that inspired the book was the public hanging of the Indigenous warrior Dundalli, the last person publicly executed in Queensland, who was labelled on his death "one of the most guiltiest and most incorrigible of the aboriginal natives of this quarter" by The Brisbane Courier, evaded capture for 14 years, and was convicted in 1854 of two murders whose charges may have been trumped up.
