# E-E-A-T And The Shift Toward Institutional Authority

SEO Lily Ray's account of why Google results feel different from five or six years ago starts with Google's response to the disinformation panic around the 2016 election, which involved questioning the notion that the most reliable information could be chosen by a form of popularity — meaning how many links a site received from other sites. As a result the algorithm seemed to change its approach to links, especially for news and for sites offering legal, financial, or health advice, and instead paid more attention to what Google came to call E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness.

The relation holds over a restricted range rather than across all queries. Not every search query takes E-E-A-T into account; Google has described heightened concern over sites that could impact safety, happiness, and the ability to be an informed citizen. Within that range the effect is large: "E-E-A-T has had a pretty big impact on what types of results you see," Ray said, and her extensive research on how sites have fared under the new guidelines shows the pattern — Urban Dictionary, down; Mayo Clinic, up. Some people consider E-E-A-T part of what is making results better than ever; others see it as a form of censorship, disproportionately affecting right-wing perspectives.

The structural consequence is a reversal of Google's founding logic: for certain kinds of information Google had undone one of the fundamental elements that made its results appealing from the start, so that instead of wild-west crowdsourcing, search was often reinforcing institutional authority. This felt complicated at best. When it comes to health and wellness, for example, quackery is often in the eye of the beholder, and everyone knows someone who has struggled with the limits of Western medicine; so much of the original draw of the internet was the opportunity for outlier voices to be heard alongside established experts and elites. Looking back on all that had changed around what first attracted people to Google, from the introduction of ads to efforts to keep users within the universe of Google products, this seemed to be the last straw.

Ray herself is a 30-something jet-setter with black-line tattoos and an asymmetrical, dyed blonde pixie cut, caught for lunch in Brooklyn between speaking gigs in Chicago and Berlin, on a day when she was also managing a 35-person team at her digital marketing agency, posting multiple times an hour on social media, dog-sitting a Pomeranian whose "daddies" were at Burning Man, caring for her own mini Australian shepherd, and organizing a house party she expected to be late for because she first had to perform a rooftop DJ set at a different party.
