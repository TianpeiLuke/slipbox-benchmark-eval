# X's Bot Countermeasures

X's plan against bots is a layered procedure, not a single fee: the $1-per-year charge is one piece of a broader strategy that may also include payment verification, phone verification and ID verification, on top of the traditional bot-catching methods X already runs — heuristics and models that detect fake accounts and engagement. Director of engineering Eric Farraro set this out on X on 18 October 2023, answering what he called "a lot of cynical takes about the $1 'Not a Bot' feature and the verification program in general."

The steps, as Farraro described them, stack rather than replace one another. First, price the account: charging raises the cost of account creation, which today is pennies for a human or a script — "Increasing the cost to even $1 starts to make this process much more expensive," he explained. Second, add identity friction: "At X, we're exploring using payment & phone verification, as well as ID verification, as part of a larger strategy to fight bots." Third, keep the existing detection layer: "We of course use more traditional heuristics and models to detect fake accounts [and] engagement on the platform. These two things are not mutually exclusive." Musk described the same first step in cost terms: "Because a bot costs a fraction of a penny — call it a tenth of a penny — but even if it has to pay…a few dollars or something, the effective cost of bots is very high," and added that every time a bot creator wanted to make another bot, they would need another new payment method.

The procedure's preconditions are narrow. The plan to charge X new users $1 per year was at that point live only in the test markets of New Zealand and the Philippines, and it gates writing rather than reading: "Correct, read for free, but $1/year to write," Musk posted on October 18, 2023. It is the latest significant change to the network formerly known as Twitter since its acquisition by Musk, who has revamped its subscription offering, refocused efforts on supporting creators and rebranded the network as "X," as part of a broader plan to turn it into an everything app encompassing text, live and recorded audio and video calling, payments and more.

Both men are explicit about where the procedure does not reach. Farraro's stated goal was "to make it difficult and expensive enough that it's less and less viable," as opposed to stopping the bot problem outright. Musk's version: the fee "won't stop bots completely, but it will be 1000x harder to manipulate the platform" — "It's the only way to fight bots without blocking real users." Farraro also gave the reason the detection layer must keep evolving: within a matter of years, AI will be able to mimic human interactions by doing things like solving CAPTCHAs and generating photos and videos that will be "undetectable by human or AI countermeasures." His framing question was, "If you can avoid getting identified as a bot, why can't an intelligent AI do the same?" He concluded that other networks would likely follow suit in the years ahead.

The plan matches what Musk had said earlier. He had told Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu the previous month that X would be "moving to a small monthly payment" for use of the X system — "It's the only way I can think of to combat vast armies of bots" — in a conversation that had largely focused on AI tech, regulation and hate speech, in the wake of Musk's spat with the Anti-Defamation League, which had accused both Musk and X of being antisemitic. Combating bots and spam is something Musk complained about repeatedly even before acquiring the social network, though critics including WordPress founder Matt Mullenweg have suggested that fees alone will not stymie the efforts of determined spammers.
