# FTX's Spending Served Bankman-Fried's Status, Not Philanthropy

The Verge's reporter argued that at every turn the scams at FTX and Alameda led back to making Bankman-Fried more important — not to giving money away, as his stated Effective Altruism rationale claimed. The grounds are the pattern of what the money bought: Singh testified to more than a billion dollars spent on celebrity endorsements, which made FTX very famous even though the futures platform did not operate in the US; the political donations gave Bankman-Fried influence in the highest echelons of power; and Ellison testified to venture investments that gave him more dominance in the tech world.

The warrant joining the pattern to the conclusion is that spending reveals priorities better than statements do: it is easy to lie with words and harder to lie with money. Bankman-Fried went around telling people he wanted to make a lot of money in order to give it away, an idea of the Effective Altruism movement he affiliated himself with, but the amount he funneled to his Paper Bird vehicle alone was ten times the size of his philanthropic spending as outlined by the prosecution's expert Peter Easton — and all of it was vastly outweighed by his investments, the places where he could prove he was very smart by investing in the right companies early.
