# The Flexport Forecast Dispute And The Contested Whistleblower Claim

A source close to Petersen told CNBC that a mid-August meeting in Texas went poorly for Clark because a so-called whistleblower — a senior finance executive — stepped forward shortly beforehand and told Petersen that the numbers being presented were "not real"; CNBC's reporting attacks the factual premise of that account, since documents seen by CNBC and conversations with people with direct knowledge of the board meeting make it clear that there were no substantiated whistleblower actions or allegations of financial impropriety.

The underlying dispute was about forecasts, not fraud. For much of the summer Clark had pushed then-CFO Kenny Wagers and his financial planning and analysis team to realign Flexport's year-end and 18-month forecasts, for obvious reasons: at the beginning of 2022 it cost around $14,500 to move a single container across the Pacific, and by late 2022 prices of ocean freight from Asia to the U.S. West Coast were down 90% from a year earlier due largely to weakening global demand — and because Flexport makes money charging fees for transporting goods, its business was getting hammered. But Wagers and Stuart Leung, a Flexport finance executive and close Petersen ally, were reluctant to pare back forecasts, frustrating Clark, who felt the projections were overly optimistic; Wagers and Leung did not respond to CNBC's interview requests. Clark ultimately prevailed, but the revised forecasts distressed Petersen, and Clark, Petersen and Wagers met in Texas in mid-August to fine-tune them. Flexport's spokesperson told CNBC: "There was no whistleblower nor was there any financial misconduct. Any allegations to the contrary are completely false." On Sept. 15, shortly after CNBC spoke with the Petersen source, legal counsel for Clark sent a cease-and-desist letter to Flexport instructing the company to preserve and retain all communications involving Clark's departure; the letter disputes the existence of a whistleblower and lists specific allegations as false and defamatory, including Petersen's claims that Clark was an unfit CEO because he overextended the company's lease obligations. Five hours after the letter was sent, the source close to Petersen contacted CNBC and asked to retract their statements and all details related to Clark's firing or the so-called whistleblower; CNBC declined to retract the statements. Petersen has since deleted several of his posts criticizing Clark.
