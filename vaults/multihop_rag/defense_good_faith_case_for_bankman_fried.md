# The Defense's Good-Faith Case For Bankman-Fried

Mark Cohen, one of Sam Bankman-Fried's lawyers, attacked the prosecution's central premise that his client was a liar who stole from customers, stating early in his opening statement that "Sam didn't defraud anyone." The defense's counter-picture is of a nerdy startup founder who acted in "good faith," not the prosecution's "cartoon of a villain"; Cohen repeatedly harped on Bankman-Fried's allegedly good-faith actions throughout his address to the jury. Bankman-Fried, whose cheekbones were more prominent after spending about seven weeks in a Brooklyn prison, appeared in court as this argument was made.

Cohen also disputed the specific claim that Alameda Research was a covert conduit. Alameda was not subterranean or shady, he said; it was a successful hedge fund. FTX was no Ponzi scheme but a "very innovative, successful company." The business practices between the two were reasonable, he argued, claiming that Alameda acted legally as an FTX customer, payment processor, and market maker — that is, a financial entity that acts as a trading partner for customers looking to buy and sell cryptocurrencies.

The defense's warrant for reading failure as something other than fraud was an analogy Cohen employed throughout: "working at a startup is like building a plane as you're flying it," and businesses sometimes fail. He specifically pointed the finger at Ellison, the former CEO of Alameda, who he said did not adequately protect her hedge fund from the inherent risk of the crypto markets. And when the walls came closing in and the aforementioned plane approached the "eye of the storm," Cohen argued, Bankman-Fried did not act like someone who was guilty — he was willing to give up his personal wealth to make customers whole. "In the end, Sam started and built two billion-dollar businesses," he concluded. "He didn't steal any money."
