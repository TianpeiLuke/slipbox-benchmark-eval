# The Cambrian Explosion and the Rise of Oxygen

The Cambrian explosion — the remarkable diversification of life in the first period of the Paleozoic, in which most of the life that exists today emerged within about 10 million years, including the first complex multicellular life and familiar groups such as mollusks and crustaceans — is attributed in part to a sustained rise in oxygen from photosynthetic life.

The sequence begins in the late Proterozoic eon, around 550 million years ago, when Earth had thawed out from a series of major glaciations, perhaps even global snowball conditions; the end of the Proterozoic marked the beginning of the Paleozoic era, which extended from around 540 million to 251 million years ago. Higher levels of oxygen allowed for more diverse, multicellular organisms because such organisms require oxygen in high enough concentrations that it can reach interior cells. The stratospheric ozone layer, which had developed during the Neoproterozoic era (1 billion to 538 million years ago), protected animals from the sun's damaging ultraviolet rays and helped populate the land. Some researchers even argue for a possible "bottleneck" effect, in which the few life-forms that survived the Neoproterozoic ice ages — Snowball Earth or not — were able to rapidly fill emerging niches as Earth thawed. The ancient supercontinent of Gondwana had formed around 600 million years ago.
