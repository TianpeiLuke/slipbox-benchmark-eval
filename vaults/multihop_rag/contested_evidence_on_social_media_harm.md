# The Contested Evidence On Social Media Harm

Buck records a reasonable counter-discussion about how many of our problems can really be laid at the feet of social media: the research on the impacts of social media on political dysfunction, mental health, and society writ large does not paint a neat portrait.

The objection attacks the causal premise rather than the description of the media ecology. Scholars have argued that putting too much emphasis on the platforms can be too simplistic and reeks of technological determinism, and have also pointed out that cultures like the United States' and the legacy media have a long history with post-truth.

That literature sits alongside a substantial body of work outlining the contours and dynamics of the current media ecology and what it does: Siva Vaidhyanathan's Antisocial Media, Safiya U. Noble's Algorithms of Oppression, Geert Lovink's Sad by Design, Shoshana Zuboff's Surveillance Capitalism, Richard Seymour's The Twittering Machine, Tim Hwang's Subprime Attention Crisis, Tressie McMillan Cottom's writing on how to understand the social relations of Internet technologies through racial capitalism, and many more. Buck's own position concedes the uncertainty without abandoning the concern: there are certainly dynamics going on that we did not anticipate, and we do not seem quite sure what to do with them, even with multiple areas of scholarship in communication, disinformation, and social media and democracy working on these inquiries for years.
