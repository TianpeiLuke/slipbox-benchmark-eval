# The Mechanics Of Connor Bedard's Shot

Connor Bedard's shot differs from the standard NHL release in the geometry of how the puck is drawn in: where almost all players point their toes at the net, set the puck at a right angle, load the shot and "drag it in and then snap it off" on a line of 40 to 50 degrees behind their body or at least level with their heels, Bedard brings the puck in "completely lateral," as shooting coach Tim Turk described to The New York Times on 17 November 2023.

The structural relations are stated in terms of angle and space. Instead of drawing the puck in at a 45-degree angle, Bedard drew it at 180 degrees "right across to the plane of his spine," and sometimes it even came in at a negative angle; it also happened in a smaller space than for other shooters, with that lateral inward pull "condensed — it's compacted." Turk's scale comparison: Vladimir Tarasenko, whom Turk calls one of the NHL's quickest shooters, takes 18 inches to pull the puck in, while Bedard did it all in 12 square inches — a one-foot box. His reaction was "I was like holy f—," he said, spelling out the whole profanity, "because the only other person that I know who does it quite like that is No. 34 in Toronto (Auston Matthews), and he still doesn't bring it in as lateral as Bedzy does."

Two further relations extend the model beyond geometry. The first is concealment: the shot is hard to defend because he doesn't show he's preparing for it. "As replicated in so many other areas of Connor's game, it's the deception and elite multi-tasking that catches opponents off guard," Nick Quinn said. "Connor's ability to create deception and change the shot angle at top speed is like very few I've ever seen. The multi-tasking involved with attempting these shots at top speed is far beyond most player's capabilities." The second is the body chain from hands to legs, described by another shooting coach who requested anonymity because he works for another NHL club: "What's really interesting is how high and left he can get his top hand. So many players pull the puck in but can't get the puck to release from under their body. His footwork is so underrated in that aspect. If you watch his front leg, at times it's literally in the air at release — most have their back leg in the air at release. When he transfers his weight, he actually clears space for his hands to get tighter because he's not afraid to actually lunge into the shot." The article's framing is that The Athletic spoke with five NHL shooting and skills coaches, his teammates, past opponents and Bedard himself to take apart, piece by piece, "The Bedard" — the nerdy science of it from his feet up to his knees, hips, hands and head, and what it looks like to goalies and to the coaches who watched him after he taught himself.
