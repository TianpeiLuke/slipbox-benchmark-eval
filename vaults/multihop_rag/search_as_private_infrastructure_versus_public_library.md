# Search As Private Infrastructure, Not A Public Library

The claim is that routing nearly all online information through a single private company has left us arguably worse off than before, because information that is controlled by a private business will always be subject to that business's bottom line — which is exactly why most countries around the world have libraries that are public institutions.

The grounds are a comparison with what the internet was supposed to be. In the beginning it was seen as an improvement on the spirit of the public library: an opportunity to transcend the gatekeepers controlling who could publish a book, allowing mankind to fully connect and share knowledge. Instead, nearly all online information runs through a single company, which assumes a veneer of civic utility, of impassive authority, when it is very much not a neutral entity.

Matt Cutts supplies the insider warrant that the veneer is doing work. "There were so many true believers at Google in the early days," he said. "As companies get big, it gets harder to get things done. Inevitably, people start to think about profit or quarterly numbers." He claimed that, at least while he was there, search quality always came before financial goals, but he believes the public underestimates how Google is shaping what they see: "I deeply, deeply, deeply believe search engines are newspaper-like entities, making editorial decisions." He speculated that the company did not want the public to think too hard about how search works because that awareness "encourages regulators and makes people realize, 'Oh, there's a lot of money here.'"

The consequence is epistemic rather than commercial. There has always been advertising and polemics from cranks, scammers, and liars, but now we see this stuff surfacing alongside truth and we cannot tell the difference, so we move through our lives with a greater sense of distrust and fear and insecurity. At the end of the day, it is Google's world, and the SEOs are only living in it.
