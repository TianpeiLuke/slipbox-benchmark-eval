# Allison Miller's Influenza, Sepsis And Leg Amputation

Allison Miller, a Virginia-based communications director who was a healthy 33-year-old at the time, lost her left leg above the knee after a case of influenza escalated into bilateral bacterial pneumonia, sepsis and septic shock; a decade later she described the sequence to Fox News Digital for an article published on October 18, 2023, which noted that this year marked ten years since those complications caused her life-changing loss.

The illness began with a sore throat and headache. When her symptoms got worse she went into urgent care for a chest X-ray, but it showed nothing alarming. "It looked like it was just some sort of run-of-the-mill stuff -- they gave me some prescription cough syrup and said to let them know if it got worse," she said. Back at home that night she started feeling worse and began having intense back pain. "Thinking it was the flu or something I could get over, I kept waiting to turn the corner -- like, this is the worst of it. It'll get better. And clearly that wasn't the case," she said. The next morning, when the back pain was causing her to have periodic blackouts, she managed to call a friend, who came over and called an ambulance. That is where her memory stops: "I remember being loaded into the ambulance -- and then I really don't remember a whole lot after that for about three weeks to a month thereafter. It just escalated very quickly."

As Miller would later learn, her influenza had caused bilateral bacterial pneumonia, which affects both lungs and is more severe than viral pneumonia. That led to sepsis, which quickly turned to septic shock. "All of my organs were shutting down," she said. "Within a short amount of time, I was in the ICU and they were doing everything they could. But ultimately, my last best chance was to be put on life support."

After five days on life support she experienced a rare complication. "A known complication of that form of life support is amputation," she said. "Some patients lose blood circulation, and that's what happened to me." Doctors ultimately had to amputate her left leg above the knee while she was still in a medically induced coma. "By the time I came out of it, it had been three weeks. I awoke in a very hazy position -- and I didn't have a left leg," she recalled. She spent another two months in the hospital and then had to do weeks of inpatient rehabilitation.
