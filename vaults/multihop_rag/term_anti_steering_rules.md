# Anti-Steering Rules

## Definition

Anti-steering rules, as the corpus uses the term, are restrictions that PayPal — and, earlier, Visa and Mastercard — impose on the merchants who accept their payment methods, alleged in a 2023 consumer class action to stifle competition from lower-cost payment platforms such as Stripe and Shopify.

## Context

The corpus introduces the term through a 2023 class-action lawsuit against PayPal: consumers represented by law firm Hagens Berman allege that PayPal's anti-steering rules stifle competition against lower-cost payment platforms such as Stripe and Shopify, and that PayPal has subjected consumers to excess charges when purchasing from merchants that accept PayPal or Venmo. The attorneys who filed the suit placed PayPal's rules in a lineage going back to the card networks: "Visa and MasterCard once imposed similar anti-steering rules on merchants accepting their cards but, after the Justice Department sued the networks for antitrust violations, they agreed in 2010 to eliminate their anti-steering rules as part of the settlement. With payments transitioning into the digital realm, PayPal has simply ripped a page right from the Visa and MasterCard [sic] playbook." Antitrust litigator Patrick McGahan framed the underlying tension as structural rather than PayPal-specific: platform companies that act as gatekeepers in a market will keep drawing antitrust suits over the fees they charge, and PayPal can be expected to respond that its own fees are themselves driven by the card schemes (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) that set the terms it operates under.

## Key Characteristics

- **Merchant-facing restriction** — the rule binds merchants who accept a given payment method, not consumers directly, by limiting the merchant's ability to steer buyers elsewhere.
- **Fee insulation** — the effect alleged is that the rules stifle competition from lower-cost platforms, letting the rule-setter avoid price competition on its fees.
- **Precedent in card networks** — Visa and Mastercard imposed similar anti-steering rules on merchants before agreeing, in a 2010 Justice Department antitrust settlement, to eliminate them.
- **Migrates across payment eras** — the lawsuit's framing treats PayPal's digital-era rules as a continuation of the same practice the card networks used, rather than a new phenomenon.
- **Tied to gatekeeper status** — commentary on the suit links anti-steering rules to a platform's position as a fee-charging gatekeeper between merchants and payment systems, and to who bears responsibility when fees are themselves imposed further up the chain (e.g., by card schemes).

## Background (external)

Outside the corpus, the U.S. Supreme Court examined the same kind of restriction in the 2018 antitrust case *Ohio v. American Express Co.*: American Express's merchant contracts contained anti-steering provisions that "prohibit merchants from avoiding fees by discouraging customers' American Express card use at the point of sale," and the Court held, 5–4 in an opinion by Justice Thomas decided June 25, 2018, that these provisions did not violate federal antitrust law.
