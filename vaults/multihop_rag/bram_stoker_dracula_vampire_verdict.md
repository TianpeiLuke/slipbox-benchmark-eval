# Bram Stoker: Vampire Verdict

Polygon's verdict on Bram Stoker, author of Dracula (1897), is: vampire.

The context is the novel's importance. The real breakthrough moment for vampires in the English-speaking world came just before the turn of the 20th century with Dracula, whose story of a Transylvanian count taking residence in the U.K. to launch a centuries-old plan of vampiric conquest and enslavement became the source of pastiches, spinoffs, and cliches almost from the moment it arrived — codifying the basic elements of vampire storytelling for the next half-century or more. Those elements: the foreign, undead aristocrat whose cosmopolitan charm and suave allure stood in for English fears and paranoias about the allure of other nations and races; the bloodsucking conversion process, targeted invariably at either women or the more effeminate among the male set, that reads alarmingly like seduction; and the vampire hunters' tricks and traps, from wooden stakes to garlic to sunlight to good old simple beheadings. The grounds for the verdict lie in Stoker's biography, which Polygon calls almost the inverse of his noble-born immortal count yet fittingly enigmatic: born to a middle-class Protestant Irish family in the 1840s, his life grew progressively more bizarre, including a keen interest in the occult studies of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and in the history and practices of the Freemasons. Cultivating a public reputation, or seeking inspiration for a never-materialized new hit story, are offered as innocent readings — but it is just possible, the piece concludes, that somewhere along the way Stoker had acquired a membership to the most elite secret society of all.
