# The Post-COVID Parts Scramble As A Breeding Ground For AOG's Growth

The end of lockdowns triggered a resurgence in international travel and a rush to secure jets and parts amid backlogs in factories — a parts scramble that Steve Borrowdale said would be a perfect breeding ground for an unscrupulous seller, because "Since COVID, raw materials are increasingly difficult to get hold of."

AOG's own trajectory tracks the same period. By early 2020 the company was worth £2.2 million, and a breakthrough came when it won a deal with logistics firm B&H Worldwide to distribute parts from its hub in Frankfurt: "The two companies have previously worked together for exports to Europe and the US from the UK, but this is the first time they have partnered in mainland Europe," the pair said in a 2020 press release. According to the filings, AOG's cash bonanza came before the COVID-19 lockdowns of that year; the pandemic grounded flights and was a disaster for the aviation industry, but the recovery that followed created the scarcity. The structural weakness sits in the supply chain itself: parts flow through a dazzling patchwork of thousands of often small manufacturers, brokers and distributors, while manufacturers and operators are heavily regulated and largely responsible for orchestrating the paper trail that follows every aviation part, from computers and fuselage panels to screw housings and washers. The clean-up scale is large — CFM has had to audit more than 500 suppliers to try to find out what items could be caught up in the crisis. The parts identified so far are not thought to include the most sensitive components, known as life-limited parts, which operate under the most stress in a jet engine and are carefully monitored by manufacturers while operating on the plane; however, even dodgy fastenings have claimed lives.
