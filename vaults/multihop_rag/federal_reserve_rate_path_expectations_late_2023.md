# Federal Reserve Rate Path Expectations Late 2023

By early December 2023 traders widely expected the Federal Reserve to hold its key interest rate steady at its meeting the following week before potentially cutting rates in March, according to data from CME Group, and Fed officials had recently hinted that the federal funds rate may already be at its peak, above 5.25 per cent, up from nearly zero early in 2022. Fed Chair Jerome Powell and others nevertheless warned Wall Street against being overzealous in its predictions about how early a cut could happen.

The bond market moved with those expectations. With inflation down from its peak two summers earlier, Wall Street was hopeful the Fed might be done with its market-shaking hikes. Investors were looking for a slowdown in the job market that came through employers cutting back on open positions rather than laying off workers, and the report cited showed employers advertised just 8.7 million jobs on the last day of October, down 617,000 from a month earlier. US Treasury yields continued to sag from the heights reached during late October: the 10-year yield fell to 4.18 per cent from 4.26 per cent late on the Monday, after having been above 5 per cent and at its highest level in more than a decade during October. The two-year Treasury yield, which more closely tracks Fed expectations, fell from 4.61 per cent just before the economic reports to 4.57 per cent, yo-yoed, and then eased back to 4.57 per cent. Lower yields were one reason cryptocurrency prices had been rising, alongside excitement about a possible bitcoin exchange-traded fund that helped send bitcoin above $US43,000 ($65,600); Robinhood Markets reported a roughly 75 per cent jump in crypto trading volumes during November from a month earlier, said customers added about $US1.4 billion in net deposits during the month, and its own stock rose 10.3 per cent.

A month earlier, in early November 2023, the same expectations were forming from a lower base. Before that week, stocks had been struggling under the weight of rapidly rising Treasury yields catching up to the Fed's main interest rate, which was above 5.25 per cent and at its highest level since 2001. Higher rates and yields slow the economy, hurt investment prices and raise the risk of something breaking in the financial system, such as the three high-profile US bank failures that rattled markets during the spring. "It was really fear that the Fed was going to go too far," said Katie Nixon, chief investment officer for Northern Trust Wealth Management. The Fed had applied that pressure intentionally, hoping to starve inflation of its fuel and to cool the job market, particularly pay raises, because it feared too-strong pay gains could create a vicious cycle keeping inflation high. Analysts said the Friday jobs report offered encouraging signals, with average hourly earnings rising less in October from September than expected, though it did not mean the job was done. Treasury yields tumbled immediately after that report, with the 10-year easing to 4.52 per cent from 4.67 per cent late Thursday and from more than five per cent the previous week, when it hit its highest level since 2007. Traders then moved up expectations for the first Fed cut, potentially by the summer, according to CME Group data. Foreign-exchange strategists at Bank of America wrote in a BofA Global Research report that for investors around the world the "Fed matters more than other central banks" and weak US data is "the only game-changer for markets". A separate report that Friday from the Institute for Supply Management said growth in US services industries such as finance and construction was weaker than economists expected and suggested a slight easing in prices.
