# Nations League Prestige And The Congested International Cycle

Jonathan Liew argues that what England lacked most was clarity — of selection, of formation, of mission and motivation — because the status of the Nations League itself was never settled, and that the post-pandemic international calendar is a kind of sadism dressed up as a panorama of opportunity.

The grounds for the first claim are the unresolved question the team was left to answer on the hoof: how much prestige should really be attached to the Nations League, and is it primarily an Olympic qualification shootout or something worth winning in its own right? The supporting evidence is that it was only when England's options had finally clarified down to one — survival or oblivion — that they recovered their poise and verve. The grounds for the second claim are the schedule itself: a Euros, a World Cup, an Olympics and another Euros squeezed into three years. The warrant joining them is that clarity and recovery both need space the calendar does not give, which is why there is a school of thought that a fallow summer might not have been the worst thing for this team — time to reflect and process, time to breathe, time to refresh. Instead they travelled to Glasgow the following Tuesday for one last push, with minds still frazzled and bodies screaming, though chaos and confusion had never felt quite so sweet.
