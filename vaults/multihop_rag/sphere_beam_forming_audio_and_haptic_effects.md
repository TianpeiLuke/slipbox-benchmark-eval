# Sphere's Beam-Forming Audio And Haptic Effects

The Sydney Morning Herald's 19 October 2023 report describes Sphere's sensory system as three layers stacked behind the screens: beam-formed audio that gives every seat its own feed, haptics that turn sound into seat-shaking vibration and felt pressure waves, and — in cinema mode — artificial wind and scents paired to key moments.

The audio layer inverts the usual arena arrangement. The screens are a bespoke assembly of state-of-the-art screens, but behind them are "a whole new kind of speaker", Lipson says: "In a regular show, the speakers are at one end pushing the sound down the room, so it's louder at the stage end and quieter further away. [But here] because everybody's sort of getting their own feed, everybody's getting an equal volume. So hearing the show in there, sonically, may be better than anywhere." When the venue is in cinema mode it uses the full range of haptics — shaping sound into vibrations that literally shake the seat and into waves of pressure that can be felt — and to those the haptic systems add artificial wind and a range of scents, paired to moments such as rocket launches or wild storms.

The range over which this holds is bounded by intent and by time. "Both [the U2 and ABBA] shows are taking a bold stab at the next-generation way of doing shows," Lipson says, but the traditional blueprint of iconic artist and audience remains key: "I'm sitting in a regular arena right now with Madonna, we are doing a very exciting show that pushes the limits of a pop show balancing staging and audience, and we are doing things that will feel fresh for her audience and for the show." Critically, he says, the staging for the U2 show is elementary in real terms despite its technological complexity, and "the Sphere, like ABBA Arena, is only touching the sides of what you can do. In five years' time and 10 years' time, two years' time, whenever the technology shifts, you'll be able to do more."
