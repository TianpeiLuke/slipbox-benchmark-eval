# Board Governance

In this corpus, board governance is illustrated by cases where a board of directors exercises (or conspicuously lacks) the power to oversee and remove a company's CEO: OpenAI's board firing Sam Altman, Flexport's board ousting Dave Clark, and FTX's absence of any board at all.

## Context

The corpus's clearest working example is OpenAI's board, which in November 2023 fired co-founder and CEO Sam Altman after "a deliberative review process" concluded he was "not consistently candid in his communications with the board, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities."
That framing treats board governance as a standing check on the CEO: the board's stated justification was not a business or legal failure but a breakdown in the information flow the board needs to do its job.
OpenAI's board itself sits atop a nonprofit structure that "preserved its nonprofit governance structure" even as the startup underneath pursued commercial growth, so the board's authority over the CEO ran through that nonprofit layer rather than ordinary shareholder control.
Board composition determines whose judgment gets exercised: OpenAI's board consisted of chief scientist Ilya Sutskever and three non-employee directors (Adam D'Angelo, Tasha McCauley, and Helen Toner), and the subsequent standoff over reinstating Altman turned on negotiating a new board (Bret Taylor, Larry Summers, Adam D'Angelo) as a condition of his return.
The corpus also frames board governance by its absence: Paradigm co-founder Matt Huang testified he was "concerned about FTX's lack of a board of directors" before investing anyway, though on cross-examination he said Bankman-Fried told him he didn't want investors as directors but did plan on having a board with experts.
Flexport's board, by contrast, illustrates governance operating through composition rather than absence: a board "made up largely of investors who were betting on the founder" held quarterly meetings on June 1 and Aug. 31 before Clark was forced to resign on Sept. 5.

## Key Characteristics

- **Power to remove or reinstate the CEO** — OpenAI's board fired Altman outright, then negotiated the terms (including its own reconstitution) under which he could return.
- **Grounded in a formal duty to oversee, not just advise** — the board's justification for firing Altman was specifically that his conduct hindered "its ability to exercise its responsibilities," i.e., an oversight duty rather than a performance complaint.
- **Structure shapes authority** — OpenAI's nonprofit board sat above the commercial subsidiary, giving it authority that ordinary shareholder-elected boards would not have over a purely for-profit company.
- **Composition determines outcome** — who sits on the board (employee vs. non-employee directors, investor-aligned vs. independent) is itself a lever contested during crises, as seen in both the OpenAI reinstatement talks and Flexport's investor-heavy board.
- **Its absence is itself notable** — the corpus flags FTX's lack of a board of directors as a specific governance gap investors identified before FTX's collapse, distinguishing "no oversight body" from "an oversight body that failed."

## Background (external)

Outside this corpus, corporate governance is generally defined as the mechanisms, processes, and relations by which corporations are directed and controlled by their boards of directors, managers, shareholders, and other stakeholders.
The widely cited Cadbury Report formulation puts it more simply: corporate governance is "the system by which companies are directed and controlled."
