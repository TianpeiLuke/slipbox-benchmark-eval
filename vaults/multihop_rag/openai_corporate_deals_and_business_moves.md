# OpenAI Corporate Deals And Business Moves

Alongside its product releases, OpenAI made a series of corporate moves in 2023: a news-content partnership with Axel Springer, an indemnity promise to business customers over copyright claims, its first acquisition, a $300 million share sale, and a stalled attempt to trademark "GPT."

OpenAI struck a deal with the Berlin-based news publisher Axel Springer, which owns Business Insider and Politico, to "help provide people with new ways to access quality, real-time news content through our AI tools"; under it OpenAI will train its generative AI models on the publisher's content and add recent Axel Springer-published articles to ChatGPT. Bowing to peer pressure, OpenAI also said it would pay legal costs incurred by customers who face lawsuits over IP claims against work generated by an OpenAI tool, though the protections seemingly do not extend to all OpenAI products, such as the free and Plus tiers of ChatGPT. In its first public acquisition in its seven-year history, OpenAI acquired Global Illumination, a New York-based startup leveraging AI to build creative tools, infrastructure and digital experiences; OpenAI wrote in a brief blog post, "We're very excited for the impact they'll have here at OpenAI. The entire team has joined OpenAI to work on our core products including ChatGPT."

On financing, OpenAI closed a $300 million share sale at a $27 billion to $29 billion valuation, with VC firms including Sequoia Capital, Andreessen Horowitz, Thrive and K2 Global picking up new shares according to documents seen by TechCrunch, and a source saying Founders Fund was also investing. That round was separate from a large Microsoft investment announced earlier in the year, which closed in January and is believed to be around $10 billion. On trademarks, OpenAI applied for a trademark for "GPT" — Generative Pre-trained Transformer — in December, and petitioned the USPTO to speed up the process, citing the "myriad infringements and counterfeit apps" beginning to spring into existence; the petition was dismissed because OpenAI's attorneys neglected to pay an associated fee and to provide "appropriate documentary evidence supporting the justification of special action," meaning a decision could take up to five more months.
