# YouTube Content ID as Blueprint for AI Rights Management

Robert Kyncl's model treats the solutions built for user-generated content as a "blueprint" that can be applied to AI-generated content on platforms. The analogy has three parts: the explosion of AI use among the public maps onto the explosion of user-generated content (UGC) a decade or two ago; just as UGC often infringed copyright, AI threatens the rights of artists and music rights holders; and therefore the same class of remedy should apply.

The remedy he describes is YouTube's Content ID. "When YouTube was formed… people started to upload content, including copyrighted material, which, obviously, put YouTube into hot water with lots of different copyright holders. And I had the privilege of working through a lot of that and fix it up. But we made a very important decision, which was to go above and beyond the law, and build a fingerprinting software that allowed us to track the copyright on our platform, and then have commercial relationship[s] with copyright holders to send them the money. Out of that we built a multi-billion-dollar business, which now is a multi-billion-dollar business per year. And it was an incredible new revenue stream for everyone. AI is that with new super tools." Content ID scours uploaded videos for copyrighted content, video and audio, then offers the copyright owner the option to monetise that video or to request that it be taken down — a system that has essentially allowed YouTube users to upload content without paying much attention to copyright while ensuring copyright owners are paid.

Two conditions bound the model. First, the same thoughtfulness must be applied to AI as was applied to UGC, and "we have to make sure that artists have a choice" — detection alone is not the point; owner election between monetisation and takedown is. Second, refusal is not available as a strategy: "You have to embrace technology, because it's not like you can put technology in a bottle. The genie is not going back."
