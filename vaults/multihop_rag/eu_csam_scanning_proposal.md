# The EU CSAM Scanning Proposal

The EU CSAM-scanning proposal is a controversial proposed law on the scanning of child sexual abuse material spearheaded by the European Union's home affairs commissioner, Ylva Johansson, whose final shape is still being determined by the European Council and MEPs as co-legislators — and which, as of TechCrunch's report of 25 October 2023, had become the subject of a Commission investigation into whether its own promotional advertising broke EU digital rules.

The immediate occasion for that definition is an admission. Johansson confirmed the Commission is investigating whether or not it broke recently updated digital governance rules when her department ran a microtargeted political ad campaign aiming to drive support for the proposal. At a committee hearing in the European Parliament that day she deflected MEPs' enquiries for her to give more details about the ad campaign.

The rule in question is the Digital Services Act, which includes provisions relating to online advertising — among them a prohibition on the use of sensitive personal data, such as political opinions, for targeting ads. The ads in question ran on X (formerly Twitter), which is already expected to be compliant with the DSA, having been designated by the Commission as a so-called Very Large Online Platform (VLOP) back in April.

The boundary case that makes the proposal distinctive is institutional rather than technical: the Commission itself not only proposed the DSA but is responsible for oversight of VLOPs' DSA compliance. If EU officials have used X's ad-targeting tools to break the bloc's own digital rulebook, it is the very definition of an awkward situation — the author, the enforcer and the suspected infringer being the same institution.

The campaign that triggered the investigation was found from outside. Its existence was spotted the previous month by technologist Danny Mekić, and an article with his findings ran in the Dutch newspaper De Volkskrant earlier in October 2023. Using the public ad transparency tools the DSA requires VLOPs to provide, Mekić found the Commission had run a paid advertising campaign on X targeting users in the Netherlands, Sweden, Belgium, Finland, Slovenia, Portugal and the Czech Republic — countries that were not supportive of Johansson's CSAM-scanning proposal according to leaked minutes from a September 14 meeting of the European Council, a co-legislative body that is involved, along with MEPs, in determining the final shape of the CSAM law.

Two features of the campaign's content distinguish it from routine institutional communication. Per Mekić, the campaign, which apparently racked up millions of views on X, insinuated that opponents of the proposed legislation did not want to protect children — messaging he dubbed "a form of emotional blackmail". The ads also included what he suggested is a misleading claim that the majority of Europeans support the proposal, based on a survey that highlighted "only the benefits but not the drawbacks of the proposed legislation"; other surveys, by the research firms YouGov and Novus, that highlighted the drawbacks showed "virtually no support" for the plan among the European population.
