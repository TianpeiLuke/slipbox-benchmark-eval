# Stephen King: Vampire Verdict

Polygon's verdict on Stephen King, author of 'Salem's Lot (1975), is: vampire — complimentary.

'Salem's Lot, King's second published book after the breakout success of 1974's Carrie, concerns a writer who returns to his childhood hometown in rural Maine only to discover gradually but gruesomely that the entire society is in thrall to the undead. Polygon's literary point is that where the vampire stories of the Victorian era used their monstrous antagonists as allegories for the deepest fears of the fading 19th century, King's novel encapsulated post-Watergate suspicions that government corruption had infected all levels of the community. The grounds for the verdict are King himself: he famously dwells in the least accessible reaches of rural New England, maintains a schedule, lifestyle, and ability to defy death that would make Rasputin blush — he survived a direct hit from a car, just for starters — and has been frozen in an ageless state throughout his career that could fairly be described as "non-specifically ancient." The piece argues he is not only a vampire but not even trying very hard to hide it, while conceding that given King's recent high-profile stances against corporate power and in favor of trans rights, he makes a strong case for vampire kind — hence the complimentary verdict.
