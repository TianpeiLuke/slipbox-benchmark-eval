# The AI Legislation Gap Behind Biden's Executive Order

Biden's AI executive order can only be a stopgap, because there is no legislative remedy to potential AI risks and abuses outside of those that can be applied to tech companies in general — and many have argued over the years that those general remedies are also inadequate. The order therefore props up the "voluntary" practices many companies are already choosing to implement, and the limits on what a president can do with a wave of their hand mean it amounts to a lot of sharing results, developing best practices and providing clear guidance.

The grounds for that reading are the state of federal action and the state of prospective law. Federal action on social media and de facto monopolies like Amazon and Google has been sporadic, though a hawkish new FTC may change that trend. Meanwhile, a comprehensive law defining and limiting the use of AI seems as far off now as it was years ago: the industry and technology have evolved so quickly that any rule would likely be outdated by the time it was passed, and it is not even really clear what ought to be legislatively limited as opposed to being left to state law or expert agencies.

The warrant joining grounds to claim is that executive authority cannot substitute for the institutional machinery a durable regime would need. Perhaps the wisest approach is to set up a new federal agency dedicated to regulating AI and technology, but this cannot be accomplished by fiat. In the meantime, the EO at least instructs several AI-focused groups, like one in the Department of Health and Human Services dedicated to handling and assessing reports of AI-related harms in healthcare.
