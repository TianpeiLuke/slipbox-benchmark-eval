# Permian-Triassic Boundary Warming and Oxygen Collapse

At the Permian-Triassic boundary, CO2 levels spiked, driving massive warming that dried out the interior of Pangea, killed the moisture-dependent forests and thereby cut organic-matter burial, so that atmospheric oxygen dropped precipitously to concentrations as low as 15% — and the combination of greenhouse warming and low oxygen produced widespread hypoxia, a state in which organisms simply cannot take in enough oxygen to support metabolism.

Plate tectonics had by then brought all the continents together into a single giant continent, Pangea, straddled across the equator, and it was already difficult for maritime moisture to penetrate deep into the centre of the continent; rapid greenhouse warming made it even hotter and drier, according both to climate model simulations of the end of the Permian and analyses of the fossil river deposits from Pangean floodplains. The sudden drying would have led to the massive die-off of the tenuous forests that had arisen over the course of the Paleozoic, which meant less burial of organic matter on land, assisted perhaps by decreased carbon export to the deep oceans due to a collapsing marine food web. The drop in oxygen was a further contributor to the mass die-off. It also reshuffled which animals could thrive: the proto-mammals that had come to dominance during the Permian — the synapsids and therapsids — had thrived off high oxygen levels, and as oxygen concentrations dropped they were now poorly suited to their environment, while plummeting oxygen levels helped dinosaurs become the dominant group on Earth.
