# The Permian-Triassic Extinction: Scale of Losses

The deadliest extinction event took place at the end of the Permian period, roughly 250 million years ago: an estimated 90% of all Permian species disappeared from the face of the planet, earning the Permian-Triassic (P-T) extinction its nickname, the Great Dying.

Marine organisms were hit especially hard, with 96% of species perishing. Gone were the trilobites familiar to amateur fossil collectors everywhere — primitive arthropods that were the distant ancestors of the modern horseshoe crab — whose nearly 300-million-year moment ended despite their having survived the earlier Ordovician extinction event. Not only were the vast majority of marine invertebrates gone, but so were the earliest fish species; on land, more than two thirds of amphibian and reptile species and nearly one third of insect species were wiped out. Another iconic casualty was Meganeuropsis, a giant dragonfly with a nearly three-foot (0.9 meter) wingspan often included in artist depictions of the Carboniferous period. By comparison, the most well-known extinction event, which ended the reign of the dinosaurs, occurred roughly 66 million years ago.
