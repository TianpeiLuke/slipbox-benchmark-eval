# How Android's OEM licensing — Play bundling plus Anti-Fragmentation Agreements — constrains rival app stores

At the start of Epic Games' antitrust trial against Google on November 6, 2023, Epic's case against Google's Android app store rested on the terms Google imposes on handset makers rather than on any ban on sideloading, which Android does allow. Two contract layers do the work. First, Google bundles the Play Store with other Google apps, and Android OEMs (original equipment manufacturers) have to agree to that bundling in order to use Android. Second, the "Anti-Fragmentation Agreements" (AFAs) Google signs with those OEMs prevent them from modifying Android to allow for frictionless downloading of apps outside of Google Play. Together, Epic argued, these agreements make it hard for alternative app stores to succeed and reach consumers, and 90% of apps are still downloaded through Google Play. Epic's illustration: a deal it had made with the OEM OnePlus to put Epic games on OnePlus devices through the Epic Games app had to be cancelled, because OnePlus was concerned the arrangement would have it bypassing the Google Play Store.
